# Contemporary Floating Staircase Engineering + Slatted Balustrade

The visible timber conceals a sophisticated engineered structure. Understanding how these staircases actually work is essential to specifying, installing and maintaining them properly.

## What Makes a Staircase "Float"

Floating staircases have:

- **Open risers** — no vertical board between treads.
- **No traditional string visible** — the structural spine is hidden.
- **Shadow gaps beneath every tread** — no visible support.
- **Solid timber treads** projecting from the wall or a central spine.
- **Hidden structural fixings** — no visible brackets, screws or plates.

The result is a sculptural architectural feature where the treads appear to hover in space.

## Primary Structural Support (Hidden)

The staircase's real load path is invisible. Common concealed structures:

- **Concealed steel spine beam** — a single steel spine runs beneath the tread centres; treads bolt to steel plates welded to the spine.
- **Steel box-section frame** — a hollow steel frame concealed within the tread depth.
- **Steel cantilever brackets fixed into a reinforced wall** — treads cantilever from the wall side; requires a structurally-reinforced masonry or steel-frame wall.
- **Fabricated steel staircase frame** — the entire staircase skeleton is fabricated in steel and clad in timber.

These components carry **almost all** of the structural load.

## Timber Treads Fixed to the Steel

Treads bolt to the concealed steel structure using:

- Steel plates welded to the spine or wall brackets.
- Hidden bolts through the tread underside.
- Threaded rods.
- Epoxy-fixed anchors.

Fixings are recessed into the tread from below and plugged so the top surface reads clean.

## The Outer Timber Rail

Along the open (balustrade) side, a concealed timber or steel rail runs beneath or alongside the treads. This rail:

- Aligns the treads.
- Provides fixing points for the balustrade battens (or glass panel base shoes).
- Helps prevent lateral movement.
- Connects all treads into one rigid assembly.

## Vertical Slatted Balustrade — What It Does and What It Doesn't Do

The most common contemporary balustrade for floating staircases is a full-height vertical timber slatted screen.

### What the Slats Do

- **Balustrade** — fall protection at the open side.
- **Safety barrier** — regulatory role, resists horizontal loads from a person leaning against the handrail.
- **Architectural feature** — the visual signature of the staircase.
- **Privacy screen** — filters view between staircase and adjacent living area.
- **Visual divider** — separates the stair from the room without closing it off.

The slatted balustrade is engineered as a system. Once every batten is installed — into the outer support rail at the bottom, into the handrail at the top, sometimes into hidden steel channels with concealed dowels or threaded fixings — the complete assembly behaves as a single rigid framework.

### Structural Contribution

Although individual battens are relatively slender, the continuous system:

- Reduces handrail flex.
- Resists sideways movement.
- Distributes loads applied to the balustrade.
- Improves overall rigidity.
- Stabilises the outer edge of the staircase assembly.

### CRITICAL — What the Slats Do NOT Do

**The battens are NOT designed to carry the vertical weight of people using the stairs.** That load is transferred through the concealed steel frame or wall fixings. The slats' role is to support the balustrade system and resist HORIZONTAL forces (someone leaning against the handrail), not the vertical live load of foot traffic.

Confusing these two roles is a common design misunderstanding. Nex should be careful to explain that removing or changing the slatted screen changes the balustrade + horizontal stiffness but does not compromise the primary vertical load path (which is the concealed steel structure).

## Frameless Glass Balustrade (Alternative or Twin-Balustrade)

Some designs use frameless glass instead of (or in addition to) the slatted screen. Toughened laminated safety glass fixed into concealed channels or recessed base shoes. Same load-role as the slats — resists horizontal loading, does not carry vertical live load.

**Twin-balustrade designs** combine both: slatted screen on the outer side (architectural feature + privacy), frameless glass on the wall side or landing (maintains open views + maximises light).

## Integrated LED Lighting — Three Common Systems

Contemporary floating staircases typically use three integrated lighting systems working together:

- **Wall-mounted LED tread lights** — recessed into the plaster wall, one light positioned beside selected treads. Warm-white, low glare, night-time navigation.
- **Concealed handrail lighting** — continuous LED strip beneath or behind a wall-mounted timber handrail. Illuminates the stair flight, creates a floating effect, highlights the wall surface, improves safety.
- **Ceiling cove lighting** — hidden LED lighting around the ceiling perimeter creates indirect ambient illumination, enhancing double-height spaces.

Cable routes, driver locations and switching should be planned during the building's structural phase, long before finishes are installed.

## Trade Coordination — Non-Negotiable

This type of staircase requires close collaboration between:

- **Architect** — overall building design + spatial planning.
- **Structural engineer** — sizing the concealed steel + wall reinforcement + tread cantilever loads.
- **Staircase manufacturer** — timber treads + slatted balustrade fabrication.
- **Steel fabricator** — the concealed spine or brackets or frame.
- **Electrician** — LED wiring, drivers, conduit routes.
- **Joiner** — on-site trimming, handrail fitting, alignment.
- **Interior designer** — coordinating finishes, wall treatments, lighting design.

If any of these are missing or brought in late, the staircase will either compromise structurally (undersized steel) or aesthetically (visible fixings, exposed cables, misaligned battens).

## Enhancement Opportunities

- **RGBW LED tread lighting** for scene-based colour control.
- **Motion-activated stair lighting** with soft ramp-up.
- **Hidden storage** integrated into a low bench at the stair base.
- **Twin-balustrade upgrade** — add frameless glass on the open side of an existing slatted design (or vice versa).
- **Acoustic slat panels** — swap plain timber slats for acoustic-backed slats (rockwool or foam behind) if the stair transfers too much sound between floors.
- **Handrail-integrated LED** if not already fitted.

## Construction Knowledge for Nex

Nex should understand that:

1. **In contemporary floating staircases, the visible timber conceals a sophisticated engineered structure.**
2. The apparent simplicity of floating treads and vertical slatted balustrades is achieved through **concealed steelwork and precision joinery**.
3. **Primary vertical load** goes through the concealed steel frame or wall fixings.
4. **The vertical slatted balustrade** provides fall protection + horizontal-load resistance + architectural feature — it does NOT carry vertical foot-traffic load.
5. **Cable routes and driver locations** must be planned during the building's structural phase, not retrofitted later.
6. **Trade coordination** between architect, structural engineer, staircase manufacturer, steel fabricator, electrician and joiner is non-negotiable — this is a multi-discipline installation, not a single-trade job.

## Enhancement Opportunity

Nex should treat images of floating slatted-balustrade staircases as reference for **integrated building features rather than isolated joinery items**. The staircase, balustrades, lighting, glazing, flooring and surrounding walls are conceived as one coordinated system. Proper planning during the architectural design stage is essential to accommodate concealed steelwork, electrical wiring for LED lighting and precision fixing details that allow the finished staircase to appear clean, seamless and structurally effortless.
