# Staircase Panelling Construction Knowledge

## Purpose of Panelling

Decorative staircase panelling is a non-structural finish installed over or alongside an existing wall, staircase string, cupboard or timber framing.

It serves several purposes:

- Creates a luxury architectural appearance.
- Hides uneven plasterwork.
- Conceals services and pipework.
- Creates hidden storage.
- Protects walls from scuffs.
- Visually links the hallway, staircase and landing.
- Adds value to the property.

Panelling should **never** be assumed to provide structural support unless specifically designed by an engineer.

## Existing Wall Types

Before specifying panelling, identify the wall construction. Possible wall types:

- Plastered block wall
- Brick wall
- Timber stud wall
- Plasterboard on timber studs
- Dot-and-dab plasterboard
- Existing MDF lining
- Plywood lining
- Under-stair timber framing

Different wall types require different fixing methods.

## Wall Flatness

Professional installers first check wall flatness. Typical process:

- 2 m straight edge
- Spirit level
- Laser level

If the wall varies significantly, before decorative mouldings are fitted the installer will: **pack battens · skim plaster · install new MDF lining**. Luxury panelling should always be installed onto a flat surface.

## Batten Systems

Sometimes decorative panels are not fixed directly to masonry. Instead installers create a timber batten framework.

**Typical timber:** 25 × 38 mm or 25 × 50 mm softwood.

Benefits: perfectly straight wall · easier fixing · space for cables · insulation · LED wiring · hidden pipework.

## Service Void

High-end installations often create a small cavity behind the panel system.

**Typical depth: 20–50 mm.**

This service void allows: electrical cables · Ethernet · alarm wiring · LED transformers · speaker cable · pipework · heating controls — without cutting into masonry.

## Timber Movement

Solid timber expands and contracts. Therefore large hardwood panels require expansion gaps. MDF remains far more stable. For painted panelling **MR-MDF is normally preferred**.

## Priming

All MDF should be sealed before painting — especially: cut edges · machined edges · routered profiles.

**Failure to seal MDF edges causes:** swelling · poor paint finish · visible joints.

## Panel Spacing

Luxury installations use consistent spacing. Common reveal: **50 mm · 60 mm · 75 mm**. The reveal should remain consistent throughout the hallway, stairs, landing and upper hallway.

## Golden Rule of Panel Design

**Never simply divide the wall equally.** Instead: find the visual centre → work outward → adjust panel sizes slightly until they appear balanced.

Professional designers prioritise appearance over mathematical equality.

## Visual Alignment

Panel layouts should align with architectural features where possible: newel posts · doors · windows · radiators · arches · landings · ceiling beams. Creates a cohesive design.

## Triangular Walls

The triangular wall beneath a staircase presents a design challenge. Professional solutions:

- Following the stair pitch with angled panels.
- Using a horizontal dado rail with rectangular lower panels.
- Installing tongue-and-groove boarding.
- Using vertical slatted panels.
- Creating a concealed cupboard with matching panels.

The chosen method depends on the style of the property.

## Curved Staircase Panelling

Curved staircases require specialised techniques:

- Flexible MDF
- Kerf-cut MDF
- Steam-bent timber
- Segmented mouldings
- Hand-shaped hardwood mouldings

Curved panelling requires precise templating and is significantly more labour-intensive than straight staircases.

## Template Making

Complex areas are rarely measured directly. Professionals use templates made from: hardboard · cardboard · thin MDF · door-skin plywood. Templates are checked before cutting expensive materials.

## Mitre Joints

Decorative mouldings are joined using mitres. Internal corners **45°** · external corners **45°**. Uneven walls often require custom angles rather than perfect 45° cuts. Professional installers frequently adjust mitres on site for a seamless appearance.

## Coping

Instead of mitring inside corners, some joiners cope mouldings.

**Benefits:** tighter joints · less affected by timber movement · easier to maintain over time. Common in traditional installations.

## Caulking

After installation, decorators use flexible decorators' caulk to fill: small gaps · joint lines · moulding edges · wall imperfections.

Caulk should **not** be used to hide poor carpentry. Large gaps indicate inaccurate installation.

## Painting Process

Typical sequence: fill nail holes → sand smooth → prime repairs → apply undercoat → sand lightly → first top coat → sand lightly → final top coat.

Premium finishes often involve spray painting for a flawless appearance.

## Access Panels

Hidden doors should be virtually invisible. Methods: magnetic catches · push-to-open latches · concealed hinges · flush finger pulls · continuous panel lines. Panel mouldings continue across the door to disguise the opening.

## Under-Stair Storage Integration

Panelling can conceal: cupboards · shoe storage · coat storage · vacuum storage · wine storage · pull-out drawers · pet areas · electrical cupboards.

**The decorative layout should remain uninterrupted, even where doors are incorporated.**

## Material Estimation

A professional quotation typically calculates:

1. Total wall area (m²)
2. MDF sheet quantity
3. Moulding length (linear metres)
4. Skirting length
5. Dado rail length
6. Cornice length (if required)
7. Adhesive quantity
8. Pins or nails
9. Caulk
10. Primer
11. Paint
12. Labour hours
13. Waste allowance (typically 10–15%)

## Quality Standards

A high-quality installation should have:

- Straight, level mouldings.
- Consistent panel spacing.
- Clean mitre joints.
- No visible nail heads after painting.
- Smooth caulked joints.
- Uniform paint finish.
- Panels aligned with architectural features.
- Flush access doors where fitted.
- Even shadow lines and reveals.
- Crisp transitions between skirting, panelling and staircase components.

## Nex Design Decision Rules

When advising users, Nex should apply these principles:

1. Use **Shaker** or **flush panels** for modern and contemporary homes.
2. Use **raised-and-fielded** or **bolection** mouldings for Georgian, Victorian or Edwardian properties.
3. Recommend **tongue-and-groove boarding** for cottages, farmhouse, Scandinavian or coastal interiors.
4. Suggest **vertical timber slats** for ultra-modern or minimalist staircases with integrated LED lighting.
5. Where the staircase has a **sheeted string or enclosed side**, treat it as an ideal substrate for applied mouldings or decorative panelling.
6. **Continue panelling** from the hallway, up the staircase and onto the landing wherever practical to create a unified architectural feature.
7. **Avoid oversized or undersized panels** — maintain balanced proportions that suit the wall height and staircase pitch.
8. Recommend **concealed storage doors only where they can blend into the panel layout** without disrupting symmetry.
