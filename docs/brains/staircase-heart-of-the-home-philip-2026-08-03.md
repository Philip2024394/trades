# The Staircase as the Heart of the Home

## Why Every New Home Should Be Designed Around the Staircase

Many homeowners spend months choosing kitchens, bathrooms, flooring and paint colours, yet give very little thought to the staircase until construction has already begun. In reality, the staircase is one of the most permanent and influential features in any property.

Unlike furniture, a staircase cannot simply be moved or replaced without major structural work. Its location, size and design influence almost every room around it.

A staircase should never be seen as "just a way upstairs." It is an investment in the home's architecture, functionality and long-term value.

## Design the Home Around the Staircase

Professional architects often begin by establishing how people will move through the building. The staircase plays a major role in that journey.

Questions include:

- Where do people enter the home?
- Which room do they see first?
- How do they move between spaces?
- How does natural light travel through the property?
- Where will family members gather?
- Which views should be highlighted?
- How can circulation feel effortless?

The staircase often provides the answers.

## Every Staircase Creates Dead Space

Every staircase creates areas beneath, beside and around it. These spaces can become valuable assets if planned correctly.

Examples: under-stair storage cupboards · utility cupboards · wine storage · home office areas · reading nooks · pet spaces · boot rooms · coat storage · pantry extensions · hidden plant rooms.

Planning these spaces during the design stage maximises the home's usable area.

## Think About the Future

Many homeowners only consider today's requirements. Professional planning considers the next 20–30 years.

Future considerations: growing families · elderly relatives · reduced mobility · home working · smart home technology · electric vehicle charging · solar power integration · future redecorating · staircase lighting upgrades · decorative wall panelling.

A staircase should be designed to adapt with the home.

## Coordinate With Window Design

Windows and staircases work together. A carefully positioned staircase can capture morning sunlight · create dramatic double-height spaces · frame garden views · reduce reliance on artificial lighting · improve ventilation. Large feature windows often become far more effective when designed around the staircase.

## Avoid Wasted Hallways

Poor staircase planning often creates long corridors · awkward corners · unusable spaces · narrow hallways · blocked sight lines. Good planning allows the staircase to improve circulation rather than restrict it.

## Ceiling Design

The staircase also affects ceiling design. Consider vaulted ceilings · feature pendants · skylights · roof lanterns · exposed beams · bulkheads · plaster details. Lighting and ceiling design should complement the staircase.

## Flooring Transitions

The staircase connects different floor finishes. Planning should include timber flooring · carpet · tiles · luxury vinyl · engineered wood · stone. Transitions should feel natural and consistent throughout the home.

## Interior Style

The staircase should reinforce the home's design language.

- **Modern homes** — clean lines · glass · oak · concealed lighting.
- **Traditional homes** — painted timber · turned spindles · decorative newels · classic mouldings.
- **Industrial homes** — steel · timber · exposed fixings.
- **Scandinavian homes** — pale timber · minimalist detailing · open spaces.

The staircase should belong to the house, not look like an afterthought.

## Budget Planning

Planning the staircase early also helps manage costs. Early budgeting allows homeowners to decide whether to include hardwood treads · glass balustrades · decorative panelling · integrated lighting · bespoke handrails · feature newel posts · under-stair storage · premium finishes. Making these decisions before construction begins reduces the likelihood of expensive variations later.

## A Collaborative Process

The best staircase projects are created through collaboration.

- The **homeowner** brings: lifestyle needs · design preferences · future plans.
- The **architect** contributes: spatial planning · overall building layout · planning compliance.
- The **structural engineer** ensures: safe structural support · floor openings · load paths.
- The **staircase specialist** contributes: staircase geometry · manufacturing expertise · practical installation knowledge · material selection · detailing · long-term performance.

Together they create a staircase that is attractive, practical and buildable.

## Why Early Decisions Save Money

Changing a staircase after construction has started may involve redesigning structural openings · altering floor joists · moving walls · changing electrical routes · relocating plumbing · replastering · replacing flooring · delaying other trades. These changes can significantly increase both time and cost. Making informed decisions during the drawing stage is usually far more economical.

## The Homeowner's Checklist

1. Is the staircase in the best possible location?
2. Does it create a welcoming entrance?
3. Is there enough natural light?
4. Will it suit our lifestyle in ten years' time?
5. Have we planned for lighting and electrical services?
6. Can under-stair space be used effectively?
7. Will furniture move easily between floors?
8. Does the staircase complement the kitchen and living spaces?
9. Have we consulted a staircase specialist before finalising the plans?

## Nex Professional Principle

Nex should treat staircase planning as one of the earliest design decisions in a project. The staircase should be considered alongside the kitchen because both are permanent, bespoke elements that shape how the home functions and feels. By encouraging early collaboration between the homeowner, architect, structural engineer and staircase specialist, Nex can help create homes with better circulation, improved natural light, more efficient use of space, easier construction and greater long-term value. The goal is not simply to fit a staircase into a house, but to make it an integral part of the home's architecture from the very first drawing.
