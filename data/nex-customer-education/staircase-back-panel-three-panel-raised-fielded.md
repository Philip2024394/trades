# Staircase Back Panel — Three-Panel Raised and Fielded Design

This image shows a three-panel raised and fielded timber panel, a classic joinery design that can be adapted beautifully as the rear panel or back panel of a staircase. Using this style creates a high-end furniture appearance and ensures the staircase looks attractive from every angle, not just the front.

## Staircase Back Panel Design

The back of a staircase is often overlooked, yet it can become an important architectural feature, particularly in open-plan homes where the rear of the staircase is visible from living spaces, hallways, or dining rooms.

Instead of leaving the structure exposed or covering it with plain plasterboard, timber back panels provide a premium, fully finished appearance.

## Three-Panel Raised and Fielded Design

This example uses a traditional three-panel configuration, consisting of:

- A solid outer frame (stiles and rails).
- Three equal-width raised and fielded panels.
- Decorative moulded panel beads surrounding each panel.
- Clean, symmetrical proportions.

The vertical panels make the staircase appear taller while maintaining a balanced and elegant appearance.

## Traditional Joinery

Raised and fielded panels have been used for centuries in fine joinery. They are commonly found on:

- Staircase back panels
- Internal doors
- Wall panelling
- Cabinetry
- Library joinery
- Traditional entrance halls

The moulded profiles create subtle shadow lines that add depth and craftsmanship to what would otherwise be a flat surface.

## Matching the Staircase

For the best results, the rear staircase panel should be manufactured from the same timber and finish as the staircase itself.

This creates continuity between:

- Newel posts
- Handrails
- Strings
- Treads
- Side panels
- Back panels
- Internal doors
- Skirting boards
- Architraves

When all these elements match, the staircase feels like an integrated piece of bespoke furniture.

## Practical Benefits

A timber back panel offers more than visual appeal:

- Conceals the structural framework of the staircase.
- Protects the underside from damage.
- Improves the finished appearance from adjoining rooms.
- Reduces visible gaps and services.
- Can improve acoustic performance by enclosing the stair structure.
- Creates opportunities for hidden access panels if required.

## Design Flexibility

Although this example shows three equal panels, the same concept can be adapted in many ways:

- Two large panels for a simpler appearance.
- Four or six narrower panels for taller staircases.
- Square panels beneath quarter landings.
- Horizontal panels for contemporary interiors.
- Shaker-style recessed panels.
- Beaded tongue-and-groove panels for cottage or farmhouse homes.
- Flush modern panels with concealed joints for minimalist interiors.

## Premium Appearance

A staircase finished on both sides demonstrates attention to detail. Whether viewed from the hallway, living room, or landing, the staircase appears complete and professionally crafted.

The raised and fielded three-panel design shown here is particularly suited to:

- Oak staircases
- Walnut staircases
- Mahogany staircases
- Maple staircases
- Painted hardwood staircases

Its timeless proportions complement both traditional and transitional interiors, making it one of the most versatile choices for the rear face of a staircase. When combined with matching doors and wall panelling, it creates a refined, bespoke interior that adds lasting value and character to the home.

## The Straight-Flight Rear Application

The three-panel raised design is often used on the full-height rear side of a straight-flight staircase. Applied at this scale, the back panel becomes one of the largest visible timber features within a hallway — an important element of the overall interior design rather than simply an enclosure. Ideal for homes where the back of the staircase is fully visible from living spaces or open-plan interiors, the design conceals the stair structure while showcasing the craftsmanship of the timber.
