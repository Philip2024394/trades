# Floating Stairs – Exploded Assembly, Load Design, and UK Building Regulations

The image shows an exploded assembly of a modern cantilever (floating) staircase. Rather than the timber tread carrying the structural load, the steel does all of the structural work and the oak is primarily a decorative and walking surface.

Here's what is happening from the wall outwards.

## 1. Steel Stringer (the backbone)

- The large triangular steel plate is the main stringer.
- It is typically fabricated from 10–15 mm structural steel plate (sometimes thicker depending on calculations).
- It is bolted or welded to the main building structure.
- Every tread transfers its load back into this steel stringer.

## 2. Steel Tread Support

Each tread has a welded steel support that projects from the stringer.

Think of it like a heavy-duty steel cantilever beam.

```
Wall
│
│  Steel stringer
│█████████████
│
├──────── Steel support
│
│
```

These supports are normally made from:

- Rectangular hollow section (RHS).
- Box section.
- Laser-cut folded steel.
- Fully welded fabrication.

The support is designed to resist:

- Vertical bending.
- Twisting.
- Deflection.
- Vibration.

## 3. Oak Tread

The oak tread is actually a box.

Inside it is a hollow cavity.

```
Oak top
──────────────

      Hollow

──────────────
Oak bottom
```

That cavity slides over the steel support.

Once fully pushed on, it is locked into position by:

- Screws.
- Hidden bolts.
- Threaded inserts.
- Epoxy.

Nothing is visible from outside.

## 4. Spacer Bolts

The two bolts visible at the front aren't supporting the tread vertically.

They normally:

- Locate the tread.
- Prevent movement.
- Allow adjustment.
- Clamp the oak to the steel.

Most of the weight is carried underneath by the steel support.

## Why Is It So Strong?

People often assume the oak is carrying the load.

It isn't.

The structural path is:

```
Person
   ↓
Oak tread
   ↓
Steel support
   ↓
Steel stringer
   ↓
Wall
   ↓
Building structure
```

The oak mainly spreads the pressure over the steel.

## Typical Design Loads

A properly engineered floating staircase in the UK is normally designed around:

**Domestic loading:**

- 2.0 kN/m² floor loading.
- Concentrated tread load approximately 1.5–2.0 kN.

Many engineers design the steel with additional safety factors.

A single tread can often comfortably support:

- 250–400 kg static load.

…before the steel begins approaching its design limits.

Actual capacity depends on:

- Projection length.
- Steel size.
- Weld quality.
- Fixing into wall.
- Engineer's calculations.

Commercial stairs are often considerably stronger.

## Does It Bounce?

A well-designed staircase should feel almost solid.

Poor designs suffer from:

- Slight vibration.
- Springiness.
- Squeaking.
- Movement.

The stiffness comes almost entirely from the steel fabrication.

## Does Timber Shrink?

Yes.

The oak moves seasonally.

That is why:

- Slotted holes.
- Isolation tape.
- Neoprene.
- Concealed fixings.

…are often used so the timber can expand without splitting.

## Does It Squeak?

If manufactured correctly:

**Very little.**

Squeaks usually come from:

- Timber rubbing on steel.
- Loose bolts.
- Timber shrinkage.
- Poor fitting.

Premium manufacturers isolate timber from steel using rubber or specialist tapes.

## Building Regulations (UK)

The staircase still has to comply with UK Building Regulations. The fact that it is steel instead of timber does not exempt it from the same functional requirements.

It must comply with the relevant parts of Approved Document K (Protection from falling, collision and impact) and the structural requirements of Approved Document A. Structural steelwork should also be designed to the relevant Eurocodes or equivalent structural standards.

The following requirements still apply:

### Going and rise

Typical domestic limits include:

- Maximum rise: 220 mm.
- Minimum going: 220 mm.
- Pitch generally not exceeding 42°.

These are the same regardless of whether the stair is timber or steel.

### Open risers

Yes, floating staircases can have open risers.

However, they must satisfy the same rule as any other domestic staircase:

**A 100 mm sphere must not be able to pass through the opening between treads.**

So even though the stair appears to float, the gap between treads is still regulated.

### Width

Normal domestic widths still apply.

Typically:

- 800–1000 mm is common.
- Luxury homes often use 1000–1200 mm.

### Handrails

A floating staircase still requires:

- Handrails where required by the regulations.
- Guarding where there is a risk of falling.
- Balustrades that prevent a 100 mm sphere passing through.

The "floating" appearance does not remove these safety requirements.

## Closed String vs Open String

The closed-string / open-string terminology mainly applies to traditional timber staircases, where the sides of the treads are housed within or exposed alongside timber strings.

A floating steel staircase is generally treated differently in terms of construction:

- The steel stringer is the structural string.
- The oak treads are fixed to cantilevered steel supports rather than housed in timber strings.
- Building Control is concerned with the finished stair's safety, strength, geometry, guarding, and compliance, not whether it resembles a traditional closed-string or open-string timber stair.

## Why Are These Stairs Expensive?

A high-end floating staircase is expensive because much of the work is hidden:

- CNC laser-cut steel components.
- Precision welding.
- Structural engineering calculations.
- Deflection control.
- Hidden fixings.
- CNC-machined oak treads.
- Factory trial assembly.
- Powder coating.
- Careful installation and adjustment on site.

The result is a staircase where the steel structure does the engineering, while the oak provides the finished appearance. When designed and installed correctly, these staircases are extremely strong, durable, and can last for decades with minimal maintenance.
