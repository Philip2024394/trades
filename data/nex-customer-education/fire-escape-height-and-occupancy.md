# Fire Escape Staircases – How Height and Occupancy Drive the Design

Yes, in many cases fire escape design is judged by the number of people using the building, but that's only one factor. There is no single maximum height at which a fire escape staircase is allowed.

The design depends on several factors:

- Building height.
- Number of occupants.
- Building use (house, flats, office, factory, hotel, etc.).
- Travel distance to an exit.
- Number of escape routes.
- Fire resistance of the building.

## Occupancy Is Very Important

For commercial and public buildings, the more people expected to use the escape stair, the wider it generally needs to be.

For example:

| Occupants | Typical Stair Width |
|---|---|
| Up to 60 people | Around 1000 mm |
| 60–110 people | Around 1100 mm |
| More than 110 people | Width increases according to the required evacuation capacity |

These figures are illustrative; the exact required width depends on the applicable building regulations and fire strategy.

## Is There a Maximum Height?

No. Fire escape stairs can serve buildings many storeys high.

Examples:

- 2-storey office.
- 5-storey apartment block.
- 10-storey hotel.
- 20-storey commercial building.

As buildings get taller, additional fire safety measures are usually required, such as:

- Multiple protected escape stairs.
- Fire-resistant construction.
- Smoke ventilation or pressurisation systems.
- Refuge areas.
- Firefighting shafts and lifts in some larger buildings.

## For NEX Staircase Intelligence

Rather than asking *"What height is your staircase?"*, NEX design intelligence could ask a smarter sequence of questions:

- What type of building is it? Residential, commercial, industrial, or public?
- How many storeys?
- What is the floor-to-floor height?
- Approximately how many occupants will use the staircase?
- Is it the primary means of escape or a secondary escape stair?
- Is the staircase internal or external?
- Which country's building regulations apply?

From those answers, NEX design intelligence could determine the likely requirements for:

- Stair width.
- Number of escape stairs.
- Landing sizes.
- Handrails.
- Balustrade height.
- Structural loading.
- Fire protection.

That kind of decision tree is much more useful than simply asking for dimensions because it guides the user toward a compliant design before any drawings or quotations are produced.
