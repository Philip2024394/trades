# Understanding Woodworm – What It Is and What You Can Do

Woodworm is one of the most misunderstood problems in timber. Many people believe that a "woodworm" is a worm living inside the wood. In fact, it is the larval stage of several species of wood-boring beetles. The damage is caused by the larvae, while the adult beetle is responsible for laying the eggs.

## Where Does Woodworm Come From?

Woodworm starts with a flying beetle.

The life cycle is:

- An adult wood-boring beetle flies to suitable timber.
- The female lays tiny eggs in cracks, rough grain, or existing holes.
- The eggs hatch into larvae (woodworm).
- The larvae tunnel through the wood while feeding.
- After several years, the adult beetle emerges by chewing a small round exit hole.
- The beetle flies away to mate and begin the cycle again.

The small holes you see are usually exit holes, not entrance holes.

## Do Woodworms Eat Wood?

Yes — but not in the same way humans eat food.

The larvae feed on the cellulose within the timber as they tunnel through it. Their digestive system allows them to break down parts of the wood to obtain nutrients.

Some varieties of beetle prefer softwood, while others can attack certain hardwoods. Different beetle types have different preferences.

## How Deep Can They Live?

Surprisingly deep.

Depending on the beetle variety and the size of the timber, larvae may tunnel:

- Just beneath the surface.
- Several inches (centimetres) into the wood.
- Deep inside large beams or thick hardwood boards.

Some tunnels can run in many directions, making the damage impossible to see from the outside.

## How Long Can They Live Inside?

Woodworm larvae grow slowly.

Many common varieties remain inside timber for 2 to 5 years, while some can remain inside the wood for much longer before emerging as adult beetles.

During this time, they continue feeding and tunnelling.

## Why Do They Like Wood?

Wood provides:

- Food.
- Shelter.
- Protection from predators.
- A stable environment for development.

However, not all timber is equally attractive.

Wood-boring beetles generally prefer timber that is:

- Damp.
- Untreated.
- Poorly ventilated.
- Beginning to decay.

Dry, well-maintained timber is much less attractive to many varieties.

## Can They Attack New Staircases?

It is uncommon for a newly manufactured hardwood staircase made from properly kiln-dried timber to contain active woodworm.

During commercial kiln drying:

- Moisture is removed.
- Temperatures are high enough to kill insects, larvae, and eggs when carried out correctly.

This is one reason kiln drying is an important stage in timber production.

## How Do I Know If Woodworm Is Active?

Look for signs such as:

- Fresh, pale wood dust (called frass) beneath the holes.
- New holes appearing over time.
- Adult beetles emerging during the warmer months.
- Weak or crumbly timber.

Old holes on their own do not necessarily indicate an active infestation.

## Can I Block the Holes?

Yes — but only after confirming there is no active infestation.

If the woodworm is no longer active, small holes can be repaired by:

- Filling them with colour-matched wood filler.
- Using specialist timber repair resin.
- Sanding the repaired area.
- Applying stain, oil, lacquer, or paint to match the surrounding timber.

Simply filling holes without confirming the infestation has ended is not recommended, as any remaining insects could still emerge.

## How Do You Get Rid of Woodworm?

Treatment depends on the severity of the infestation.

For active infestations, options may include:

- Improving ventilation.
- Reducing dampness.
- Applying appropriate woodworm treatment products according to the manufacturer's instructions.
- Replacing severely damaged timber if its structural strength has been affected.

In historic buildings or where significant structural timber is involved, a specialist timber survey may be appropriate.

## Can Woodworm Be Prevented?

You cannot guarantee that flying beetles will never enter a building, but you can greatly reduce the risk by:

- Keeping timber dry.
- Repairing leaks promptly.
- Providing good ventilation.
- Avoiding long-term damp conditions.
- Using pressure-treated timber outdoors where appropriate.
- Inspecting timber regularly.

Healthy, dry timber is much less attractive to wood-boring beetles than damp or decaying wood.

## The Good News for Staircases

If your staircase has been manufactured from quality kiln-dried timber, a few small holes are far more likely to be old, inactive marks from the tree's life before processing than evidence of living woodworm.

If you notice fresh wood dust, new holes, or signs that the damage is increasing, it's worth asking a timber specialist to inspect it. In many cases, however, a handful of old holes are simply cosmetic and can be repaired as part of the finishing process without affecting the strength or safety of the staircase.
