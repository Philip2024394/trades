# Floating Stairs – How a Real Installation Actually Works

The typical construction direction used by UK floating-stair manufacturers (wall-mounted / mono-stringer / half-landing systems) rarely matches the tidy floating-in-mid-air imagery you see in marketing photos. In a real installation the lower stringer is normally fixed hard against a flat structural wall, and installers fit the oak treads from the bottom upward while kneeling on the floor. The landing section is supported by the upper structure, not floating unsupported.

This article walks the real sequence, using realistic UK residential construction (plasterboard wall, unfinished floor, boxed oak treads, powder-coated steel) as the reference.

## What Is Happening in a Real Installation?

### Actual installation order

1. **Steel stringer fixed to wall** — chemically anchored into concrete, blockwork, or a steel frame.
2. **Landing frame installed** — tied into the floor structure above.
3. **Tread support arms checked** — a laser level confirms every support is at the correct height and pitch.
4. **Oak treads slid on from the front** — each boxed tread slides over a concealed steel arm.
5. **Hidden fixings tightened** — grub screws or threaded inserts lock the tread to the steel from underneath or the side.
6. **Final alignment** — gaps, level, and nosing lines are adjusted before glass or balustrades are fitted.

## Why the Stringer Sits Against the Wall

For a true floating stair, the wall side carries most of the moment (twisting force) generated by anyone standing on the outer end of the tread.

A real section looks like this:

```
Flat structural wall
████████████████
┃ Steel stringer
┃██████████████
┃━━ concealed arm ━━━━━
┃                Oak tread
```

The steel is usually 10–15 mm plate with welded box-section tread arms. The wall carries the load back into the building structure — the tread is only ever cantilevered a short distance.

## How the Oak Tread Actually Fits

This is the key detail most marketing photos hide.

The oak is not a solid slab bolted onto a bracket — it is a box.

```
Oak box tread
┌──────────────────┐
│                  │
│   Hollow cavity  │
│                  │
└──────────────────┘
        ▲
        │ slides over
        │
Steel arm ━━━━━━━━━
```

The oak is machined with a hollow cavity, then pushed onto the steel arm and locked with hidden fixings from underneath or the side. Nothing is visible from outside once the tread is home.

## Centre-Support (Mono-Stringer) Alternative

Not every floating staircase is wall-fixed. A **centre-support** system uses a single mono-stringer running underneath the centre line of each tread, so the tread cantilevers a short distance both left and right of the stringer rather than out from a wall.

Structural characteristics:

- Suits situations where no strong wall is available for cantilever fixing.
- Mono-stringer sits directly beneath the tread centre line.
- Each tread cantilevers left and right of the stringer rather than out from a wall.
- Often used when the staircase is more central to the room rather than hugged to a wall.

Both systems (wall-fixed cantilever and centre-support mono-stringer) can be finished with the same boxed oak treads, glass balustrades, and hidden LED lighting — the visible product looks similar, the concealed engineering is what differs.

## Realistic UK Time Frames

These are the timings NEX design intelligence sees most often from bespoke staircase manufacturers.

| Stage | Typical time |
|---|---|
| Survey + laser measure | 1–3 days |
| CAD drawings | 3–7 days |
| Structural engineer | 1–2 weeks |
| Steel fabrication | 3–5 weeks |
| Oak machining + finishing | 1–2 weeks |
| Site installation | 2–4 days |
| Glass / handrail | 1 day |

Total project: roughly 6–10 weeks from survey to completion.

For a half-landing stair specifically, three days on site is very typical:

- **Day 1:** fix steel + landing.
- **Day 2:** fit oak treads.
- **Day 3:** fit glass / handrail + final snagging.

## Does It Cost the Same as a Timber Staircase?

Usually no.

A floating staircase sits at the premium end of the domestic staircase market because of the additional structural engineering, CNC steel fabrication, precision welding, hidden fixings, and specialist installation.

Roughly relative to a standard softwood staircase (which is usually the most economical option a UK homeowner can specify):

- A **hardwood closed-string staircase** typically sits several times higher.
- An **oak feature staircase** with premium detailing sits higher again.
- A **floating steel + oak staircase** is typically several times the cost of a traditional hardwood staircase.
- A **floating staircase with frameless glass balustrades** is normally the most expensive residential category.

The final figure varies with the span, timber species, balustrade choice, finish, and complexity of the steelwork.

## Why the Marketing Photos Look So Different

Marketing photography for floating stairs is almost always shot after the installation is complete, the site is cleaned, walls are painted, floors are finished, and the balustrades are polished. The messy reality of chemical anchors in blockwork, laser levels leaning against unfinished floors, and installers kneeling to slide boxed oak treads onto concealed steel arms rarely makes the brochure.

Understanding the actual sequence helps in three ways:

- It sets a realistic expectation of what install day looks like.
- It explains why the wall type matters — a plasterboard partition cannot carry a floating staircase alone.
- It clarifies why the timber and steel must be designed together — the boxed oak tread is manufactured to fit a specific steel arm, and mixing suppliers rarely works.
