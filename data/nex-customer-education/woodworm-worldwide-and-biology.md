# Woodworm Around the World – Where It's Found and How It Survives

Woodworm exists in many countries around the world because the insects that cause "woodworm" are actually wood-boring beetle larvae. They do not come from one single beetle — different beetles attack wood in different climates.

## Countries Where Woodworm Is Commonly Found

### Europe

- 🇬🇧 United Kingdom (very common in old houses, roof timbers, floors, staircases)
- 🇮🇪 Ireland
- 🇫🇷 France
- 🇩🇪 Germany
- 🇳🇱 Netherlands
- 🇧🇪 Belgium
- 🇪🇸 Spain
- 🇮🇹 Italy
- 🇵🇹 Portugal
- Scandinavian countries (especially in older wooden buildings)

### North America

- 🇺🇸 United States
- 🇨🇦 Canada

Common in older homes, barns, wooden furniture, and historic buildings.

### Asia

- 🇯🇵 Japan
- 🇨🇳 China
- 🇮🇩 Indonesia
- 🇹🇭 Thailand
- 🇲🇾 Malaysia
- 🇵🇭 Philippines
- 🇮🇳 India

Warm, humid countries often have many wood-boring insects, although the varieties can be different from European woodworm.

### Australia & New Zealand

- 🇦🇺 Australia
- 🇳🇿 New Zealand

Especially where timber buildings and imported wood products are present.

### Africa & South America

Many countries have wood-boring beetles, particularly in tropical forests and areas using untreated timber.

## Can Woodworm Survive?

Yes — they can survive for years inside wood.

The life cycle is usually:

- Adult beetle lays eggs in cracks, old holes, or rough timber.
- Eggs hatch into tiny larvae (the "woodworm").
- Larvae tunnel inside the wood and eat it.
- They can remain hidden for 2–5 years, sometimes longer depending on beetle type and conditions.
- They become adult beetles, drill out, and leave small holes.

The visible holes are usually the exit holes from adult beetles, not where they entered.

## How Deep Can Woodworm Live Inside Wood?

It depends on the beetle type and the timber:

- Small furniture beetle larvae: often a few millimetres to several centimetres deep.
- Larger wood-boring beetles: can tunnel deep into beams, posts, and thick timber.
- In large structural wood, larvae can survive near the centre, protected from surface treatments.

A surface spray may not kill larvae already deep inside.

## Why Do They Like Wood?

They are attracted because wood provides:

- ✅ Food — larvae digest wood fibres (mainly cellulose).
- ✅ Protection — tunnels keep them hidden from predators.
- ✅ Stable temperature — inside timber is safer than outside.
- ✅ Moisture — many varieties prefer slightly damp timber.

They usually prefer:

- Old timber.
- Damp wood.
- Softwood such as pine.
- Untreated timber.

Some varieties also attack hardwoods like:

- Oak
- Walnut
- Mahogany
- Teak

## Do They Actually Eat the Wood?

Yes. The larvae eat the wood, not usually the adult beetles.

The larvae slowly digest the timber and create:

- Tunnels.
- Weakened areas.
- Powdery dust called frass.

A staircase with woodworm may look perfect on the outside but have hidden internal damage.

## Where Do They Come From?

They do not appear from inside the wood by magic.

Usually:

- Adult beetles fly into buildings through windows, doors, roof gaps, or vents.
- They lay eggs on timber.
- The larvae hatch and start eating.

They can also arrive through:

- Second-hand furniture.
- Imported timber.
- Old reclaimed wood.
- Pallets.
- Untreated construction wood.

Yes — many wood-boring beetles can fly, although the adult beetles are often small and rarely noticed.

## Can You Stop Them in a Staircase?

### If Holes Are Active

Look for:

- Fresh light-coloured powder beneath holes.
- New holes appearing.
- Soft or crumbling timber.

Treatment options:

- Insecticide woodworm treatment that penetrates timber.
- Replacing badly damaged sections.
- Controlled heat treatment (professional method).

### If Holes Are Old

Many old holes are historical and no longer active. A carpenter can check by:

- Looking for fresh frass.
- Checking timber strength.
- Monitoring holes over time.

For valuable timbers like teak, walnut, mahogany, or oak staircases, it is important not to just fill holes and paint over them before confirming whether the insects are still alive.
