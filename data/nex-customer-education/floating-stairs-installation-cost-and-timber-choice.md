# Floating Stairs – Installation Sequence, Cost, and Choosing Your Timber

The image shows a typical installation sequence for a modern floating staircase with a half landing. Although it's a rendered illustration rather than a real installation, it represents the process quite well.

## Stage 1 – Installing the Structural Steel

The black steel stringers are installed first because they form the structural skeleton of the staircase.

The installation team will:

- Position the lower stringer.
- Bolt or chemically anchor it into the concrete floor or structural steel.
- Fix the upper section to the floor structure.
- Use laser levels to ensure the staircase is perfectly level and aligned.

This is the most important part of the installation. If the steel is even a few millimetres out of position, the oak treads may not fit correctly.

## Stage 2 – Installing the Landing

The crew then installs the half landing.

The landing becomes a structural platform that:

- Supports both flights.
- Keeps the staircase rigid.
- Transfers loads into the building.

The landing is often one of the heaviest components and may require two or more installers to position safely.

## Stage 3 – Fitting the Oak Treads

Once the steel framework has been checked:

- Each oak tread is slid onto its concealed steel support.
- Hidden bolts or threaded inserts secure the tread.
- The fit is checked for level and spacing.

No visible screws should be seen on a premium floating staircase.

## Stage 4 – Final Adjustments

The installers then:

- Adjust tread spacing.
- Tighten all fixings.
- Check the pitch.
- Clean the oak.
- Prepare for the balustrade installation.

Only after everything is perfectly aligned are the glass panels or handrails fitted.

## Typical Installation Time

For a standard domestic floating staircase:

| Stage | Typical Time |
|---|---|
| Site survey | 1–2 hours |
| Manufacturing | 6–10 weeks |
| Steel installation | 1 day |
| Landing installation | Same day or next day |
| Oak tread installation | 1 day |
| Glass balustrades | 1 day |
| Final finishing | Half day |

Most installations take 2–4 days on site, assuming the building is ready and there are no delays.

## Is a Floating Staircase More Expensive Than a Timber Staircase?

In most cases, yes — usually significantly so.

A floating staircase sits at the premium end of the domestic staircase market because of the additional structural engineering, CNC steel fabrication, precision welding, hidden fixings, and specialist installation. As a rough sense of scale (not a quote):

- A **standard softwood staircase** is usually the most economical option a UK homeowner can specify.
- A **hardwood closed-string staircase** typically sits several times higher than softwood.
- An **oak feature staircase** with premium detailing sits higher again.
- A **floating steel + oak staircase** is typically several times the cost of a traditional hardwood staircase.
- A **luxury floating staircase with frameless glass balustrades** is normally the most expensive residential category.

The final figure varies with the span, timber species, balustrade choice (glass · stainless · timber), finish, complexity of the steelwork, and the number of laser-cut / welded components required.

## Why Does It Cost More?

Unlike a traditional timber staircase, a floating staircase requires:

- Structural engineering calculations.
- CNC laser-cut steel components.
- Precision welding.
- Powder-coated steel.
- Hidden fixing systems.
- CNC-machined timber treads.
- Careful trial assembly in the factory.
- Specialist installers.

A large proportion of the cost is in the concealed steel structure rather than the visible timber.

## What Timber Can You Choose?

Most staircase manufacturers offer a range of hardwoods, including:

- **American White Oak** – the most popular choice; durable, stable, and available in many stain colours.
- **European Oak** – slightly more pronounced grain with a traditional appearance.
- **American Black Walnut** – rich, dark brown, premium look.
- **Ash** – pale with a bold grain, often painted or stained.
- **Sapele** – warm reddish-brown hardwood.
- **Maple** – very light, smooth, and modern.
- **Beech** – fine grain and often painted.

The timber can usually be finished with:

- Clear lacquer.
- Hardwax oil.
- Stain.
- Painted finish (depending on the species).

## Do I Need to Supply the Timber?

In almost every case, no.

A reputable staircase company will manage the entire project, including:

- Measuring your property.
- Producing CAD drawings.
- Structural engineering.
- Sourcing kiln-dried timber.
- Selecting matching boards.
- Machining the treads.
- Finishing the timber.
- Fabricating the steel.
- Powder coating.
- Delivery.
- Installation.
- Final inspection.

This ensures the timber and steel are designed to work together and that warranties remain valid.

Some companies may allow you to provide your own timber — perhaps if you have reclaimed oak or a specific species — but this is less common. They will usually need to inspect it for moisture content, stability, and quality before agreeing to use it.

## A Typical Customer Journey

1. Initial consultation and site survey.
2. Design drawings and quotation.
3. Approval of the design.
4. Timber species, colour, and finish selection.
5. Structural engineering.
6. Manufacturing of the steel and timber.
7. Factory quality checks and trial assembly.
8. Delivery to site.
9. Installation over 2–4 days.
10. Final inspection and handover.

For most homeowners, the staircase company provides a complete design-to-installation service, so you generally don't need to source materials or coordinate different trades yourself. This turnkey approach helps ensure the finished staircase is structurally sound, visually consistent, and compliant with UK Building Regulations.
