# Small Holes in a Hardwood Staircase – Is It Really Woodworm?

Finding a few small holes in a new or recently installed hardwood staircase can understandably cause concern. Many people immediately think the staircase has an active woodworm infestation. In reality, that is not always the case.

There are several reasons why small holes may be present, and understanding how hardwood is prepared and manufactured helps explain why.

## How Hardwood Is Prepared

Before hardwood such as oak, walnut, ash or mahogany is turned into a staircase, it goes through a long preparation process.

After the tree is felled, the timber is sawn into boards and then kiln dried. During kiln drying, the timber is heated under carefully controlled conditions to remove moisture until it reaches the correct moisture content for interior use.

This drying process also creates temperatures that are sufficient to kill any insects, larvae or eggs that may be present inside the timber. Properly kiln-dried timber supplied by reputable mills is not expected to contain living wood-boring insects.

## Why Are Small Holes Sometimes Visible?

The holes you see may have been created before the tree was felled, while it was still growing in the forest.

Some insects naturally bore into living or recently felled trees. Once the timber is kiln dried, the insects are no longer alive, but the tiny tunnels or exit holes may still remain as part of the natural character of the wood.

These holes are historical defects rather than signs of an active infestation.

## Timber Inspection

Every hardwood plank is inspected before it is selected for manufacture.

However, timber is a natural material and every board is unique.

During inspection:

- Knots are assessed.
- Splits are checked.
- Grain direction is examined.
- Natural defects are identified.
- Small holes or marks may be noted.

Because rough-sawn timber has an unfinished surface, very small holes can sometimes be difficult to spot until the timber has been machined and sanded.

## What Happens During Manufacturing?

As the staircase components are machined, sanded and assembled, each piece is inspected again.

If a small isolated hole is found, it is often repaired using a colour-matched wood filler or specialist timber repair compound before the final sanding and finishing process.

If several holes appear close together, or if a section suggests more extensive insect damage, a quality manufacturer will usually reject that portion of the board and cut around it or replace it entirely.

This helps ensure only structurally sound timber is used.

## Does a Small Hole Mean Active Woodworm?

Not necessarily.

An active woodworm infestation usually shows additional signs, such as:

- Fresh, light-coloured wood dust (known as frass) beneath the holes.
- New holes appearing over time.
- Multiple holes concentrated in one area.
- Weak or crumbly timber.
- Evidence of beetles emerging during the appropriate season.

If none of these signs are present, the holes may simply be old, inactive marks that existed before the timber was manufactured.

## Hardwood Quality

High-quality hardwood staircase timber is selected for both appearance and strength.

Because timber is a natural product, small natural characteristics can occasionally remain without affecting the performance of the staircase.

In many grading rules for hardwood, limited natural features are acceptable provided they do not reduce the structural integrity of the timber.

## If You're Unsure

If you discover small holes after installation, ask your staircase supplier or carpenter to inspect them.

A professional can usually determine whether they are:

- Natural historical insect holes.
- Manufacturing defects.
- Filling that has fallen out.
- Or evidence of an active infestation that requires further investigation.

## The Important Point

A few small holes do not automatically mean your staircase has woodworm.

In most cases, hardwood used for staircases has been kiln dried, inspected, machined and finished long before it reaches your home. The kiln-drying process eliminates living insects, and any small holes that remain are often natural marks left from the tree's life before it was processed.

If the timber is sound, dry, and shows no signs of fresh frass or ongoing activity, the holes are usually cosmetic rather than structural. Nevertheless, if you notice new holes appearing or fresh wood dust collecting beneath them, it's sensible to have the staircase inspected so the cause can be confirmed.
