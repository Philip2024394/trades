### The governing principle

- The newel is the structural anchor for the handrail and balustrade. If the newel is loose, the entire handrail can become unstable. *(L33)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
- Newel post installation is one of the most important jobs on a staircase because everything above the string level hangs off the newels. *(L35)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
- **Installation is safety-critical.** NEX may present these techniques as reference knowledge and DIY-confidence guidance (see `customer-faq-staircase.md` Group I), but the actual work should be done by someone competent to judge whether the site conditions match the technique. *(L18)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
  - **verification note (I18 fixed):** restored source verbatim · re-tagged directive/rule

### Two distinct installation jobs

- **Refurbishment** — removing an existing newel and fitting a replacement into an installed staircase. *(L39)*
  - **type:** classification
- **New staircase installation** — a factory-made staircase being assembled on site. *(L40)*
  - **type:** classification

### Newel fixing methods (removal-side awareness)

- **Older staircases** may use: mortise and tenon joints · large timber dowels · wedges · glue · coach screws · nails. *(L52–L59)*
  - **type:** classification
- **Modern staircases** may use: threaded newel fixings · hanger bolts · steel fixing plates · concealed brackets · proprietary fixing kits. *(L61–L67)*
  - **type:** classification
- **Each fixing type requires a different removal method. Guessing risks damaging the string, tread, or surrounding timber.** *(L69)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
  - **verification note (I19 fixed):** re-tagged directive/rule

### Refurbishment removal sequence

- **Step 1 · Remove the handrail assembly.** Normally remove: handrail · balusters · baserail (where necessary). **This exposes the post and makes it easier to inspect what fixing is actually holding it in place.** *(L71–L79)*
  - **type:** process
  - **verification note (I3 + I5 fixed):** restored "Normally" qualifier and trailing inspection clause
- **Step 2 · Look for hidden fixings.** Check for: timber plugs · filler hiding screws · removable caps · bolts underneath the staircase · access panels. Many fixings are deliberately hidden. **A visible newel may reveal its fixing only after a plug is removed or an access panel opened.** *(L81–L91)*
  - **type:** process
  - **verification note (I6 fixed):** restored trailing example sentence
- **Step 3 · Determine the fixing type.** Typical possibilities: *(L93–L107)*
  - **Option A:** Handrail → Newel → Bolted to floor joist
  - **Option B:** Handrail → Newel → Mortise & tenon into string
  - **Option C:** Handrail → Newel → Steel fixing plate
  - **Each requires a different removal method — the pattern matters.**
  - **type:** process
  - **verification note (I2 + I7 fixed):** restored parallel Option A/B/C structure + closing "the pattern matters"
- **Step 4 · Remove carefully.** The goal is to save as much of the staircase as possible. Avoid: splitting strings · damaging treads · damaging flooring. *(L109–L117)*
  - **type:** process
- Sometimes the post comes away intact. Sometimes it must be cut into sections before removal to avoid damaging the fixings-that-can't-easily-be-reached **without breaking the surrounding structure.** *(L117)*
  - **type:** factual
  - **verification note (I8 fixed):** restored trailing clause

### Common refurbishment shortcut

- Many staircase renovators do not remove the entire original post. Instead they: (1) remove handrail · (2) remove spindles · (3) cut the old newel square · (4) leave the lower structural section in place · (5) fit a new box newel over the existing core, OR use a spigot/dowel system. *(L121–L127)*
  - **type:** process
- **This** is quicker and avoids disturbing the staircase structure. The choice depends on the design and how much of the visible newel needs to change vs how much can stay as a hidden core. *(L129)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
  - **verification note (I24 fixed):** removed the added word "shortcut"

### New staircase installation sequence

- Professional staircase manufacturers usually pre-machine the joints. Factory-built staircases often have mortices · tenons · dowel holes · numbered components — the installer assembles per the drawings. *(L135)*
  - **type:** factual
- Typical installation sequence: fit string → fit newel → check square → fit first tread → fit first riser → glue wedges → fit remaining staircase. *(L139–L153)*
  - **type:** process

### The critical geometry check

- Experienced staircase manufacturers recommend checking the newel with a carpenter's / roofing square against the tread, rather than relying only on a spirit level. *(L157)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
- **The reason:** the tread establishes the staircase geometry. A newel that is perfectly plumb by spirit level but not square to the tread will throw the entire balustrade out of alignment. *(L159)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
- **The tread is the reference, not gravity.** *(L159)*
  - **type:** directive/rule

### Adhesives, dowels, wedges

- Normally PVA woodworking adhesive is applied to: mortise · tenon · dowels. *(L163–L167)*
  - **type:** process
  - **verification note (I21 fixed):** removed added "during new installation"
- Large amounts of glue are often used inside structural joints. **This is a case where more glue is genuinely better** — it fills voids and locks the joint against future movement. *(L169)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
- **Many timber staircases use hardwood dowels. Purpose:** *(L173–L177)*
  - **Alignment** — the dowel guides the mating parts together in the correct position.
  - **Strength** — adds shear strength across the joint.
  - **Pulling the joint together** — a well-fitted dowel + glue creates a tight seat.
  - **type:** factual
  - **verification note (I9 + I1 fixed):** restored lead-in + source "Purpose:" framing
- Traditional housed strings use wedges to lock treads and risers into their housings. *(L181)*
  - **type:** factual
- Wedge sequence: string housing → tread inserted → glue applied → wedge driven tight → locks everything together. *(L183–L193)*
  - **type:** process
- **The wedges tighten the joint and help prevent movement.** A modern equivalent (mechanical fixings + adhesive) achieves the same result, but the traditional wedge is still widely used, particularly on premium hardwood staircases. *(L195)*
  - **type:** factual
  - **verification note (I10 + I22 fixed):** restored first sentence + removed added "as traditional wedges"

### Clamping and cure time

- Professional installers often clamp the assembly while the glue cures — keeps the joints fully seated. **The strap/tensioner in `staircase-install-strap-clamp-handrail-balusters.jpg` shows this technique used at scale across a whole flight.** *(L199)*
  - **type:** process
  - **verification note (I11 fixed):** restored second sentence with reference-image example
- **Many manufacturers recommend allowing glued joints to cure fully before putting the staircase into normal use** to reduce future movement or squeaks. *(L201 first sentence)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
- **Walking on a freshly-glued staircase before cure is one of the most common installation-day mistakes.** *(L201 second sentence)*
  - **type:** warning
  - **verification note (v3 restored):** strong-form warning atom silently dropped in v2

### Site adaptation is normal

- Real-world houses have uneven walls · imperfect openings · floors slightly out of level. Even a perfectly-manufactured staircase often needs on-site adjustment — see `staircase-install-dryfit-oak-onsite.jpg`. **This is not a manufacturing failure; it's the reality of fitting bespoke joinery into a real building.** The dry-fit stage exists precisely to identify these adjustments before any glue is applied. *(L205)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
- Real-world installation is not "assemble as manufactured." It is measure · trim · align · then fix. Uneven walls and imperfect openings mean each step must be cut to size to fit. *(L25 image caption)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
- Winder installation involves more coordination than a straight flight because each winder step's geometry depends on the others. *(L26 image caption)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
- Free timber releases stresses when clamped into place — cross-references wood-intelligence Principles 3 and 7. *(L24 image caption)*
  - **type:** factual
  - **verification note (v3 restored):** strap tensioning image caption fact linking installation clamping to wood-intelligence movement/environment principles

### Baluster fitting (system-dependent)

- **The correct method depends on the type of staircase. A timber balustrade is a safety feature — balusters must be securely fixed and correctly spaced.** *(L302)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
  - **verification note (I12 + I20 partial fix):** restored L302 verbatim
- **Never apply a universal baluster-fitting procedure.** Identify the staircase system first (grooved · direct-fix · metal spindle · glass panel), then follow the method for that system. *(L418)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
- Four common systems, each with a different installation method: grooved handrail + baserail · handrail without grooves · metal spindle system · glass panel system. *(L308–L313)*
  - **type:** classification
- **Take photographs before removing everything** — captures the original layout for reference. *(L322)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
  - **verification note (I23 fixed):** source verb "before removing everything" preserved
- Before fitting every spindle, check: equal spacing · straight alignment · correct height. *(L328–L332)*
  - **type:** process
- Before using glue, dry-fit: place every baluster in position · check they all reach the handrail correctly · make sure the handrail sits naturally. *(L336–L341)*
  - **type:** process
- **Don't assume every spindle is exactly the same length** — small variation is normal in bespoke work and site-cut spindles. *(L342)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
  - **verification note (I15 fixed):** re-tagged directive/rule

### Renovation cladding (structural integrity first)

- **Decorative covers should not be used to hide structural problems.** *(L454)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
  - **verification note (I16 fixed):** re-tagged directive/rule
- Before fitting **any** cladding, verify: *(L449)*
  - the staircase structure is solid *(L450)*
  - no rot or water damage *(L451)*
  - no loose treads or strings *(L452)*
  - any squeaks or movement are repaired first *(L452)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
  - **verification note (I4 + I17 fixed):** "any" cladding preserved · re-tagged directive/rule
- Cladding hides but does not fix loose treads, damaged strings, moisture damage, or movement. Covered-over structural problems become worse **(moisture trapped · movement continues · repair now requires removing the cladding as well). The cost of ignoring this rule is much higher than the cost of following it.** *(L526)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
  - **verification note (I13 + I14 fixed):** parenthetical + cost-comparison sentence restored

### Part 8 · Site assembly and section delivery (v3 restored · entire domain)

- **Many bespoke staircases are not delivered as one complete piece** because they would be too large or heavy to get through doors, hallways, or tight corners. *(L574)*
  - **type:** factual
  - **verification note (v3 restored):** Part 8 entire domain silently absent from v2

- **The critical distinction (site assembly · governing rule):** *"A staircase designed for assembly maintains quality. A staircase cut apart on site to make it fit loses strength and accuracy."* *(L578)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
- The first is professional practice. The second is a workshop failure being disguised as an installer problem. *(L580)*
  - **type:** expert perspective

- **Straight staircase delivery options:** assembled flight with newels · handrail · balusters delivered separately · OR strings separate · treads and risers fitted on site. *(L584–L587)*
  - **type:** classification
- **Large or bespoke staircase delivery pattern:** often delivered in sections (bottom newel + first steps · middle flight · top landing section · balustrade + handrail + balusters delivered together). *(L589–L596)*
  - **type:** classification
- Each section's joints are **already planned** in the factory. Assembly follows the plan, not improvisation. *(L598)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
- Professional manufacturers use: housed string joints · dowels · tenons · wedges · bolts · concealed connectors · engineered fixing systems. The staircase is built so the installer can **accurately reconnect** everything on site. *(L602)*
  - **type:** factual

- **Three quality-preservation mechanisms for on-site assembly:** *(L604–L621)*
  - **1 · Accurate factory machining.** Tread housings · riser grooves · newel joints · handrail angles — all machined precisely before delivery.
  - **2 · Correct numbering.** Good companies mark parts (e.g. A1 Bottom newel · A2 First tread · A3 Baluster position 1 · B1 Upper flight · B2 Landing section) — prevents mistakes when reassembling on site.
  - **3 · Factory dry assembly before delivery.** Many quality workshops build the staircase in the factory · check fit · dismantle carefully · package · reinstall on site. **It's like a test build** — catches problems before the site visit.
  - **type:** process

- **What should NOT happen (four-step failure sequence):** *(L624–L628)*
  - ❌ Deliver a complete staircase
  - ❌ Discover it will not fit through the door
  - ❌ Cut strings and joints on site to force it in
  - ❌ Rebuild without proper connections
  - **type:** warning
- **Cost of the failure sequence:** reduced strength · alignment issues · degraded appearance · future squeaks. *(L630)*
  - **type:** warning

- **The one-way problem — dismantling a fully-glued staircase.** A factory-assembled staircase using strong glue joints **may not come apart cleanly** · may damage timber · may weaken joints when rebuilt. Professional staircases intended for site assembly are made differently — the joint strategy is chosen at design time, not decided when the van gets stuck at the front door. *(L634)*
  - **type:** warning

- **Curved staircases are often always in sections.** Large sweeping staircases (like American-style grand staircases) are often delivered in sections including curved strings · laminated handrails · curved landing sections · pre-shaped balustrades. Assembly requires specialist installers — this is not a straight-flight-with-extra-parts. *(L638)*
  - **type:** directive/rule

- **Does site assembly reduce value? No.** Many premium staircases are assembled on site. Quality comes from timber selection · manufacturing accuracy · joint design · installer skill. **A fully assembled staircase is not automatically better.** *(L642)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
  - **verification note (v3 restored):** counters a customer misconception — belongs to directive/rule + expert perspective mix
- **Actually the opposite is often true** — a good staircase maker will **prefer** controlled site assembly in many houses because it reduces damage risk during transport and lets the staircase be fitted perfectly to the building. **Site assembly is a professional choice, not a compromise.** *(L644)*
  - **type:** expert perspective

- **Six customer questions before ordering (Part 8 workshop-side answers · composes with customer-faq Group A + E):** *(L648–L653)*
  - Is the staircase delivered assembled or in sections?
  - Has it been factory trial fitted?
  - Are joints designed for site assembly?
  - Who installs it?
  - Are installation instructions provided?
  - Are any cuts required on site?
  - **type:** classification
  - **verification note (v3 restored):** concrete customer-question atoms silently dropped in v2
