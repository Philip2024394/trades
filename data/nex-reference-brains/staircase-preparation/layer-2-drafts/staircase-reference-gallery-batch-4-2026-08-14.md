## Questions and Answers (informed by this batch)

### Q · Staircase type identification

**Q4.b4.001** · How can NEX identify a straight-flight staircase from a photo?
A · A straight flight rises in a single continuous plane from bottom to top with no turn or landing between. In this batch, 20 of 37 images are straight flights. Key visual cues: parallel stringers running in one plane, treads all facing the same direction, no visible winder or landing.

**Q4.b4.002** · What distinguishes a winder / quarter-turn from a straight flight?
A · A winder / quarter-turn changes direction (usually 90°) either at a landing or through wedge-shaped winder steps. Visual cues: at least one tread that is wider on one side than the other (wedge-shape), OR a small landing where the direction changes. In this batch, 9 of 37 images show winder or quarter-turn configurations.

**Q4.b4.003** · What is a half-turn (U-shape) staircase?
A · Two flights running parallel with a 180° turn (usually via a landing between). One image in this batch (img-4) shows this configuration.

**Q4.b4.004** · What defines a "curved" or "sweeping" staircase?
A · Treads follow a continuous curve rather than a straight axis. In this batch, img-29 and img-33 both show sweeping/curved forms. The curve may be gentle (img-33) or full sweep (img-29 with symmetric flared base).

**Q4.b4.005** · What is a central-spine (mono-stringer) staircase?
A · A structural design where a single central beam runs the length of the flight, with treads cantilevering symmetrically off both sides. Very rare and structurally ambitious. img-35 is the sole example in this batch — matt black steel central spine with light oak treads cantilevering off both sides.

### Q · Direction and orientation

**Q4.b4.006** · Why does NEX record staircase ascending direction?
A · Direction (back-left / back-right / back-centre / sweeping) is a customer-facing property: it determines which wall the stair sits against, which side has the balustrade, and how the stair reads spatially. Never inferred beyond what the pixels support. When view angle obscures direction, mark "ambiguous view".

**Q4.b4.007** · Can two staircases with identical construction have different "directions" in imagery?
A · Yes. img-20 and img-21 in this batch are essentially the same construction (bullnose start, turned oak spindles, brass rods, panelled wainscot) but img-21 uses dark carpet + black lower wainscot, creating a completely different visual mood while direction is identical.

### Q · Balustrade type classification (2026-08-14 batch evidence)

**Q4.b4.008** · What is a "turned spindle" balustrade and when is it typical?
A · Classical wooden spindle with a lathe-turned profile — usually a bulb, urn or vase-shaped detail along its length. In this batch 9 of 37 images use turned spindles, and every single one is in a traditional English setting (cottage, Georgian revival, country estate). Never observed in modern minimal designs.

**Q4.b4.009** · What is a "slim square spindle" balustrade?
A · A plain square-section spindle (often 20-30mm) with no turned profile. In this batch it appears in modern UK builder-grade contexts (img-14, img-15, img-17) and in modern-traditional hybrids (img-13, img-22, img-25). It's the dominant contemporary UK balustrade style at builder-grade level.

**Q4.b4.010** · What is a "horizontal rod" balustrade?
A · Multiple parallel stainless-steel rods running horizontally between newels (rather than vertical spindles). In this batch 3 of 37 images use this — always in modern industrial contexts with warm walnut (img-5, img-27) or grey painted timber (img-34). Never in traditional settings.

**Q4.b4.011** · What is a "slim vertical rod" balustrade?
A · Multiple closely-spaced thin metal rods running vertically (usually matt black 10-16mm powder-coated). Often functions as both balustrade AND partial screen. Seen in img-6, img-30, img-33 — always in modern open-plan or industrial-modern contexts.

**Q4.b4.012** · What is an "ornate metal baluster" (as distinct from turned spindle)?
A · A metal (not wood) baluster with a non-plain profile — often elongated ellipse, lyre-shape, or scroll. img-18 is the single example: matt black powder-coated elongated-ellipse balusters. Sits between classical turned spindle (in silhouette) and modern rod (in material).

**Q4.b4.013** · What is a "vertical timber slat" balustrade?
A · Multiple vertical timber slats (often 25-40mm square-section or slat-profile) running full height. Can function as both balustrade and screen. img-7, img-8, img-24 show this — always modern-luxury or zen-minimalist settings.

**Q4.b4.014** · What is a "frameless glass" balustrade?
A · A single sheet of structural glass (usually 12-19mm toughened + laminated) held by stainless-steel standoffs or a base channel, with no visible frame. In this batch 6 of 37 images use frameless glass — always with either a wooden or metal top handrail cap (img-1, img-31, img-32, img-35, img-36, img-37). Every example is modern-architect grade.

**Q4.b4.015** · What is a "panelled" balustrade (no spindles)?
A · A solid wainscot-style panelled infill replacing spindles entirely. Rare. img-19 is the sole example: recessed field panels form the entire balustrade with a continuous LED handrail on top. Very classical + modern LED combination.

### Q · Structural / joinery details

**Q4.b4.022** · What is a "cantilever" or "floating" tread?
A · A tread supported only from one side (usually a wall on one side, or a central spine on both sides) with no visible stringer or riser below. The tread appears to float. In this batch, 11 of 37 images show cantilever / floating tread constructions — always modern-architect grade.

**Q4.b4.023** · What is a "bullnose" starting step?
A · A wider bottom tread with a rounded outward-projecting edge, usually wrapping around the starting newel. It softens the entry and provides a wider first step. 8 of 37 images have bullnose starting steps (img-2, img-4, img-10, img-16, img-20, img-21, img-33, img-37).

**Q4.b4.024** · What is a "wall-wrapped rounded bullnose"?
A · A special bullnose variant where the rounded starting step curves outward AND returns to meet the adjacent wall. Rare. img-37 shows the only example — the bottom step wraps around the newel base and returns as a curved plinth into the wall.

**Q4.b4.025** · What is a "flared / sweeping" starting step?
A · The bottom 2-4 steps widen dramatically outward (usually with a curved profile) to create a grand entry sweep. Signature of country-estate and Victorian revival staircases. img-29 (symmetric flare with horse-head newels) and img-33 (single-side sweep) show this.

**Q4.b4.026** · What is an "integrated understair storage" unit?
A · Purpose-built cabinetry (usually matching the stair species) fitted into the triangular void under the stringer. img-16 shows a good example: matching pine cabinet doors (double) + 3 drawers with brushed chrome cup handles.

**Q4.b4.027** · What is a "panelled apron" on a stringer?
A · Decorative recessed panels applied to the outer face of the stringer (the diagonal side of the stair visible from below/beside). Matches wall wainscot when present. Observed in img-13, img-19, img-20, img-21, img-25, img-28.
