# Staircase Handrail Components

Handrails are made up of several distinct components that combine to form a continuous, secure and comfortable railing. This document catalogues the components observed in batch 9 evidence and explains how they fit together.

---

## 1 · Handrail moulded profile

The moulded profile is the cross-section of the handrail — the shape that gives it its feel and defines how it accepts the balusters/spindles.

**Batch 9 evidence (img-13):** pine handrail cross-section showing:
- **Top handhold** — rounded / gently mounded top surface where the hand naturally grips
- **Side finger grooves** — moulded shoulders on either side that guide the fingers under the handhold
- **Under-side spindle groove** — a rectangular groove running the length of the underside into which square-topped balusters fit

This is a **standard traditional handrail profile** commonly used with square-top balusters. Variants include:

- **Round mopstick** — simple round handrail (like a broom handle) · common on wall-mounted grab rails
- **Square handrail** — plain square cross-section · minimalist / architect-modern
- **Contoured moulded** — the batch 9 profile with distinct handhold + finger groove + spindle groove
- **Flat oval** — pill / stadium cross-section · modern-transitional
- **Grooved with fillet** — same as batch 9 but with a removable **fillet strip** covering the baluster groove between balusters

---

## 2 · Base rail (matched pair with handrail)

**Batch 9 evidence (img-28, 29):** oak base rail with baluster groove profile + fillet strip.

The base rail is the horizontal element that:
- Runs along the bottom of the balustrade
- Accepts the bottoms of the balusters into a corresponding groove
- Provides the visual and structural counterpart to the handrail

The base rail is often supplied as a **matched pair with the handrail** — same profile family, same timber, same groove size — so the balustrade reads as one integrated system.

The **fillet strip** is a thin timber strip that fills the baluster groove between balusters, hiding the gap and providing a clean visual finish. Fillets are cut to length on install.

On an **openstring / cut-string staircase** the base rail may be omitted on the stair flight (see landing-railings doc for the three-way base-rail rule). The landing balustrade normally has a horizontal base rail regardless.

---

## 3 · Turned newel post

**Batch 9 evidence (img-14):** pine turned newel post — chunky square base + turned column + turned finial cap.

The turned newel is the classical newel design — a lathe-turned column with characteristic profiles (bulb, urn, tapered column, moulded collar, turned finial top). The batch 9 example shows:
- **Chunky square base** (the section that sits into the starting step and receives the base rail)
- **Turned column** (the visible tapered/moulded portion)
- **Turned finial cap** (the decorative top — a small turned bulb-and-collar element)

Turned newels pair naturally with **turned spindles** (bulb-and-urn balusters) and classical mouldings elsewhere in the staircase.

Alternative newel shapes:
- **Square panelled newel** — modern-classical alternative with panelled inserts on the visible faces
- **Plain square** — architect-modern minimal
- **Stainless-clad** — industrial luxury
- **LED-inset** — modern feature-lit

---

## 4 · Swan-neck / gooseneck handrail fitting

**Batch 9 evidence (img-15):** pine swan-neck / gooseneck handrail fitting — the S-curved transition piece that raises the handrail up before continuing horizontal.

The swan-neck (also called gooseneck) is a **prefabricated timber curve** used at specific transitions in the handrail. Common uses:
- Where the stair handrail rises up to meet the higher landing handrail height
- At half-landings where the handrail steps up between flights
- Where the handrail transitions from an inclined section to a horizontal section at a different elevation

The swan-neck is normally shop-turned/CNC-machined as a discrete fitting and installed between two straight handrail sections. It preserves the continuous line of the handrail through geometry changes that a straight joint could not accommodate.

Related fittings:
- **Quarter-turn** — 90° horizontal turn (change of direction on a landing)
- **Half-turn** — 180° handrail turn (rare, top of half-landing designs)
- **Ramp** — a gentle rising ramp fitting (subtle elevation change)
- **Volute** — the terminating scroll (see §5)

## 5 · Volute / scroll handrail termination

**Batch 9 evidence (img-11 full scene + img-12 isolated component):**
- **img-11:** full scene showing a dark mahogany handrail terminating over a white turned newel with a two-step curl (the handrail curves over and down onto the newel top with a small volute)
- **img-12:** isolated pine volute component — the swirl at the base of a handrail

The volute is a **prefabricated curled scroll fitting** that terminates the handrail. It provides:
- A traditional / classical finishing motif
- A structural termination that anchors the handrail into the top of the newel post
- A visual signature that echoes cut-string scroll brackets and other classical stair joinery

Volutes are commonly used:
- On the bottom newel of a bullnose or D-shape starting step
- On the top newel of a formal staircase
- Wherever a formal handrail run needs a decorative termination

Contemporary staircases normally use a **simple curved return** (a straight fitting bending down onto the newel top) rather than a volute. The volute reads classical/traditional.

---

## 6 · Wall-mounted handrail kit

**Batch 9 evidence (img-18):** full stainless steel wall-mounted handrail kit — 3 sections of oak dowel handrail + polished stainless brackets + end caps + fixings.

Wall-mounted handrails are used:
- Where a staircase has a wall on one side (the wall-fixed side of a wall-fixed staircase)
- Where a second handrail is required for accessibility or compliance on both sides
- On landings/corridors where a handrail runs along a wall
- As a subsidiary handrail on a formal grand staircase (main balustrade + wall-side handrail)

Typical kit contents:
- **Dowel handrail sections** (oak, mahogany, or other timber) — round handrails ready to cut to length
- **Brackets** — wall-mounted supports at spaced intervals (polished chrome, brushed stainless, matt black, or brass)
- **End caps** — cover the exposed end grain of the cut handrail
- **Fixings** — screws sized for the wall substrate

Wall-mounted handrails must fix into **structural material** (same principle as newel-post fixings in the landing-railings doc). Fixing only into plasterboard is not acceptable — the fixing must reach studs, timber grounds, or masonry.

---

## 7 · Pyramidal timber wedges (tread-riser assembly)

**Batch 9 evidence (img-27):** two triangular pine wedges — glue-blocks used inside the staircase carriage to reinforce the tread-riser corner.

Wedges are internal joinery components not visible on the finished staircase. They:
- Reinforce the internal corner between tread and riser
- Prevent movement (creaking, deflection) over time
- Are glued into the internal angle where the tread meets the riser and the string housing

Not decorative — installers need to know about them because they distinguish a **properly wedged bespoke staircase** from a nail-only assembly. Wedged construction is the traditional method and normally the correct choice for a quality staircase.

## 8 · How the components combine

A typical traditional staircase assembly, bottom to top:

```
Starting step (bullnose/D-shape/square platform)
        ↓
Bottom newel post (turned/square/panelled)
        ↓
Volute or curved return (handrail termination onto newel top)
        ↓
Handrail (moulded profile) + Base rail (matched profile) with Balusters between
        ↓
Cut-string scroll brackets on the string (traditional cut-string only)
        ↓
Swan-neck / gooseneck at half-landing transition (if present)
        ↓
Handrail continues up to the top newel
        ↓
Top newel post
        ↓
Newel cap (flat / ball finial / pyramidal / metal)
        ↓
Landing base rail + balusters + landing handrail
        ↓
Half newels against the wall where the landing railing terminates
```

Each component is chosen from the compatible family (traditional / modern / minimal / industrial) — mixing families is a deliberate design choice, not a default.

## 9 · Handrail-components Q&A (NEX advisor evidence)

**Q · What is a moulded handrail profile?**
A · The cross-section shape of the handrail. Common profiles include round mopstick, plain square, contoured moulded (with handhold + finger grooves + spindle groove), flat oval, and grooved-with-fillet. The choice determines the tactile feel and how balusters attach.

**Q · What is a fillet strip on a handrail or base rail?**
A · A thin timber strip that fills the baluster groove between balusters, hiding the gap and providing a clean visual finish. Cut to length on install.

**Q · What is a swan-neck / gooseneck?**
A · A prefabricated timber S-curve used at specific handrail transitions — where the stair handrail rises up to meet the higher landing handrail, at half-landings, or where the handrail changes elevation. Preserves a continuous handrail line through geometry changes.

**Q · What is a volute?**
A · The curled scroll fitting that terminates a handrail — commonly at the bottom newel of a bullnose starting step. Reads classical/traditional. Contemporary staircases usually use a plain curved return instead.

**Q · Do modern staircases use volutes?**
A · Not normally. The volute is a classical/traditional motif. Modern and minimalist staircases use a plain curved return where the handrail bends down onto the newel top without decorative scroll.

**Q · What is a mopstick handrail?**
A · A plain round handrail (like a broom handle). Common on wall-mounted grab rails and simple secondary handrails. Not normally used as a main balustrade handrail.

**Q · What is a wall-mounted handrail kit?**
A · A prefabricated kit containing round timber dowel handrail sections + brackets (polished chrome, brushed stainless, matt black or brass) + end caps + screws. Used where a staircase has a wall on one side, or where a second handrail is required on both sides.

**Q · Can I add a wall-mounted handrail to an existing staircase?**
A · Yes — commonly. The fixing must reach structural material (studs, timber grounds, or masonry). Fixing only into plasterboard is not acceptable.

**Q · Do I need wedges inside my staircase?**
A · A quality traditional staircase uses glue blocks (wedges) internally to reinforce the tread-riser corners. Not visible on the finished staircase but distinguishes bespoke wedged construction from nail-only assembly.

**Q · What is a turned newel?**
A · A classical newel post shaped on a lathe with characteristic profiles (bulb, urn, tapered column, moulded collar, turned finial top). Pairs naturally with turned spindles and other classical joinery.

**Q · What is a matched-pair handrail and base rail?**
A · A handrail and base rail supplied as a coordinated pair — same profile family, same timber, same baluster groove size — so the balustrade reads as one integrated system.
