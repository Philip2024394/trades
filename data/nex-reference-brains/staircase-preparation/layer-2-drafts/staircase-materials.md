### Specification and value (Principle 1)

- A professional tradesperson does not buy "a staircase". They buy timber thickness, profile sizes, machining quality, strength, finish level, and lifespan. *(L21)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
- Two staircases quoted at wildly different prices are often different specifications, not different values. *(L23)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
- Suppliers can reduce cost by reducing material specification (thickness, profile size, section) without the customer realising the two quotes are not like-for-like. *(L32)*
  - **type:** expert perspective

  - **type:** factual

  - **type:** factual

  - **type:** factual

  - **type:** factual

  - **type:** factual

### Knot knowledge (Principle 6 · NEW SECTION v2)

- **A knot is not automatically a defect.** Judge every knot by three axes: type (live vs dead), size, and location in the finished component. *(L337)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
  - **verification note (v2 restored):** P6 entire atomic set was silently dropped in v1 · this rule is the P6 gate
- **Live knot (sound knot)** — from a branch that was still alive while the tree was growing. Wood fibres connected to surrounding timber · usually tight and solid · often the same colour family as surrounding timber · less likely to fall out. The tree continues growing and surrounds the live branch with new wood — the connection stays intact. *(L344–L351)*
  - **type:** classification
- **Dead knot (black knot)** — from a branch that died but remained inside the tree. Dark colour · black appearance · poor connection to surrounding wood · can loosen or fall out during drying or machining. The tree continues growing around the dead branch, but the old branch wood is no longer actively connected to the new growth. *(L353–L360)*
  - **type:** classification
- **Why dead knots become black:** dead branches don't seal cleanly. Moisture enters through cracked bark; decay organisms follow; the knot area darkens through slow decomposition long before the tree is harvested. *(L363–L364)*
  - **type:** factual
- **Aggressive drying (kiln pushed too fast) makes it worse** — the outside dries faster than the inside · stresses develop · cracks appear · resin migrates · colour changes intensify. Pine especially contains resin, which can darken further around knots. *(L366)*
  - **type:** factual
- **Location matters as much as type.** The same knot can be perfectly acceptable in one component and completely unacceptable in another. *(L390)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
- **Small sound knot placement matrix:** hidden riser (painted) ✓ fine · painted newel (concealed by decoration) ✓ fine · visible clear-finished tread nose ⚠ cosmetic concern only · clear-finished handrail (grip area) ✗ reject · structural string / load path ✗ reject. *(L394–L398)*
  - **type:** classification
- **Loose dead knot placement matrix:** hidden riser (painted) ⚠ may be OK if not loose · painted newel (concealed by decoration) ⚠ depends on size · visible clear-finished tread nose ✗ reject · clear-finished handrail (grip area) ✗ reject · structural string / load path ✗ reject. *(L394–L398)*
  - **type:** classification
- An experienced maker instinctively decides *"this piece can go into a hidden riser but not into a visible tread nose"*. NEX must eventually carry the same judgement — surfacing knots with a **classification + placement recommendation**, not a pass/fail verdict. *(L400)*
  - **type:** expert perspective

### Timber grade language

- **Clear grade** — few or no visible knots · used for premium visible work (handrails · newels · tread nosings). *(L406)*
  - **type:** classification
- **Prime grade** — occasional small sound knots acceptable · used for most quality staircase work. *(L407)*
  - **type:** classification
- **Character grade** — visible knots intentionally shown · used for rustic aesthetics. *(L408)*
  - **type:** classification
- **Rustic grade** — heavy knot pattern · used decoratively or where knots become a feature. *(L409)*
  - **type:** classification
- **NEX should never present a grade as universally "better"** — grade is only meaningful once the component and its final application are known. *(L411)*
  - **type:** directive/rule

### Timber movement (Principle 7)

- **A customer thinks:** *"the wood has shrunk, so the staircase was made wrong."* *(L439)*
  - **type:** customer perception
- **An experienced staircase maker thinks:** *"timber movement is normal — the question is whether the timber was correctly dried, designed, stored, installed, and finished."* *(L439)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
  - **verification note (M10 fixed):** customer-vs-expert contrast structure preserved
- A staircase is expected to move slightly. **The skill is controlling where and how that movement happens.** *(L441)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
- Wood movement must be managed through correct drying, design, storage and installation. **Movement itself is never a defect — uncontrolled movement is.** *(L449)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
- A stable staircase is not one that never moves; it is one where movement is expected and controlled. *(L459)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
- **Wood is a living material after harvesting.** Even after a tree is cut, the cells remain responsive · moisture continues moving in and out · the timber seeks balance with the surrounding air. **A metal staircase does not breathe. A timber staircase does.** *(L465)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
  - **verification note (v2 restored):** metal-vs-timber contrast is the WHY explanation for all downstream movement facts · silently dropped in v1
- Wood does not shrink equally in every direction. *(L469)*
  - **type:** factual
- **Long grain (along the length):** very little movement · a 3m handrail rarely becomes dramatically shorter. *(L471)*
  - **type:** factual
- **Across the grain:** much more movement · affects tread width · riser width · panels · boards. *(L472)*
  - **type:** factual
- **Through thickness:** noticeable movement · important for treads · handrails · window boards. *(L473)*
  - **type:** factual
  - **verification note (M4 fixed):** three grain-direction bullets preserved as distinct facts
- A 900mm oak tread may shrink from 900mm to 895–898mm across its width as moisture stabilises — **the exact amount depends on species · moisture content · board orientation · environment.** *(L475)*
  - **type:** factual
- **That's not a defect; that's timber.** *(L475)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
- **The wider the piece, the more important:** timber selection · grain orientation · drying · construction method. This is why manufacturers sometimes join pieces — a carefully made laminated or edge-jointed component can be more stable than one wide solid board. **"One piece is always better" is not correct.** *(L510–L512)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
  - **verification note (v2 restored):** expert-judgement atom directly countering a customer misconception · silently dropped in v1
- **Timber is not defective because it moves. Timber is defective when movement was not considered during selection, drying, design, or installation.** *(L597)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
  - **verification note (v2 restored):** customer-education capstone Philip authored as the summary line for all P7 content · silently dropped in v1

### Common movement conditions

- **Bow** — curve along the length. Causes: uneven drying · internal stress · irregular grain · cutting position in log · poor storage. **Trade view:** judged by severity + intended component. *(L481)*
  - **type:** classification
- **Cup** — curve across the width. Causes: uneven moisture between faces · quarter-sawn preferred to minimise. **Trade view:** small cup on a wide board may still be planed flat. *(L482)*
  - **type:** classification
- **Twist** — corners move differently. Causes: unstable grain · poor drying · tension in timber. **Trade view:** usually harder to correct than bow or cup. *(L483)*
  - **type:** classification
- **Crook** — sideways curve along the edge. Causes: uneven growth · reaction wood. **Trade view:** depends on length + component. *(L484)*
  - **type:** classification
- **Checking** — small cracks as stress releases. Cause: over-fast drying · surface dries much faster than interior. **Trade view:** not every crack is a failure · location matters. *(L485)*
  - **type:** classification
- **Shrinkage / gaps** — small joints open after installation. Cause: timber acclimatising to new environment. **Trade view:** distinguish normal seasonal adjustment from a defect. *(L486)*
  - **type:** classification
  - **verification note (M5 fixed):** cutting-position cause restored for bow, Trade view column restored per row

### Sawing patterns

- **Plain sawn** — rings appear curved (cathedral pattern) · higher yield from the log · more visual figure · more movement potential across the width. *(L560)*
  - **type:** classification
- **Rift sawn** — rings close to 45° · balance between yield and stability · less pronounced figure · moderate movement. *(L561)*
  - **type:** classification
- **Quarter sawn** — rings close to vertical (nearly perpendicular to face) · lower yield · very stable · shows medullary rays on oak (attractive on treads and premium panels) · minimal movement. *(L562)*
  - **type:** classification
  - **verification note (M7 fixed):** source parentheticals preserved

### Moisture (Principle 8)

- **The goal is not "zero moisture". The goal is timber moisture matching the environment where it will live.** *(L607)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
- Typical indoor target moisture content for UK: 8–12%. Common workshop target: 9–11%. *(L616)*
  - **type:** factual
- Timber too wet at installation shrinks and gaps open; timber too dry absorbs moisture and expands. **Correct moisture at installation prevents both failure modes.** *(L618)*
  - **type:** factual
  - **verification note (M12 fixed):** summary sentence preserved
- Two main instruments to test moisture content: pin moisture meter and pinless moisture meter. *(L624)*
  - **type:** factual
- **Pin moisture meter** — small metal pins penetrate the wood and measure electrical resistance (wet timber conducts differently from dry timber). *(L630)*
  - Advantages (✓): more accurate at a specific depth · can test below the surface · **good for stair parts and joinery**. *(L632–L634)*
  - **type:** factual
- **Pinless moisture meter** — sits on the surface, uses electromagnetic sensing. *(L636)*
  - Advantages (✓): no pin marks · quick testing across many boards. *(L638–L639)*
  - Concern (⚠): less precise · affected by thickness and surface conditions. *(L640)*
  - **type:** factual
- Correct moisture testing measures both ends, the middle of each board, near any knots, and different faces. *(L646–L649)*
  - **type:** process

### Component size and moisture behaviour (v2 restored · was silently dropped)

- **Component size dramatically affects moisture behaviour.** A single MC number is misleading if you don't factor in component size. A skilled manufacturer does not judge a 90mm newel post and a 28mm tread by the same test. *(L679–L681)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
  - **verification note (v2 restored):** entire component-size section was silently dropped in v1
- **Baluster** (38 × 38mm) — moisture escapes easily · dries fast · reads consistent. *(L684)*
  - **type:** factual
- **Tread** (28mm thick × 900mm wide) — thin enough to dry evenly · wide face means movement across width matters. *(L685)*
  - **type:** factual
- **String** (38mm thick × long) — structural · length + section make replacement difficult · MC must be right before install. *(L686)*
  - **type:** factual
- **Handrail (long)** (e.g. 4.2m length) — long enough to bow noticeably if MC is wrong · storage matters as much as MC. *(L687)*
  - **type:** factual
- **Newel post** (90 × 90mm+) — centre dries much slower than surface · surface may read 10% while core reads 14%. *(L688)*
  - **type:** factual
- **Example:** a 90mm oak newel post — surface at 10% MC · centre at 14% MC. The outside says dry, but the inside is still adjusting. **Ignore this and the newel will move after installation.** *(L690)*
  - **type:** warning
- **The rule:** the larger the timber section, the more important controlled drying and acclimatisation become — not because it has a higher moisture percentage overall, but because moisture moves in and out more slowly. *(L692)*
  - **type:** directive/rule

### Kiln drying and cycle quality (v2 restored)

- Before timber reaches a staircase workshop, the mill controls temperature · airflow · humidity through a kiln cycle. The timber is slowly dried to reduce cracking · warping · excessive movement. *(L706)*
  - **type:** process
- **Fast drying creates problems:** surface dries too quickly · internal stress builds · checking (small cracks) appears · the board may look dry on the outside but hold moisture inside. *(L708–L713)*
  - **type:** warning
- **"Kiln dried" on a delivery note is not the whole answer — the cycle quality matters as much as the fact that a kiln was used.** *(L715)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
  - **verification note (v2 restored):** kiln quality directive silently dropped in v1

### Moisture-at-installation failure modes (v2 restored)

- **If timber is too wet at installation:** handrail installed at high MC → house heating starts → timber loses moisture → shrinks → joints open · cracks may appear. Possible outcomes: gaps · movement · twisting · splits. *(L718–L731)*
  - **type:** warning
- **If timber is too dry at installation:** very dry timber can absorb moisture from the air · expand · create pressure at joints. Example: a dry tread installed in a humid environment may expand across its width, potentially pushing against risers or strings and creating pressure damage. *(L735–L737)*
  - **type:** warning
  - **verification note (v2 restored):** both failure-mode flows silently dropped in v1
