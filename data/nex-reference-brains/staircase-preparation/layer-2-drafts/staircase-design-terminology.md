### Design timing (Principle A)

- Staircases affect floor openings · ceiling heights · doors · lighting · walls · storage areas. A staircase designed too late becomes a compromise. *(L40)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
- **The value of a staircase designer decreases sharply the later they are involved. Peak value is at the house design stage; near-zero value is the day the installer arrives.** *(L46)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
  - **verification note (D8 fixed):** re-tagged from expert perspective to directive/rule. Source presents this in a JSON "rule" block · framed as a rule.
- Design sequence: house design stage → staircase concept → structural openings confirmed → manufacturing drawings → construction → installation. *(L26–L38)*
  - **type:** process

### Design economy (Principle B)

- **Many people assume "a designer adds cost." An experienced designer often reduces waste.** *(L53)*
  - customer belief · then expert correction
  - **type:** customer perception (first sentence) · expert perspective (second sentence)
  - **verification note (D5 fixed):** restored the customer perception setup that the whole principle is designed to correct. Draft v1 kept only the expert view and dropped the customer belief.
- Worked example: for a customer wanting a luxury oak staircase, the designer proposes: Oak treads (visible · touched daily) · Oak handrail (visible · touched daily) · Painted newels (visible but paint hides pine) · Painted strings (mostly hidden by carpet or wall). Result: staircase looks premium · costs materially less than "all oak everything". That's design judgement, not cost cutting. *(L55–L62)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
- **Spend more on:** handrail · visible treads · newel posts · feature balustrade — anything the customer touches or sees. *(L66)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
- **Spend less on:** hidden structure · painted components · areas nobody sees. *(L68)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
- Customer perception of quality tracks the components they interact with. Premium hidden material returns no perceived value. *(L75)*
  - **type:** expert perspective

### Design must start with the building (Principle C)

- A customer sees a beautiful curved staircase online and wants it. But the house may not have enough space, correct structure, or budget. **The design must start with the building.** *(L81)*
  - **type:** customer perception (first two sentences) · directive/rule (final sentence)
  - **verification note (D6 + D9 fixed):** restored the customer scenario setup. Re-tagged the final directive from expert perspective to directive/rule.

### The three most expensive staircase mistakes (Principle C)

- **Mistake 1 · Wrong measurements.** Small errors create large problems. Critical measurements: floor-to-floor height · available length · opening size · headroom · wall positions. *(L85)*
  - **type:** directive/rule (avoid this mistake)
- **Mistake 2 · Choosing style before layout.** Style must fit the building geometry, not the other way around. *(L86)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
- **Mistake 3 · Ignoring the handrail.** It's what people touch every day. A premium staircase gets its luxury feel from handrail profile · smooth sanding · timber selection · finish quality. *(L87)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
- **verification note (D3 fixed):** each of the three mistakes now carries its full explanation from source. Draft v1 had compressed them to just the titles, losing the reasoning behind each one.

### Journey design (Principle D)

- **Great designers** think of the staircase as a journey through the house, not a set of components. Every position along the journey is a design opportunity. *(L91)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
  - **verification note (D4 fixed):** restored plural "Great designers" from source (was singular "A great designer" in draft v1).

### Landing as design feature (Principle D)

- A landing can become a major design feature: glass overlooking the hall · feature window behind staircase · timber panelling · artwork wall · lighting feature. *(L108)*
  - **type:** classification
- The landing is often overlooked. *(L106)*
  - **type:** expert perspective

- **Storage:** pull-out drawers · cupboards · shoe storage. *(L112)*
  - **type:** classification
- **Display:** bookshelves · lighting · wine storage. *(L113)*
  - **type:** classification
- **Working space:** small office · reading area. *(L114)*
  - **type:** classification
  - **verification note (D19 fixed):** removed the "under-stair" label that had been appended to each fact in v1. Source uses "Storage:", "Display:", "Working space:" under the "Under-stair (valuable space)" section header. The section header now labels this subsection instead.

### Wall-side detailing (Principle D · where cheap staircases look unfinished)

- Premium details: matching timber panels · tongue-and-groove boards · decorative mouldings · shadow gaps · hidden lighting. *(L118)*
  - **type:** classification
- **The staircase should look good from every angle.** *(L118)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
  - **verification note (D10 fixed):** re-tagged from expert perspective to directive/rule.

### Proportion (Principle E)

- A beautiful staircase is often about proportion, not just expensive materials. *(L122)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
- The relationship between rise (step height) and going (step depth) determines walking comfort and appearance. *(L122)*
  - **type:** factual
- A staircase can be technically correct but feel uncomfortable if the proportions are wrong. *(L122)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
- Designers balance walking comfort · appearance · available space. *(L124)*
  - **type:** expert perspective

### Design for future repair (Principle F)

- A good staircase designer thinks 20–50 years ahead: *(L128)*
  - Can the handrail be refinished?
  - Can damaged treads be repaired?
  - Are replacement parts available?
  - Will the style age well?
  - **type:** directive/rule
- The cheapest staircase is not always the cheapest over its lifetime. *(L135)*
  - **type:** expert perspective

### The architectural family principle (Principle G)

- **The staircase should not be designed alone. It should belong to the architectural family of the house** — doors · skirting · architraves · flooring · wall panels · lighting all form one design language. *(L139)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
  - **verification note (D11 fixed):** re-tagged from expert perspective to directive/rule.
- **A staircase that fights the door style, skirting profile, or flooring language feels wrong — even when every individual component is high quality. Design coherence is a bigger contributor to perceived value than any single upgrade.** *(L180 · source JSON `reason` field)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
  - **verification note (v3 restored · post retroactive-omission-audit):** distinct expert-reasoning atom from Principle G JSON block silently dropped in v2. TERMS section listed "design coherence" but no fact-line captured the "bigger contributor than any single upgrade" claim. Restored per "no data missed" rule (Philip 2026-07-30).

### Common door-style pairings (Principle G · restored full table structure)

Source presents this as a three-column table: Door style (with description) · Matching staircase · Typical setting. Draft v2 restores all three columns.

- **4-panel doors** (raised & fielded, oak or painted) *(L145)*
  - **Matching staircase:** closed string · turned newels · traditional balusters · oak handrail · decorative brackets · timber panelling
  - **Typical setting:** Victorian · Georgian-inspired · country houses · **see reference image**
  - **type:** classification
- **Shaker doors** (flat recessed panels, clean square lines) *(L146)*
  - **Matching staircase:** oak staircase · square newels · square balusters · glass panels · optional black metal balusters
  - **Typical setting:** modern-traditional bridge · currently one of the strongest combinations
  - **type:** classification
- **Flush doors** (flat face, hidden frames) *(L147)*
  - **Matching staircase:** floating stairs · steel spine · glass balustrades · LED lighting · open risers
  - **Typical setting:** modern apartments · luxury new builds
  - **type:** classification
- **Cottage doors** (vertical boards · ledged & braced · T&G) *(L148)*
  - **Matching staircase:** rustic oak · chunky handrails · natural knots · wide boards · farmhouse styling
  - **Typical setting:** exposed beams · stone floors · country interiors
  - **type:** classification
- **Glazed doors** (glass panels · steel/timber frame) *(L149)*
  - **Matching staircase:** open staircases · glass balustrades · lighter woods
  - **Typical setting:** light-flooded modern interiors · visual connection between rooms
  - **type:** classification
- **Victorian doors** (4 or 5 panels · deep mouldings) *(L150)*
  - **Matching staircase:** turned spindles · curved handrails · ornate newels · stained glass · dark woods
  - **Typical setting:** period restorations · mahogany or dark oak
  - **type:** classification
- **Modern luxury doors** (tall · hidden frames · walnut veneer) *(L151)*
  - **Matching staircase:** sweeping · curved handrails · glass · brass details · premium hardwoods
  - **Typical setting:** architect-designed houses · high-end renovations
  - **type:** classification
- **verification note (D1 + D2 fixed):** restored the three-column table structure with the source's door descriptions (which had been entirely dropped in v1) and full setting details. The header "Common pairings" is preserved.

### Door dimensions

- Standard doors (normal ceiling height) — traditional · comfortable feel. *(L155)*
  - **type:** classification
- Tall doors (2.1m–2.4m+) — luxury · modern · larger-space feel · usually needs a staircase with stronger proportions. *(L156)*
  - **type:** classification

### Door colour compositions

- White painted doors — works with oak stairs · painted stairs · modern interiors. *(L160)*
  - **type:** classification
- Oak doors — works with oak staircase · natural timber floors · warm interiors. *(L161)*
  - **type:** classification
- Walnut / dark wood doors — works with luxury staircases · dark handrails · premium interiors. *(L162)*
  - **type:** classification

### Timber and finish selection are design decisions (Principle H · restored)

- **A designer should understand that not all oak or pine is equal** — grain direction · knot placement · colour variation · board width · stability · matching between components all matter. *(L186)*
  - **type:** directive/rule
  - **verification note (D7 major fix):** Principle H was ENTIRELY DROPPED from draft v1. Restored here as its own subsection.
- **A handrail should not be chosen the same way as a hidden string.** *(L186)*
  - **type:** directive/rule

### Finish selection (Principle H · continued)

- The same staircase can look completely different depending on finish. *(L188)*
  - **type:** factual
- **Natural oil** — shows grain · texture · warmth. *(L190)*
  - **type:** classification
- **Clear lacquer** — protection · cleaner appearance. *(L191)*
  - **type:** classification
- **Stained finish** — changes colour · character. *(L192)*
  - **type:** classification
- **Painted** — colour matching · lower timber cost acceptable. *(L193)*
  - **type:** classification

### Multi-disciplinary design (Principle I)

- The next generation of staircase designer will combine: traditional stair maker + interior designer + 3D visualisation + material expert + cost planner. *(L200–L208)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
- The best staircase companies will not just sell *"a staircase"*. They will sell *"a staircase designed specifically for your home, your lifestyle, and your budget."* *(L211)*
  - **type:** expert perspective
