# Staircase Starting Steps

The **starting step** (also called the **bottom step**, **entry step**, **feature step** or **welcome step**) is the first tread of a staircase — the tread that meets the finished floor at the bottom of the flight.

Starting steps are treated as a distinctive design element rather than a generic tread. They can widen the base of the staircase, provide the visual anchor for the bottom newel post, welcome the eye onto the flight, and — on modern staircases — carry LED lighting or feature accents.

---

## 1 · The construction–design relationship (Philip · 2026-08-14)

**The construction of the staircase determines what starting-step projections are physically possible. The designer then chooses the appropriate aesthetic treatment within what the construction permits.**

This is a **construction/design relationship**, not a compliance rule. It is not "two-sided cut-string always requires X" or "wall-fixed can only be Y." Manufacturers and bespoke joiners still have legitimate freedom in how the starting step is detailed — the geometry rule only bounds what is physically possible, not what must be chosen.

### What construction permits (physical possibilities)

- A rounded / bullnose / D-shape / half-round / oval starting step is a **projection that wraps outward beyond the flight**. An outward-wrapping profile can only exist on a side that is open.
- **Two-sided cut-string staircase** (both sides open) → both sides *can* be rounded / wrapped outward. Whether the design chooses to do so is a separate aesthetic decision.
- **Wall-fixed staircase** (one side against a wall) → the wall side *cannot* accommodate an outward-wrapping projection (the wall blocks it). The free side *can* be rounded / wrapped outward. What the free side chooses to be — bullnose, curtail, square, or nothing — is a separate aesthetic decision.
- **Square platform** or **extended-tread** starting steps project **forward, not outward** — they can work on any construction type (no wall conflict), so the aesthetic choice is unconstrained by construction on this axis.
- **No projecting starting step** (first tread flush at floor level) is always a valid choice, on any construction type, and is common where floor space or minimalism dictates.

### Common aesthetic tendencies (not rules)

- Two-sided cut-string staircases **often** treat both open sides symmetrically (both rounded or both square) because visual symmetry reads intentionally.
- Wall-fixed staircases **often** concentrate decorative detail on the free / open side because that is the side people see.
- These are common design tendencies, not required outcomes. Manufacturer detail, bespoke design and customer preference can still legitimately produce arrangements outside the common tendency.

### What NEX must not do

- Never claim a construction type **requires** a specific starting-step choice.
- Never assume the aesthetic choice from the construction type alone (e.g. don't assume both-sided rounded on every two-sided cut-string staircase — the designer may have chosen square platforms, or nothing at all).
- Never generate imagery showing an outward-wrapping projection into a wall — that is physically impossible, and NEX must respect what construction permits.
- If NEX cannot determine construction type or aesthetic intent, ask the customer. Both are customer-verifiable evidence, not inferences.

## 2 · Why starting steps exist

The starting step performs several jobs at once:

1. **Widens the base of the staircase** — a wider first step creates a more welcoming and stable-looking entry.
2. **Provides an anchor for the bottom newel post** — the newel sits within the widened profile, not on the very edge.
3. **Signals the beginning of the flight visually** — the eye reads the widened step as an invitation to climb.
4. **Softens hard geometry** — bullnose / half-round / oval profiles remove the sharp corner that a plain first tread would create at the bottom.
5. **Provides a place for LED lighting features** — modern designs commonly place LED cove wash under the starting step or LED-inset spots into the riser.
6. **Traditionally carries decorative detail** — Victorian and Georgian staircases often use elaborate bullnose or double-step designs with turned newels.

A staircase can be built with no projecting starting step at all — the first tread starts flush with the floor. This is common in tight hallways, boxed-in staircases, and where floor space is a constraint. The absence of a starting step is a **design choice**, not a mistake.

## 3 · Starting-step families (types observed in NEX evidence)

### 3.1 · Bullnose starting step
A tread with a **rounded (semi-circular) front edge** that projects forward from the second tread. The simplest and most common starting step. The rounded profile wraps forward and can also wrap around one or both sides depending on construction type.

**Sub-types by end profile:**
- **Bullnose (front only)** — rounded projection at the front, straight sides.
- **Bullnose with rounded ends** — rounded profile continues around one or both ends.
- **Bullnose one side + wall side** — rounded projection only on the free side (wall-fixed staircase).

### 3.2 · Half-round starting step
A tread whose front and one end are formed from a **single continuous semi-circular curve**, creating a distinct half-round protrusion at the base of the staircase. Reads as a soft "bump" at the entry.

### 3.3 · D-shape / oval starting step
A wider elongated bullnose forming a **flat D-shape or oval** in plan view. Popular on grand staircases and Georgian/Federal designs. Two-sided cut-string staircases commonly use D-shape/oval starting steps because both ends of the D can wrap outward.

### 3.4 · Square platform starting step
A **flat rectangular step** without any rounded profile — just a plain wider platform tread at the base. Reads as contemporary, minimalist, architect-modern. Common on modern square-spindle and stainless-rod staircases where a rounded bullnose would clash with the minimalist geometry.

### 3.5 · Double starting step (stacked)
**Two starting steps stacked** at the base of the flight — either two D-shape / oval bullnoses (traditional grand entry) or two square platforms (contemporary). The double step widens the base considerably and creates a stronger visual anchor.

### 3.6 · Combined step (square platform bottom + bullnose top)
A **square platform at the very bottom + a D-shape / bullnose step above it**. Combines the width of a platform with the softened geometry of a bullnose. Common on wider grand staircase entries.

### 3.7 · No projecting starting step
The **first tread starts flush** at the floor level with no projection or rounding. Common in narrow hallways or where minimalism/space is required. Always a valid choice.

## 4 · How the starting step meets the newel post

The **bottom newel post** almost always sits at the free/open corner of the starting step:

- **Bullnose / half-round / D-shape / oval** → the newel is normally set into the curved profile, with the timber wrapping around its base. The rounded projection accommodates the newel's footprint.
- **Square platform** → the newel sits at the corner of the platform, flush with the edge.
- **Wall-fixed staircase** → the free-side newel sits at the free end of the widened profile; the wall side has no newel and the tread terminates against the wall skirting.
- **Two-sided cut-string staircase** → newels sit at BOTH free-side ends of the widened profile.

The bullnose/D-shape profile also typically incorporates a **volute** or **scroll handrail termination** where the handrail curls down onto the newel — see the handrail-components knowledge doc for the volute component.

## 5 · Materials and finishes observed in NEX evidence

Batch 9 evidence shows bullnose starting-step treads in the following timber finishes:

- **White painted** (fully painted timber tread + white riser support)
- **Natural light oak** (untreated / clear-oil finish · pale golden)
- **Pale maple / beech** (very light cream)
- **Warm cherry** (mid-red-brown natural)
- **Red mahogany** (rich reddish-brown)
- **Dark walnut** (dark chocolate brown)
- **Rustic dark oak** (variegated grain · knot-visible)
- **Pale unfinished beech-like** (very pale golden)

Each of these can be combined with painted (usually white or off-white) risers to create a **two-tone starting step** — a common English cottage-and-modern-British finish where the tread and the visible skirting/apron read as separate elements.

For hardwood staircases the starting step normally matches the flight treads. For carpeted staircases the starting step is often left as **exposed timber** (bullnose showing) even where the rest of the flight is carpeted — the bullnose becomes the feature.

## 6 · Starting steps and LED lighting integration

Modern staircase designs frequently combine the starting step with LED lighting:

- **LED cove wash under the starting step** — indirect glow spilling onto the floor from beneath the bullnose profile
- **LED-inset step lights on the starting-step riser** — small square inset spots recessed into the riser face
- **LED strip along the perimeter of the starting step** — continuous glow outlining the bullnose edge
- **LED under-nosing on each tread** — continuous glow just below each nosing (not exclusive to starting step, but often extends from it)

LED integration is easier on a wider starting step because the profile has more surface area to accommodate the driver, wiring, and reflection surface.

## 7 · Cut-string scroll brackets and their relationship to the starting step

A **cut-string staircase** exposes the sides of the treads and risers. On traditional cut-string staircases, decorative **scroll stair brackets** are commonly applied to the string just below each tread nosing — small carved or CNC-cut ornamental profiles that echo the volute at the handrail.

When a cut-string staircase has a bullnose starting step, the scroll bracket at the base of the string terminates in a **larger scroll motif** that visually anchors the starting step. This is a design language — the starting step, the volute, and the scroll brackets belong to the same visual vocabulary.

On both-sided cut-string staircases, scroll brackets appear on **both** stringers. On wall-fixed cut-string staircases, scroll brackets appear only on the **free-side** stringer (the wall-side is boxed in).

## 8 · Choosing a starting-step type

Considerations:

- **Construction type** — two-sided cut-string vs wall-fixed (governs which sides can be rounded — see §1).
- **Available floor space** — a projecting starting step needs the floor area to project into. Narrow hallways may not permit it.
- **Staircase style** — grand / period / traditional staircases favour bullnose / D-shape / half-round; modern / minimalist staircases favour square platform or no projection.
- **Newel post design** — a large turned or ornate newel benefits from a bullnose/D-shape base to accommodate its footprint; slim modern newels work well on a square platform.
- **Flooring transition** — the starting step edge must resolve cleanly against the floor finish (LVT / laminate / hardwood / tile / carpet). A bullnose can mask a slight misalignment; a square platform requires precise floor alignment.
- **LED lighting intent** — planned LED cove wash or riser lights are easier to detail on wider starting steps.
- **Traffic flow** — a projecting starting step should not obstruct the natural walking path through the hallway.

## 9 · Two-tread starting sequences

Starting steps can be delivered as **two stacked steps** at the base:

- **Two D-shape / oval bullnoses** — traditional grand entrance, popular on Georgian/Federal designs. Both bullnoses share the same profile; the second (upper) is set back by one tread depth.
- **Two square platforms** — contemporary minimalist grand entrance. Both platforms rectangular.
- **Combined (square platform + D-shape / bullnose above)** — the platform provides the widest base; the bullnose above provides the softened transition to the flight.

Two-tread sequences require additional floor space and are more common on wider staircases (grand halls, hotel-scale, generous domestic).

## 10 · Starting-step Q&A (NEX advisor evidence)

**Q · What is a starting step?**
A · The first tread of a staircase — the tread that meets the finished floor at the bottom of the flight. It is treated as a distinct design element rather than a generic tread, often projecting forward with a rounded, half-round, D-shape or square-platform profile.

**Q · Does every staircase need a starting step?**
A · No. A staircase can begin with the first tread flush at the floor with no projection. That is a valid design choice — common in narrow hallways, boxed-in staircases and minimalist modern designs.

**Q · Should the starting step have a rounded profile on both sides, or just one?**
A · Construction determines what is **physically possible**; the designer then chooses within that:
- **Two-sided cut-string staircase** (open both sides) → CAN be rounded on both sides. Often is, for visual symmetry. May equally be square platforms, or a mix, or no projection at all — that is an aesthetic decision, not a construction requirement.
- **Wall-fixed staircase** (one side against the wall) → CANNOT wrap a rounded projection into the wall. The free side CAN be rounded (or square, or nothing) — again an aesthetic decision within what construction permits.

**Q · Does a wall-fixed staircase always have a rounded starting step on one side?**
A · No — it CAN have one on the free side; it might equally have a square platform, a standard tread, or no projection at all. The wall only prevents outward wrap on the wall side; it does not require a specific choice on the free side. Manufacturer detail and bespoke design still vary.

**Q · What are the main starting-step profiles?**
A · Bullnose (rounded front · straight sides) · half-round (rounded front + one end) · D-shape / oval (elongated wide bullnose · flat outer curve) · square platform (flat rectangular) · double-stacked (two of any of the above) · combined (square + bullnose above) · none (flush first tread).

**Q · Where does the bottom newel post sit on a starting step?**
A · At the free / open corner of the widened profile. On a bullnose/D-shape it sits within the curved profile with the timber wrapping around its base. On a square platform it sits at the corner. Wall-fixed staircases have one bottom newel (on the free side); two-sided cut-string have two (one at each free end).

**Q · Can the starting step have a different finish from the rest of the flight?**
A · Yes — commonly. Carpeted staircases often leave the bullnose starting step as exposed timber even where the rest of the flight is carpeted. Two-tone starting steps (natural timber tread + painted riser) are also very common.

**Q · What is a volute or scroll on a starting step?**
A · The volute is a curled scroll at the end of the handrail where it meets the bottom newel — often forming a swirl over a bullnose starting step. The scroll motif is echoed in cut-string decorative brackets applied along the string. Volute + bullnose starting step + scroll brackets belong to a single traditional design language.

**Q · Do square platform starting steps work with all balustrade styles?**
A · Best with modern / minimalist balustrades — square spindles, glass panels, matt-black metal rods, brushed stainless. A square platform can look wrong under a classical turned-spindle balustrade where a bullnose would be expected. Match the starting step to the balustrade language.

**Q · Can I add LED lighting to my starting step?**
A · Yes — commonly. Options include LED cove wash spilling from beneath the bullnose profile, LED-inset spots in the riser face, LED strip along the bullnose edge, and LED under-nosing on each tread. Wider starting steps make LED integration easier.

**Q · Are two-stacked starting steps a valid design?**
A · Yes. Two-stacked bullnoses (traditional grand entrance) and two-stacked square platforms (contemporary grand entrance) are both established designs. Requires more floor space than a single starting step. Common on wider domestic and hotel-scale staircases.

**Q · What if there is not enough floor space for a projecting starting step?**
A · Use a flush first tread (no projection). Do not compromise floor space or walking path. The staircase still functions correctly without a projecting starting step.

**Q · Can starting steps be added to an existing staircase during refurbishment?**
A · Sometimes — depending on the construction and floor material. A bullnose starting step can be retrofitted where floor space allows and the existing string can accept the profile. Full replacement is usually simpler than adding a starting step where none existed.

**Q · What material is used for the starting step?**
A · Normally matches the flight material. Common finishes observed: white painted · natural light oak · pale maple/beech · warm cherry · red mahogany · dark walnut · rustic dark oak · pale unfinished beech-like. Two-tone (painted riser + timber tread) is very common.

**Q · Should I have a starting step with a wall on both sides (i.e. staircase between two walls)?**
A · Normally no — a projecting rounded starting step needs at least one free side to project into. A staircase enclosed by walls on both sides normally starts flush.

**Q · Are cut-string scroll brackets always present with a bullnose starting step?**
A · No — scroll brackets are a decorative traditional detail associated with cut-string staircases. They accompany bullnose starting steps in traditional/period designs but are omitted on modern cut-string staircases where minimalism is preferred. The bullnose can stand on its own without brackets.
