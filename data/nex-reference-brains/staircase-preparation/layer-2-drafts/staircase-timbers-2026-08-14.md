# Staircase Joinery Timbers

Batch 9 evidence supplied five canonical staircase joinery timbers as physical stock samples showing the newel-post blank, handrail blank, string blank and stair-tread stock in the same species. These are the everyday choices a staircase manufacturer works with — not an exhaustive list, but the practical shortlist for most UK domestic staircases.

---

## 1 · The five canonical species (batch 9 evidence)

### 1.1 · Cherry / Mahogany (rich red-brown)
Warm reddish-brown timber that darkens with age and sunlight. Fine even grain, minimal figure. Reads formal / traditional / period. Historically used on grand and Federal / Georgian staircases. **Cost: high.** Batch 9 evidence: img-01.

### 1.2 · Maple (pale cream)
Very pale creamy-white timber with a subtle uniform grain and no strong figure. Reads clean / contemporary / Scandinavian-modern. Takes stain but often left natural to preserve the pale appearance. **Cost: medium-high.** Batch 9 evidence: img-02.

### 1.3 · Beech / Ash (warm cream with tighter grain)
Warmer than maple with slightly more visible grain figure. Reads modern-classical crossover. Common on modern staircases where a warm-but-not-yellow timber is wanted. **Cost: medium.** Batch 9 evidence: img-03.

### 1.4 · Pine / Redwood (pale yellow-cream with knots)
Pale timber with clear pine grain lines and small knots. Reads cottage / farmhouse / rustic / affordable-domestic. The everyday staircase timber for painted or stained finishes. **Cost: low.** Batch 9 evidence: img-04.

### 1.5 · Oak (light golden brown with medullary rays)
Golden-brown timber with distinctive figure (medullary rays, growth-ring contrast, tiger stripes on quartered stock). Reads traditional English / country / modern-British depending on treatment. The most popular UK staircase timber. **Cost: medium-high.** Batch 9 evidence: img-05.

## 2 · What each timber section represents

Each batch 9 timber sample shows four cross-sections of the same species:

- **Newel-post blank** — the tallest chunky square section behind the group
- **Handrail blank** — the wider-than-tall rectangular section (before profile is machined)
- **String blank** — the tall wide section that becomes the string/stringer running alongside the flight
- **Tread stock** — the wide flat section that becomes tread material

This visual convention communicates: *"here are the components a staircase in this timber will be built from."*

---

## 3 · Extended timber families (not in batch 9 but common in NEX brain)

Beyond the five canonical species, staircases also use:

- **Walnut** — dark chocolate-brown with dramatic figure. Formal / architect-modern luxury.
- **Sapele / African mahogany** — reddish-brown mahogany substitute. Georgian revival.
- **Iroko** — golden-brown African hardwood; hard-wearing exterior/heavy-duty.
- **Elm** — golden-brown with wild figure. Bespoke / cottage.
- **Poplar / Tulipwood** — very pale, straight-grained, primarily used for **paint-grade** newels and spindles.
- **MDF (paint-grade)** — engineered board for painted staircases where solid timber cost or movement is a concern.
- **Reclaimed pine / oak** — character timber for cottage and rustic-industrial designs.
- **Ash (thermally modified · "brown ash")** — heat-treated for stability and darker tone.
- **Shou Sugi Ban charred pine** — Japanese-inspired flame-scorched pine (seen in batch 7 as an exotic finish).

## 4 · Choosing a timber (design considerations)

- **Style match** — pine and painted MDF read cottage; oak reads English traditional; walnut/mahogany read formal; maple reads Scandinavian-modern; ash/beech read modern-neutral.
- **Colour intent** — natural, stained, or painted. Pine and MDF are paint-grade; oak stains beautifully; mahogany is normally left natural to show the red tone.
- **Hardness / wear** — oak, walnut, cherry, mahogany all wear well as tread material. Pine dents more easily but takes carpet runners well.
- **Cost tier** — pine (low) < ash/beech (medium) < oak/maple (medium-high) < walnut/cherry/mahogany (high) < reclaimed / bespoke (variable).
- **Grain match with other joinery** — flooring, doors, kitchen — the staircase timber is normally coordinated with the surrounding joinery.
- **Painted vs natural** — if the intent is fully painted, pine or MDF is normally used; there is no cost benefit paying for oak that will be covered.
- **Two-tone options** — light oak treads + white painted risers is a canonical modern-British look; dark walnut treads + white spindles + dark walnut handrail is a canonical Federal look.

---

## 5 · Grain direction

Tread stock is normally supplied with the grain running **along the tread length** (front-to-back is across the grain). This is the standard for stair treads and is how batch 9 tread samples are shown.

Handrail blanks are supplied with grain running along the length of the handrail (the direction the hand grips).

Newel posts have grain running vertically along the post.

Any tread with grain running the wrong direction (across the tread rather than along it) is either a specific bespoke choice or a manufacturing error.

## 6 · Timber Q&A (NEX advisor evidence)

**Q · What are the most common staircase joinery timbers in the UK?**
A · Pine (most common, budget-friendly, often painted or stained), oak (most popular hardwood, English traditional and modern-British), beech/ash (modern-neutral), maple (Scandinavian-modern, pale), cherry/mahogany (formal/traditional, red-brown). Walnut is also common on higher-end and architect-modern staircases.

**Q · Should I choose pine or oak?**
A · Depends on intent. Pine is lower cost and takes paint or stain well — good for painted staircases, cottage designs, or budget-conscious projects. Oak is higher cost but reads as hardwood, wears well, and works well natural or lightly finished. If the staircase will be visible timber (not painted), oak is normally preferred.

**Q · Which timber wears best as tread material?**
A · Oak, walnut, cherry, mahogany all wear well. Pine dents more easily but is often used under carpet runners where the tread face is protected. Softer timbers (poplar, tulipwood, MDF) are not normally used as bare treads — they are paint-grade for spindles/newels only.

**Q · Can I mix timbers on one staircase?**
A · Yes — common design choices include: dark walnut handrail + white painted spindles + light oak treads · oak treads + painted MDF risers · oak framework + painted spindles. The design language stays coherent even where different timbers are used for different components.

**Q · Does the newel post use the same timber as the treads?**
A · Normally yes for a coherent design. Exceptions include: white-painted newels with natural oak handrail cap (bi-colour), or matt-black metal / stainless-clad newels combined with timber treads (industrial-luxury contrast).

**Q · What is paint-grade timber?**
A · Timber (poplar, tulipwood, or MDF) used specifically for painted joinery — spindles, newels, risers — where the natural grain will be covered. Not normally used as bare tread material.

**Q · What is the reddish-brown timber I see on grand staircases?**
A · Cherry, mahogany, sapele, or dark-stained walnut. All read as formal / traditional / Federal / Georgian. Genuine mahogany is now rarely used due to restrictions; sapele is a common substitute; cherry darkens naturally with age.

**Q · Are reclaimed timbers used for staircases?**
A · Yes — reclaimed pine and reclaimed oak are common for cottage / rustic / industrial designs. Character features (knots, nail holes, saw marks) become part of the design intent.

**Q · What is Shou Sugi Ban?**
A · Japanese-inspired flame-scorched pine — the timber is deliberately charred to produce a very dark, textured, blackened surface. Rare in UK staircases but appears in high-end modern / rustic-luxury designs (batch 7 evidence). The scorching also improves rot and pest resistance.

**Q · How do I choose the right timber for my staircase?**
A · Match the timber to the overall style (traditional / modern / cottage / grand), coordinate with surrounding joinery (flooring / doors / kitchen), consider whether the surface will be painted (use paint-grade) or bare (use the correct hardwood for the design intent), and consider budget tier. Ask the customer what timbers appear elsewhere in the room before recommending — the staircase normally reads as part of the room, not a separate feature.
