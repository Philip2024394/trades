# Landing Railings — Continuity, Openstring Exception, Top-Newel Transition, Corner Fixings, Baluster Heights and Spacing

This document is the canonical NEX knowledge on how a landing railing is designed, connected, fixed and dimensioned. It expands on the earlier `landing-railings-knowledge-2026-08-14.md` with additional locked principles supplied by Philip.

The staircase and its landing must be designed **together**, as a single system, not as two separate railings. Continuity of design is the key architectural principle — supported by specific construction rules that follow.

---

## 1 · Continuity: the landing railing is a continuation of the staircase

The landing railing should normally be designed as a continuation of the staircase balustrade, not as a separate or unrelated railing system.

The handrail, balusters, newel posts, materials, colours and overall design should flow naturally from the staircase onto the landing. This creates a consistent appearance and makes the staircase feel like one complete architectural feature.

### 1.1 · The same railing design

In most staircase designs, the landing uses the same railing system as the staircase.

For example, if the staircase has:

- Oak handrail
- Oak newel posts
- Square timber balusters
- Painted base rail

then the landing would normally continue with the same:

- Oak handrail
- Newel design
- Square balusters
- Painted base rail

The landing railing **should not** suddenly change to a different spindle or handrail style unless there is a deliberate architectural reason for doing so.

### 1.2 · Continuity is the key principle

The most important design principle is continuity.

The landing should look as though the staircase railing naturally continues onto the upper floor. The handrail should connect logically, the baluster proportions should remain consistent, and the materials and finishes should match.

Good landing design is not about creating a completely different railing. It is about taking the same staircase railing design and adapting it intelligently to the landing, the staircase construction and the architecture of the house.

## 2 · Base-rail construction relationship — the three-way distinction (LOCKED)

Base rails are governed by **staircase construction type**, not by aesthetics. This is a **three-way** construction relationship that must never be reduced to a single "openstring exception" — the landing rule is independent of the stair-flight rule.

### 2.1 · The three cases

- **Closed-string / stringed staircase (housed string):**
  The staircase itself **normally has a base rail** supporting the balusters. The string is a closed housing that runs alongside the treads and typically carries a base rail into which the balusters are fixed.

- **Cut-string / open-string staircase (also called cut stringer):**
  The staircase itself **may not require a conventional base rail** because the balusters can be fixed **directly to the stair construction / stringer** (typically into the treads themselves at the exposed side, or via an approved engineered fixing arrangement). The exposed treads are the whole point of a cut-string design — a sloping base rail would obscure them.

- **Landing (all types):**
  The landing balustrade **can still require a base rail even when the staircase itself is cut-string / open-string.** The landing is a horizontal floor surface; the balusters typically need a horizontal base rail to fix into. So a cut-string staircase can correctly present:
  - **no sloping base rail on the stair flight** + **a horizontal base rail on the landing** — simultaneously.

### 2.2 · Why this is not a design inconsistency

The staircase and landing are two **different construction contexts**. The stair flight is inclined and, on a cut-string design, has an exposed stringer/tread face into which balusters can fix directly. The landing is horizontal and has no such stringer to fix into — a base rail (or another approved horizontal fixing arrangement) becomes the natural baluster support.

The visual result — no sloping base rail on the flight, a clearly defined horizontal base rail on the landing — is a **correct, coherent construction outcome**, not a design contradiction.

### 2.3 · Why this must not get lost among visual tags

The three-way relationship is a **construction fact**, not a style choice. It affects:

- What NEX quotes/estimates for a refacing job (cut-string flight has fewer base-rail linear metres; landing still needs its share)
- What a trade orders as materials (base rail lengths differ by construction type)
- What a customer expects to see on the finished staircase (they should not be surprised that the landing has a base rail the flight does not)
- How the balustrade is engineered (baluster fixings differ between stringer-mounted and base-rail-mounted)

Never let this construction relationship be flattened into "the openstring exception" — that phrase obscures the fact that **closed-string, cut-string and landing each have their own base-rail rule**.

## 3 · How the staircase handrail connects to the landing railing (top newel)

The **top newel post** is the important connection point between the staircase handrail and the landing railing. It is where the inclined handrail from the staircase terminates and the landing balustrade begins.

A well-designed staircase should make this transition appear natural, strong and intentional.

### 3.1 · Stair handrail runs to the top newel

The handrail running alongside the staircase follows the pitch of the stairs and terminates at the top newel post.

The newel provides the main support point for the end of the stair handrail. The handrail is securely connected to the newel rather than simply stopping unsupported at the top of the staircase.

Arrangement:

```
staircase handrail → top newel post → landing railing
```

### 3.2 · Landing railing connects to the other face of the newel

Where the landing continues beyond the top of the staircase, the landing handrail can connect to the **other face of the same top newel post**.

This creates a very clean junction. The newel effectively becomes the connection point between two different directions:

```
inclined handrail
      ↓
top newel post
      ↓
horizontal landing handrail
```

The stair handrail and landing handrail meet at the same newel while remaining structurally and visually distinct.

### 3.3 · What the top newel is really doing

The top newel does more than provide a decorative finish. It can provide support for:

- The end of the staircase handrail
- The beginning or termination of the landing handrail
- The landing balustrade
- The balusters
- The base rail
- The railing system at a change of direction

This makes it a key structural and visual element within the staircase.

### 3.4 · Straight-staircase configuration

On a straight staircase where the upper landing railing runs away from the staircase:

```
stair flight → inclined handrail → TOP NEWEL ← landing handrail → landing balustrade
```

The newel acts as a strong dividing and connecting element between the two directions.

## 4 · Avoiding long unsupported handrail runs — intermediate newels

Landing design should consider the length of the handrail between substantial support points.

Very long runs can create additional leverage and movement when someone pushes against or leans on the railing.

### 4.1 · Handrails experience more than vertical load

A person does not only apply force vertically. A railing can also experience horizontal or lateral loading when somebody:

- Leans against it
- Pushes against it
- Grabs it suddenly
- Uses it for support
- Falls against it

A long uninterrupted railing run can therefore require greater consideration of structural support.

### 4.2 · Introduce intermediate newels on long landings

Instead of:

```
Newel ─────────────── very long handrail ─────────────── Newel
```

use:

```
Newel ───── handrail ───── Newel ───── handrail ───── Newel
```

This makes the railing more rigid and creates a more balanced visual rhythm.

The exact spacing and structural requirements should be determined by the staircase designer/installer in accordance with the applicable UK Building Regulations and loading requirements — not a universal spacing rule.

### 4.3 · Long runs also affect appearance

A very long handrail can visually dominate a landing and may look less refined if it has excessive movement or deflection.

A well-positioned newel can:

- Strengthen the railing
- Reduce movement
- Define the landing
- Create better proportions
- Break up a long run
- Provide a natural termination point

The newel becomes both a structural support and an architectural feature.

## 5 · Corner newels need particular attention

Corner newels are especially important because they can support railing sections running in two directions.

```
landing railing → CORNER NEWEL ← landing railing
```

The newel therefore becomes a major load-transfer point.

### 5.1 · A properly designed corner connection provides

- Strong fixing to the structure
- Adequate rigidity
- Secure handrail connections
- Correct alignment
- Proper support for the balustrade
- Minimal movement under normal loading

The finished newel should feel solid rather than move independently from the floor.

### 5.2 · Corner newels must be fixed into structural material

The fixing method depends on the staircase construction and substrate. It may involve appropriate mechanical fixings, bolts, concealed fixing systems or other engineered connections.

The important principle is that the newel **should not simply be attached to a weak surface finish**.

Fixing only into any of the following may **not** provide the necessary structural support:

- Floorboards
- Thin decorative panels
- Plasterboard
- Non-structural trim

The fixing needs to transfer the railing loads into the underlying structure.

## 6 · Correct railing height

The finished height of the landing handrail and guarding must be established during design and checked against the current applicable UK Building Regulations and relevant standards for the particular staircase and situation.

Height should be measured from the appropriate finished floor or stair reference level to the top of the handrail or guarding, as applicable.

This is particularly important when dealing with:

- New staircases
- Refurbishments
- Changes in floor finishes
- Raised platforms
- Open landings
- Stairwell openings
- Changes in floor level

A visually attractive railing is not enough if its finished dimensions are incorrect.

## 7 · Baluster heights (supplied lengths before trimming)

Landing balusters are generally designed as part of the same balustrade system as the staircase, so the style, material and finish should normally match the staircase balusters. **The landing baluster height can be different** where the design or required guarding height calls for it.

### 7.1 · Common supplied-length starting point

A common practical arrangement is:

- **Staircase balusters:** supplied at approximately **900 mm** before trimming.
- **Landing balusters:** supplied at approximately **1100 mm** before trimming.

Both are cut down to the required finished height during installation.

The final height should always be established from the actual staircase and landing requirements rather than simply using the supplied baluster length. This is a **supply/manufacturing convention** — not a compliance dimension.

### 7.2 · When landing balusters use a different height/arrangement

The landing may call for a different height or arrangement where:

- A higher guarding requirement applies
- The landing has a wider opening
- A different newel arrangement is used
- A half-landing or quarter-landing configuration is used
- A feature balustrade is required
- A transition between staircase and landing railing needs to be resolved

The landing should still look like a continuation of the staircase, rather than appearing to be a completely separate railing system.

---

## 8 · Baluster spacing and positioning

The spacing between balusters is a critical safety detail. Openings should be designed so a person, particularly a child, cannot pass through them.

The applicable UK requirements should be checked for the specific installation rather than relying on a generic rule of thumb.

### 8.1 · Consistent spacing across the whole balustrade

Spacing should be considered consistently around:

- Stair flights
- Landings
- Corners
- Newel posts
- Changes in direction
- Start and end positions

The first and last baluster positions may need adjustment to maintain a visually balanced and compliant arrangement.

### 8.2 · Two balusters per tread (typical staircase starting point)

For many traditional staircase designs, a practical starting arrangement is **two balusters per tread**. This gives the staircase a balanced appearance and helps maintain consistent spacing.

### 8.3 · One baluster per tread where width is restricted

At the **top or bottom** of a staircase where the available tread width becomes restricted by the newel post, string or other construction details, **one baluster** may be more appropriate.

The exact arrangement should be checked against the actual balustrade design and required maximum gaps.

### 8.4 · Landing baluster rhythm

A landing railing should normally continue the visual rhythm established on the staircase. If the staircase has evenly spaced square balusters, the landing should maintain a similar rhythm.

However, the landing dimensions rarely divide perfectly into the same spacing used on the stair flight. The installer therefore needs to **carefully calculate the layout** rather than simply copying a measurement from the staircase.

The objective is:

```
safe spacing + consistent appearance + correct positioning
```

### 8.5 · Balusters at corners

Corners require additional attention. A baluster should not simply be positioned wherever it happens to fit. The relationship between the balusters, corner newel and handrail needs to be considered together.

A properly planned corner can maintain a clean visual transition while ensuring the guarding remains effective.

## 9 · Newel posts and baluster support (the complete landing system)

The newel posts should be considered the primary support points, while the balusters provide the infill between the base rail and handrail.

A typical landing arrangement is:

```
Newel → balusters → handrail → corner newel → balusters → newel
```

The base rail provides the lower connection for the balusters where applicable. On an openstring staircase, the stair flight may have no conventional sloping base rail, while the landing section still uses a horizontal base rail.

### 9.1 · The complete landing system

```
Strong newel fixing
        ↓
Secure handrail connection
        ↓
Correct railing height
        ↓
Correct baluster spacing
        ↓
Secure baluster and base-rail fixing
        ↓
Properly supported corners and changes of direction
```

Every component contributes to the safety and stability of the finished railing.

### 9.2 · Avoiding weak points

A landing railing can look perfectly strong while having a weak connection underneath. Potential weak points:

- Poorly fixed newels
- Loose handrail joints
- Inadequate corner connections
- Weak base-rail fixings
- Incorrect baluster connections
- Fixing into non-structural material
- Excessive unsupported railing length

These should be addressed during design and installation rather than after the railing has been completed.

---

## 10 · Handrail transitions and continuity

The handrail is one of the most important visual elements.

Where possible, the handrail should appear to flow naturally from the staircase onto the landing.

Depending on the staircase arrangement, this can involve:

- A horizontal transition
- A newel post
- A quarter-turn
- A corner connection
- A change of direction
- A landing newel
- A continuous handrail section

The connection should feel deliberate and professionally resolved. A poorly planned transition can make the staircase and landing appear like two unrelated railing systems.

---

## 11 · Newel-post positioning on the landing

Landing newels may be positioned at:

- The end of the staircase
- The beginning of a landing railing
- A change in direction
- A corner
- The end of an open landing
- Around a stairwell opening

The size and style of the newel should normally correspond with the staircase newels. A staircase with large square oak newels would generally continue that language onto the landing rather than suddenly introducing small decorative posts.

---

## 12 · The base rail

The landing base rail has several purposes:

- Provides a fixing and finishing point for the balusters
- Creates a clear visual boundary between the floor and balustrade
- Helps maintain consistent baluster positioning across the landing

It may be:

- Square
- Moulded
- Traditional
- Contemporary
- Timber
- Painted
- Stained
- Designed to match the staircase handrail

With an openstring staircase, this horizontal base rail can become more visually noticeable because it contrasts with the open construction of the stair flight.

## 13 · Landing design depends on the house

Although the railing itself should normally match the staircase, the way it is **arranged** on the landing can vary considerably, based on:

- Staircase configuration
- Position of the staircase
- Landing size
- Open or enclosed landing
- Stairwell opening
- Room layout
- Doors and windows
- Ceiling height
- Interior architecture
- Flooring
- Existing joinery
- Traditional or contemporary house design

The same staircase railing components can produce very different landing arrangements.

### 13.1 · Design opportunities

A large open landing could incorporate:

```
continuous handrail + several newels + repeated balusters
```

while a smaller landing might use:

```
newel post + short handrail + compact balustrade section
```

The components remain consistent; the arrangement changes according to the architecture.

### 13.2 · Working around doors

A landing railing may need to stop, turn or change position to accommodate a doorway. The designer needs to consider:

- Door swing
- Door opening width
- Clear access
- Newel position
- Handrail termination
- Baluster arrangement
- Available wall space

The railing should never simply be positioned without considering how people move through the landing.

### 13.3 · Working around windows

Windows can also influence landing design. A railing may need to be positioned below or around a window while maintaining its protective function. In some houses, the landing railing can frame the window and become an architectural feature.

The same staircase railing design can therefore look completely different depending on the surrounding architecture.

### 13.4 · Stairwell openings

A landing surrounding an open stairwell often provides a large area for balustrade. The railing protects the edge of the opening while visually defining the staircase void.

This can create attractive opportunities for:

- Long glass panels
- Repeated timber spindles
- Decorative metalwork
- Feature newel posts
- Continuous handrails

The staircase and landing can then become one strong architectural feature within the house.

## 14 · Matching the house design

The landing railing should also work with the surrounding house.

### 14.1 · Traditional property

- Turned timber spindles
- Moulded handrails
- Decorative newel posts
- Oak or stained timber
- Traditional stair brackets

### 14.2 · Modern property

- Square balusters
- Glass panels
- Stainless steel
- Minimal newel posts
- Simple square handrails
- Clean, uninterrupted lines

A contemporary staircase can use the same railing language throughout the stair flight and landing while adapting its layout and detailing to the space.

## 15 · Continuity across specific systems

### 15.1 · Glass balustrades

A staircase might have glass panels following the incline of the stairs. At the top, the glass can transition into horizontal panels around the landing.

The handrail remains consistent while the glass changes orientation:

```
inclined staircase panels → horizontal landing panels
```

The system is continuous even though the geometry changes.

### 15.2 · Metal balustrades

A staircase using stainless-steel or black metal balusters can continue around the landing with the same:

- Tube diameter
- Finish
- Spacing
- Handrail
- Fixing style
- Newel design

The landing becomes a continuation of the staircase rather than a separate architectural element.

### 15.3 · Traditional staircases

A design might use:

```
oak handrail + turned spindles + decorative newels + moulded base rail
```

The same components can continue around the landing. Additional newels or longer sections of balustrade may be needed, but detailing remains consistent.

Decorative features such as stair brackets, carved newel caps or moulded rails can also continue onto the landing.

### 15.4 · Modern staircases

Modern staircases often use fewer components and cleaner lines:

```
square handrail + square newels + square balusters
```

or:

```
timber handrail + glass panels + minimal posts
```

The landing should preserve that simplicity. A modern staircase can lose its visual quality if the stair flight is minimalist but the landing suddenly introduces heavy decorative components.

---

## 16 · Refurbishment opportunities

Landing railings are important when refacing or renovating an existing staircase.

A staircase may have an old handrail and balusters that no longer suit the house. Instead of replacing the entire staircase structure, the visible components can sometimes be upgraded.

Possible changes include:

- New handrail
- New landing handrail
- New balusters
- New base rail
- New newel caps
- New newel posts
- Glass panels
- Metal feature components
- New timber finishes

The existing staircase structure can remain while the visible staircase and landing are given a completely new appearance.

---

## 17 · Mixing materials

Modern staircase design allows different materials to be combined. For example:

```
Oak handrail + painted base rail + stainless-steel balusters
```

or:

```
Oak handrail + oak newels + glass panels + stainless-steel fittings
```

The landing should normally continue the same material combination. Changing materials at the landing should be **intentional** and supported by the overall interior design.

---

## 18 · Proportion is important

Even when the same staircase railing is used, the proportions may need to be considered carefully on the landing.

A large staircase may use substantial newel posts and a wide handrail. Using the same components on a very small landing could make the area feel heavy.

The designer can retain the same design language while selecting appropriate positioning and spacing for the available area. This is the difference between simply copying a staircase railing and properly designing a complete staircase system.

---

## 19 · Spare balusters (manufacturer/installer practice)

For staircase manufacturers, joiners and installers, it is sensible to keep **spare balusters in stock**.

Reasons:

- Staircase designs can change over time
- Individual balusters can become damaged during installation
- Future repairs may require matching components

Keeping several spare balusters from the original design can save considerable time later, particularly with bespoke or discontinued designs.

---

## 20 · A single design language

The strongest staircase designs usually have a clear design language. The same:

- Timber species
- Paint colour
- Metal finish
- Handrail profile
- Baluster style
- Newel design
- Base rail profile

can be carried from the staircase onto the landing.

**The geometry changes, but the design remains recognisable.**

## 21 · The landing as a design opportunity

The landing should not be considered simply as the place where the staircase ends. It is an opportunity to continue and reinforce the staircase design throughout the upper floor.

A carefully designed landing railing can:

- Frame the stairwell
- Define the upper-floor space
- Provide visual continuity
- Highlight architectural features
- Complement flooring and doors
- Work with windows and natural light
- Introduce decorative joinery
- Create a strong focal point

---

## 22 · Design and safety must work together

Good staircase joinery combines appearance with structural thinking.

The landing railing should match the staircase aesthetically, but it must also be correctly proportioned, securely fixed and designed for the forces it may experience during everyday use.

The strongest designs are those where the structural requirements are incorporated into the architecture rather than hidden as an afterthought.

The result should be a landing railing that looks clean and continuous with the staircase while providing:

- Secure support
- Appropriate guarding
- Correct dimensions
- Durable construction

---

## 23 · Important principle (summary)

The landing railing should normally look like it belongs to the staircase.

The landing does not need to be identical in arrangement, because the architecture and dimensions are different. Instead, it should use the same components and design language wherever practical.

The main exception is the construction detail of an openstring staircase, where the stair flight may not require a sloping base rail while the horizontal landing balustrade normally does.

This combination allows the staircase to retain its open architectural appearance while providing a properly finished and continuous railing around the landing.

Ultimately, the best landing railing is one that balances:

```
continuity + safety + proportion + construction + architectural character of the house
```

## 24 · Advisor Q&A (NEX chat evidence)

**Q · Should the landing railing use the same design as the staircase?**
A · Yes. Handrail, balusters, newel posts, materials, colours and overall design should normally continue from the staircase onto the landing. Changing style at the landing should be a deliberate architectural choice, not a default.

**Q · Does the staircase need a base rail?**
A · It depends on construction type — this is a three-way relationship, not a single rule:
  - **Closed-string / stringed staircase:** yes — normally has a base rail supporting the balusters.
  - **Cut-string / open-string staircase:** may not — balusters can fix directly to the stair construction / stringer or via another approved fixing arrangement.
  - **Landing (either type):** can still require a base rail, even when the staircase itself is cut-string. The landing is a horizontal surface with no stringer to fix into.

**Q · So a cut-string staircase can have a horizontal landing base rail but no sloping stair-flight base rail?**
A · Yes — that is a correct, coherent construction outcome. The stair flight and the landing are two different construction contexts and each has its own base-rail rule. Never flatten this into a single "openstring exception."

**Q · How does the staircase handrail connect to the landing railing?**
A · Through the top newel post. The stair handrail terminates at one face of the top newel; the landing handrail connects to another face of the same newel. The newel acts as a strong dividing and connecting element between the two directions.

**Q · Can the landing handrail run for a long distance without any intermediate support?**
A · It is not recommended. Long unsupported runs allow excessive movement under lateral load (leaning, pushing, grabbing, falling against). Introduce intermediate newel posts on long landings so the railing is broken up: `Newel → handrail → Newel → handrail → Newel`. The final spacing should be determined by the designer/installer under UK Building Regulations, not a universal rule.

**Q · How should a corner newel be fixed?**
A · Into structural material — never into floorboards alone, thin decorative panels, plasterboard or non-structural trim. Corner newels receive load from two directions, so the fixing must transfer that load into the underlying structure via appropriate mechanical fixings, bolts or engineered connections.

**Q · What starting height are staircase and landing balusters supplied at?**
A · A common practical starting arrangement is: staircase balusters ~900 mm supplied length; landing balusters ~1100 mm supplied length. Both are trimmed down to the required finished height during installation. This is a supply convention — the final finished height must be established from the actual staircase requirements and UK Building Regulations, not from the supplied length.

**Q · Can the landing baluster height differ from the staircase baluster height?**
A · Yes. Landing balusters can be a different height where required by guarding rules, landing configuration, half-landings, feature balustrades or transitions. The design and finish should still visually continue from the staircase.

**Q · How many balusters per tread is typical?**
A · Two balusters per tread is a practical starting arrangement for many traditional staircase designs — it gives a balanced appearance and consistent spacing. At the top or bottom of the staircase where tread width is restricted by the newel post, string or other construction details, one baluster may be more appropriate. Always check against the actual balustrade design and required maximum openings.

**Q · What about the baluster spacing on the landing?**
A · Continue the visual rhythm from the staircase, but calculate the layout — landing dimensions rarely divide perfectly into the stair-flight spacing. The objective is: safe spacing + consistent appearance + correct positioning. The applicable UK maximum-opening rule should be checked, not a generic rule of thumb.

**Q · How should corner balusters be arranged?**
A · Corners need planning. Balusters, corner newel and handrail should be considered together, not fitted where each happens to land. A properly planned corner keeps a clean visual transition while keeping the guarding effective.

**Q · What are the weak points to avoid on a landing railing?**
A · Poorly fixed newels · loose handrail joints · inadequate corner connections · weak base-rail fixings · incorrect baluster connections · fixing into non-structural material · excessive unsupported railing length. Address these during design and installation, not after.

**Q · Should manufacturers keep spare balusters?**
A · Yes. Designs change over time, balusters can be damaged during installation, and matching future repairs to bespoke or discontinued designs is difficult. Keeping several spares from the original design saves time later.

**Q · Does the landing railing need to be identical in arrangement to the staircase?**
A · No. It should use the same components and design language wherever practical, but the arrangement varies with the landing layout, doorways, windows, stairwell opening and house architecture. The design language is what continues — not the specific measurements.

**Q · How does glass railing continue from a staircase onto a landing?**
A · The glass follows the incline of the stairs, then transitions into horizontal panels around the landing. The handrail stays consistent; only the glass orientation changes. The system remains continuous even though the geometry changes.

**Q · What is the single most important principle?**
A · Continuity. The landing should look as though the staircase railing naturally continues onto the upper floor — same handrail profile, same baluster style, same newel design, same material and finish — with the top newel post carrying the handrail from staircase to landing.
