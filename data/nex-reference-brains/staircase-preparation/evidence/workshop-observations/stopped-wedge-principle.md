# The Stopped-Wedge Principle

Workshop knowledge that separates an experienced assembler from a beginner. Not in the published guidance — this is received wisdom from the workshop floor.

## Claim (Philip · 2026-07-29)

**A wedge reaching its stopping point does NOT guarantee that the tread or riser has fully seated against the routed housing shoulder.**

The wedge may have simply reached its friction limit before the component has completely closed.

## Mechanism

A wedge works by converting hammer force into sideways pressure. As it drives in, one of three things eventually happens:

1. **The tread closes fully against the housing shoulder.** ✓ Desired outcome.
2. **The wedge locks due to friction between wedge, tread, and housing.** Wedge stops moving, but the tread is still 0.5-1 mm away from the shoulder.
3. **The timber begins compressing more than the wedge advances.** Grain compression absorbs further hammer force without closing the gap.

Outcomes 2 and 3 look identical to outcome 1 from the assembler's perspective. The wedge stops. Instinct says *"it's tight"*. But the joint may not be closed.

## What happens if you keep hammering past a locked wedge

- **Split wedge** — repeated impact fatigues the taper section.
- **Crushed fibres** — housing shoulder or tread underside crushes locally.
- **Damaged housing** — the routed groove profile deforms permanently.
- **Bruised string** — impact marks on the visible face.
- **Failed joint over time** — the crushed fibres relax as the timber moves, leaving a gap.

**The objective is a fully seated joint, not the tightest possible wedge.** Professional assembly relies on confirming the joint is closed — visually and physically — not on how hard the wedge resists.

## Verification method

Professional practice per Philip:

1. **Visual check** — tread shoulder against housing, no visible line of daylight.
2. **Tread underside check** — riser fully seated (from underneath the assembly).
3. **Glue line closed** — squeeze-out at the shoulder is a positive sign, gap is not.
4. **Only then confirm the wedge** — the wedge is the last check, not the first.
