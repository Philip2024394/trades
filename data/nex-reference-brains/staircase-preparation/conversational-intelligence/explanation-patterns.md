# Explanation Patterns — "why" templates

NEX doesn't just state options — it explains **why** an option works or doesn't work, using the six-step reasoning hierarchy. This is what distinguishes an advisor from a database.

**Locked rules:**
- Every explanation flows through: Construction → Physical possibilities → Design options → Material → Finish → Preference → Manufacturer/bespoke
- Never reverse into "because X, therefore Y is required"
- Hedged language: "commonly" · "one reason" · "the usual thinking"
- Never introduce facts not in the Reference Brain

---

## Pattern EX-01 · Construction-blocks-projection explanation

**Use when:** explaining why an outward-wrapping starting step can't happen on the wall side of a wall-fixed staircase.

**Template:**
> "The reason is physical rather than aesthetic. [Feature X] projects outward from the flight — and outward wrap needs an open side to project into. On [construction type], [side] is against a wall, which blocks that projection. So on that side, the tread terminates flush against the wall skirting. On the [free/other] side, [feature X] is fully possible."

**Example filled in:**
> "The reason is physical rather than aesthetic. A rounded bullnose projects outward from the flight, and outward wrap needs an open side to project into. On a wall-fixed staircase, one side is against a wall, which blocks that projection. So on that side, the tread terminates flush against the wall skirting. On the free side, a full bullnose wrap is entirely possible."

**Source:** `starting-steps-knowledge-2026-08-14.md` §1

## Pattern EX-02 · Construction-context-for-base-rail

**Use when:** explaining the three-way base-rail rule (closed-string vs cut-string vs landing).

**Template:**
> "Base rails are about construction, not appearance. On [construction type A], [outcome A]. On [construction type B], [outcome B]. The landing is a horizontal surface with no stringer to fix balusters into, so [outcome C]. That's why [visible effect] — it's a construction outcome, not a design inconsistency."

**Example filled in:**
> "Base rails are about construction, not appearance. On a closed-string staircase (where the string covers the tread ends), there's normally a sloping base rail alongside the flight for the balusters to fix into. On a cut-string staircase (where the tread ends are exposed), the balusters can fix directly to the stair construction, so a sloping base rail may not be needed. The landing is a horizontal surface with no stringer to fix balusters into, so it normally uses a horizontal base rail regardless. That's why you can see a cut-string staircase with no sloping base rail on the flight but a horizontal base rail around the landing — it's a construction outcome, not a design inconsistency."

**Source:** `landing-railings-continuity-and-construction-2026-08-14.md` §2

## Pattern EX-03 · Design-language-match

**Use when:** explaining why a component should match the overall staircase style.

**Template:**
> "The reason [component X] normally matches [component Y] is that a staircase reads as one coordinated system rather than a set of separate parts. If [X] uses [style A] and [Y] uses [style B], the eye picks it up as a mismatch — even if each individual piece is well made. So the usual thinking is: [design principle]."

**Example filled in:**
> "The reason the starting step normally matches the rest of the balustrade style is that a staircase reads as one coordinated system rather than a set of separate parts. If the starting step is a heavy classical curtail with a volute and the rest of the flight has square modern spindles, the eye picks it up as a mismatch — even if each individual piece is well made. So the usual thinking is: pick the design language first (traditional or modern), then let all the components — starting step, spindles, newel, handrail, cap — belong to it."

**Source:** `starting-steps-types-carpet-and-design-2026-08-14.md` Part 5 §5.1

## Pattern EX-04 · Carpet-difficulty-for-curved-treads

**Use when:** explaining why a curved starting step is commonly left exposed on a carpeted staircase.

**Template:**
> "The practical reason is that [curved feature] and [flat carpet] don't sit together neatly. The carpet has to [difficulty]. The result can [visual outcome]. So a common design choice is [common alternative] — the [feature] stays visible as the feature of the entry, and the carpet begins on [where it begins]."

**Example filled in:**
> "The practical reason is that a curved bullnose and flat carpet don't sit together neatly. The carpet has to follow the curve, tension unevenly, and often requires specialist cutting to terminate cleanly. The result can hide the shape of the tread underneath. So a common design choice is to leave the bullnose in exposed timber and let it be the feature of the entry, with the carpet beginning on the second tread up."

**Source:** `starting-steps-types-carpet-and-design-2026-08-14.md` Part 3

## Pattern EX-05 · Continuity-principle

**Use when:** explaining why the landing railing should match the staircase balustrade.

**Template:**
> "The strongest staircase designs read as one continuous system from the bottom of the flight to the far end of the landing. [Handrail], [balusters], [newels], [material], [finish] — if they all belong to the same family, the whole staircase feels intentional. If the landing suddenly switches to a different style, it can look like two separate railings that happened to meet at the top."

**Example filled in:**
> "The strongest staircase designs read as one continuous system from the bottom of the flight to the far end of the landing. Handrail profile, baluster style, newel design, timber, and finish — if they all belong to the same family, the whole staircase feels intentional. If the landing suddenly switches to a different style, it can look like two separate railings that happened to meet at the top. So the usual recommendation is: same railing system, adapted where the geometry changes (corners, doorways, stairwell openings)."

**Source:** `landing-railings-continuity-and-construction-2026-08-14.md` §1

## Pattern EX-06 · Top-newel-transition

**Use when:** explaining how the handrail moves from stair to landing.

**Template:**
> "The top newel post is the anchor. The stair handrail runs up the flight and terminates on one face of the top newel. The landing handrail connects to another face of the same newel — usually perpendicular. That way one solid newel carries both handrails, and the transition looks intentional rather than two separate rails meeting at the top."

**Source:** `landing-railings-continuity-and-construction-2026-08-14.md` §3

## Pattern EX-07 · Long-unsupported-run

**Use when:** explaining why intermediate newels break up long landings.

**Template:**
> "A handrail experiences lateral load (someone leaning, pushing, grabbing) — not just weight. Over a long run without support, a handrail can move too much under that lateral load. Intermediate newels reduce the movement, keep the rail rigid, and also break up the visual line so a long landing feels less like one uninterrupted rail."

**Source:** `landing-railings-continuity-and-construction-2026-08-14.md` §4

## Pattern EX-08 · Corner-newel-structural-fixing

**Use when:** explaining why corner newels need proper substrate.

**Template:**
> "A corner newel takes load from two directions — one railing pulls one way, the other pulls another. That force has to transfer into structural material to keep the newel solid. Fixing only into floorboards, thin trim or plasterboard doesn't reach the structure, so the newel can move independently of the floor. The fixing has to bite into joists, beams, timber grounds, or masonry."

**Source:** `landing-railings-continuity-and-construction-2026-08-14.md` §5

## Pattern EX-09 · Baluster-supplied-length

**Use when:** explaining the 900 mm / 1100 mm supplied lengths.

**Template:**
> "Those are supply conventions from the manufacturer, not compliance dimensions. Staircase balusters typically come at around 900 mm, landing balusters at around 1100 mm, and both are trimmed to the actual finished height during installation. The finished height depends on the staircase, the guarding requirement, and current UK Building Regulations for the specific installation — never the supplied length alone."

**Source:** `landing-railings-continuity-and-construction-2026-08-14.md` §7

## Pattern EX-10 · Two-balusters-per-tread

**Use when:** explaining the typical baluster count.

**Template:**
> "Two balusters per tread is a common practical starting arrangement for many traditional staircase designs — it gives a balanced appearance and consistent spacing. Where the top or bottom of the flight has restricted tread width (because of the newel post, string, or other construction), one baluster per tread may be more appropriate. The actual spacing always has to comply with the current UK maximum-opening rule for the specific installation."

**Source:** `landing-railings-continuity-and-construction-2026-08-14.md` §8.2

## Pattern EX-11 · Component-family-cohesion

**Use when:** explaining why one component choice constrains others.

**Template:**
> "One choice usually pulls others with it. If you pick [component X in style A], the [related components] normally belong to the same style. That's why: [design reason]. Mixing families is a legitimate deliberate choice for a specific effect, but by default the family holds together across the whole staircase."

**Example filled in:**
> "One choice usually pulls others with it. If you pick a bullnose starting step with a turned newel post, the spindles normally follow — turned timber spindles rather than square modern ones, and often a moulded handrail with a volute termination. That's why: the eye reads all these as one design language. Mixing families (say, a bullnose starting step with matt black metal spindles) is a legitimate deliberate choice for a specific hybrid effect, but by default the family holds together across the whole staircase."

**Source:** `starting-steps-types-carpet-and-design-2026-08-14.md` Part 5 + `staircase-handrail-components-2026-08-14.md` §8

## Pattern EX-12 · Physical-possibility-vs-aesthetic-choice

**Use when:** explaining that construction bounds physical possibility but doesn't dictate aesthetics.

**Template:**
> "There's a useful distinction here: construction determines what's physically possible, but the designer chooses the aesthetic within what's possible. So [construction type] permits [possibilities]. It doesn't require any of them specifically — that's the aesthetic decision. And we can rule out [impossibility] because [physical reason]."

**Example filled in:**
> "There's a useful distinction here: construction determines what's physically possible, but the designer chooses the aesthetic within what's possible. So a wall-fixed staircase permits: a rounded bullnose on the free side, a square platform, an extended tread projecting forward, or no projection at all. It doesn't require any of them specifically — that's the aesthetic decision. And we can rule out a rounded bullnose wrapping into the wall, because a wall physically blocks the outward projection."

**Source:** `starting-steps-knowledge-2026-08-14.md` §1 + memory `feedback_nex_construction_design_relationship_framing.md`

## Meta-explanation rules

- **Explanation is what makes NEX feel like an advisor.** Without the "why", NEX is a database.
- **Never explain by saying "because that's the rule."** Always trace the reason (physics · construction · design language · Building Regulations).
- **Never overstate.** "Commonly" · "the usual thinking" · "the practical reason" · "one reason".
- **Never invent physics or construction facts.** All explanations trace to Reference Brain sources.
- **Explanations can be short.** A one-sentence "why" is often more useful than a paragraph. Match the length to what the customer asked.
- **The customer's question dictates depth.** Don't explain physics if they didn't ask why.
