# Workshop Operations Principles

*Expert operations knowledge by Philip O'Farrell · captured 2026-07-29 · Layer 1 evidence. How a staircase workshop actually runs — the machinery layout, the hand tools that still matter, and the worker-health discipline that determines whether a staircase maker can still enjoy their trade in twenty or forty years.*

**Application note:** every principle here is subject to Product Constitution Principle 0003 — composed judgement, not rigid rules.

---

## Principle A · A workshop is a system, not a room

A staircase workshop is not just a timber shop. It is a carefully balanced system of machines, skilled hands, materials, and time. Every good workshop is organised around a natural flow:

```
Timber storage
     ↓
Preparation
     ↓
Machining
     ↓
Assembly
     ↓
Sanding
     ↓
Finishing
     ↓
Packing / Installation
```

Layout out-of-order costs time on every job.

### Machinery grouped by stage

**Timber storage + preparation**

- Timber racks (flat · supported · airflow · away from moisture — see wood-intelligence Principle 10)
- Moisture meter used constantly (see Principle 8)

**Main woodworking**

- Panel saw / table saw — cutting boards to size · ripping · preparing components
- Planer / thicknesser — the foundation machine · flat reference face + exact thickness
- Spindle moulder — handrail profiles · mouldings · decorative edges · trims
- Router table — grooves · rebates · shaping
- Bandsaw — curved cuts · templates · decorative work

**Staircase-specific**

- Staircase cutting machine — string cutting · tread positioning · riser grooves
- CNC router — curved panels · decorative designs · cut strings · newel details · baluster components (accuracy · repeatability · complex designs)
- Lathe — turned newels · traditional balusters · decorative spindles (skilled turner territory)
- Press / clamping — laminated handrails · curved components · glued assemblies (especially critical for lamwood + curved handrail work — see `material-profile-lamwood.md`)

**Sanding**

- Wide belt sander — consistent finish prep across long panels
- Edge sanders — handrails · shaped components
- Hand sanding stations — final skilled inspection · never fully replaced by machine

**Finishing**

- Spray booth (extraction · controlled environment) — lacquer · paint · oils · stains
- Drying racks

**Installation kit**

- Cordless drills · impact drivers · circular saws · jigsaws · routers · oscillating multi-tools
- Common brands: Festool · Makita · DeWalt · Milwaukee

## Principle B · Hand tools still separate good from great

Even modern factories still use hand tools. A staircase workshop needs:

- **Chisels** — bevel edge · mortise (fine adjustments · joints · fitting)
- **Hand planes** — smoothing plane · block plane · shoulder plane (final fitting · correcting small imperfections)
- **Measuring** — tape measure · folding rule · combination square · sliding bevel · spirit level · laser level (**critical** — small errors create large problems)
- **Marking** — marking knife · pencils · marking gauge · chalk line
- **Hammers and mallets** — joints · adjustments · assembly
- **Clamps** — sash · quick · pipe · specialist curved (a staircase workshop uses many)

### Specialist staircase tools

- Stair gauges — attached to a square for repeated stair marking
- Router templates — consistent tread positions · baluster holes · newel fittings
- Spindle drilling jig — accurate baluster spacing

## Principle C · Small vs large workshop capability differences

- **Small traditional workshop** — planer/thicknesser · table saw · spindle moulder · bandsaw · lathe · hand tools · skilled craftsmen
- **Larger manufacturer** — CNC routers · automated sanding · production lines · barcode tracking · CAD/CAM systems · finishing booths

NEX must know which category the workshop it's serving falls into — recommendations that assume CNC capacity when the workshop is a five-person traditional shop are wrong (and vice versa).

## Principle D · Worker health determines career length, not talent

A skilled staircase maker with 20–40 years of experience develops knowledge machines cannot replace: timber selection · reading grain · fitting · finishing · solving problems on site. **The goal is not to leave the trade early — it is to work safely enough to stay healthy.**

The core rule:

> **A great staircase craftsman is not just someone who can make beautiful stairs. They are someone who can still enjoy making them 20, 30, or 40 years later because they protected their health along the way.**

### Wood dust is the biggest long-term risk

Wood dust can be harmful with regular exposure over years. The risk depends on: type of wood · amount of dust · exposure time · whether extraction is used · whether dust is inhaled.

**Fine dust is the main concern** — it can travel deep into the lungs. The dangerous dust is often the dust you cannot see.

**Health problems linked with wood dust:**

1. **Nose and throat irritation** — blocked nose · sneezing · itchy throat · coughing after work
2. **Skin irritation** — itchy skin · redness · rash after handling timber
3. **Breathing reactions** — wheezing · chest tightness · shortness of breath
4. **Wood allergies** — some species (cedar · iroko · mahogany · walnut · exotic hardwoods) trigger reactions more often, but common woods can also affect some people

### How to recognise a possible wood allergy

Look for a **pattern**. Example: fine on weekends but after sanding oak → blocked nose · after cutting MDF → coughing. That pattern suggests reaction to something in the workplace. Common signs:

- Nose: sneezing · congestion · runny nose
- Eyes: watering · itching · redness
- Skin: rash · itching
- Lungs: cough · wheezing · breathing difficulty

Persistent symptoms → **see a doctor**. NEX must never provide medical diagnosis — always refer to a doctor for assessment.

### The controls that professional workshops use

**1 · Extraction systems (most important control).** Machine extraction · ducting · dust collectors · filtered systems. Capture dust at the machine **before it reaches the breathing zone**.

**2 · Respiratory protection.** Especially during sanding · hand finishing · site fitting. A proper dust mask / respirator suitable for fine wood dust.

**3 · Clean workshop habits.** Vacuum dust · remove regularly · **avoid blowing dust around with compressed air** (that spreads fine dust into the air) · keep machines maintained.

### Other risks a workshop must manage

- **Hearing** — machines are loud (saws · planers · CNC) · hearing protection matters
- **Hands and joints** — repetitive sanding · heavy timber handling · awkward fitting positions · good technique helps · take breaks

## Why these four principles matter as a set

- **Principle A** stops NEX from treating the workshop as a bag of machines · layout and flow are the productivity foundation.
- **Principle B** stops NEX from assuming automation replaces hand tools — the final fit is where quality is felt.
- **Principle C** stops NEX from proposing recommendations that assume capabilities the workshop does not have.
- **Principle D** stops NEX from optimising for output at the expense of the people who make the output — long-term worker health IS long-term workshop value.

A generic AI advising on workshop operations will fail all four. An experienced workshop owner holds all four in composition (Principle 0003).
