# Timber Lifecycle Principles · From Tree to Graded Component

*Expert principles by Philip O'Farrell · captured 2026-07-28 · Layer 1 evidence. The journey a piece of timber takes from a growing tree to a finished staircase component — with the trade principles that govern each stage. NEX must understand this lifecycle to reason about material value, availability, and why a "long clear oak handrail blank" is a fundamentally different product from a "board of oak", even though both are technically the same species from the same forest.*

**Application note:** every principle in this file is subject to **Product Constitution Principle 0003** — trade principles never become rigid rules. NEX composes them into contextual judgement.

## Stage 1 · The tree in the forest

### There is no single "harvest at X metres" law

Trees are usually harvested based on a combination of factors — height alone is only one input:

- Age
- Diameter
- Timber quality
- Forest management plan
- Market demand
- Species
- Local regulations

### Typical harvest ages and dimensions by species

| Species | Group | Typical harvest age | Typical height | Typical diameter | Common use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pine | Softwood | 25–60 years (plantation) | 20–40m | 30–60cm | Construction, joinery, engineered products |
| Spruce | Softwood | 40–80 years | 30–50m | Varies | Construction timber, roof timber, engineered products |
| Oak | Hardwood | 80–150+ years | 20–40m | Large diameter needed for premium staircase | Staircases, furniture, doors, flooring |
| Walnut | Hardwood | 60–100+ years | 20–30m | Medium-large | Luxury interiors |
| Ash | Hardwood | 60–100 years | 25–35m | Medium | Modern staircases, sports equipment |

Softwoods are usually grown in managed plantation cycles (planted → thinned → harvested → replanted). Hardwoods take much longer and are less easily replaced — see `timber-market-principles.md` Principle B.

### The staircase-manufacturer point

A tree being tall does not mean it produces premium staircase timber. A staircase manufacturer wants:

- Straight trunk
- Large diameter
- Few defects
- Good grain
- Long clear sections

A premium oak suitable for large staircase components (long clear handrails · wide tread blanks · large newel billets) may be much older than the harvest-age range above. Those trees are the constrained resource.

### Forestry regulations (country-dependent)

Forestry is usually controlled through government regulations · forestry licences · environmental rules · protected woodland laws. Many countries require harvesting permits · replanting plans · biodiversity protection · sustainable cutting limits.

A landowner normally cannot cut any forest without considering local rules. This slows the response of supply to demand — which is one of the seven forces driving timber prices captured in `timber-market-principles.md` Principle A.

## Stage 2 · How sawmills source trees

Four main pathways:

**1 · Private forestry land** — a company owns forests · grows trees · harvests timber · sells logs to a mill.

**2 · Government / public forests** — some countries own large forest areas. Timber sold through contracts · auctions · approved harvesting companies.

**3 · Timber auctions** — common in some regions. A forest authority offers a defined harvest (e.g. 500 oak trees) · companies bid · highest approved bidder wins harvesting rights.

**4 · Vertically-integrated forestry companies** — large timber companies may own forests · manage forests · harvest trees · operate sawmills. Gives them control from tree to finished timber.

Each pathway carries different price and availability characteristics — a vertically-integrated supplier can offer different consistency than a company bidding at auction.

## Stage 3 · Reading the tree at the sawmill (rings intelligence)

*Reference image: [`reference-images/tree-rings-cross-section.jpg`](reference-images/tree-rings-cross-section.jpg) — hardwood cross-section showing concentric growth rings. Each ring is a year. Ring spacing tells the story of growth conditions, forest competition, and slow-grown vs fast-grown timber.*

Each year a tree normally creates a new growth layer visible in the log's cross-section.

```
     Outer bark
     ──────────
    /          \
   |  ring ring |
   |  ring ring |
    \          /
     ──────────
```

The **centre is oldest**. The **outside is newest** growth.

### Wide rings vs narrow rings

**Wide rings** usually mean fast growth · good sunlight · plenty of water · less competition. Often seen in plantation softwoods. Faster growth · looser grain · lower density typically.

**Narrow rings** usually mean slower growth · competition with other trees · older timber · difficult growing conditions. Slow-grown timber tends toward tighter grain · different strength characteristics · different appearance. In many premium hardwood contexts, narrower rings are actively preferred.

### Species-specific ring characteristics

**Hardwood (e.g. oak):** rings help show age · growth speed · grain pattern. Oak also has visible features — medullary rays · pores · characteristic grain — that create the attractive appearance used in furniture and staircases. Some cutting directions (quarter-sawn) expose these features intentionally.

**Pine:** shows earlywood (lighter, softer growth) and latewood (darker, denser growth). Spring growth = lighter band · summer growth = darker band. This creates the visible pine grain pattern many customers recognise.

### Why this matters for staircase companies

A customer sees *"Oak staircase"*. The manufacturer sees a much richer set of questions:

- What country?
- What forest?
- How old was the tree?
- Growth speed?
- Grade?
- Ring pattern?
- Knot structure? (see Principle 6 in wood-intelligence-principles.md)
- Moisture level?
- Cutting direction?

**Two oak boards from the same species can have completely different value.** That difference is invisible to the customer and central to the manufacturer.

## Stage 4 · From log to graded board

*Reference image: [`reference-images/oak-sawing-patterns-plain-rift-quarter.jpg`](reference-images/oak-sawing-patterns-plain-rift-quarter.jpg) — same oak species shown in three different sawing patterns (Plain Sawn · Rift Sawn · Quarter Sawn). The cutting direction is decided at the sawmill and materially affects the board's yield, stability, and figure. Choosing the cutting pattern for a specific component (e.g. quarter-sawn for premium visible treads) is part of staircase-quality craft.*

Most trees do not become staircase parts. A tree goes through a grading and sorting process at the sawmill, and only a small percentage becomes premium staircase or joinery material.

### The core principle

> **A tree is not sold as one quality product. It is divided into different grades for different markets.**

### The journey

```
Tree harvested
     ↓
Log arrives at sawmill
     ↓
Log inspected and graded
     ↓
Cut into boards
     ↓
Boards sorted by quality
     ↓
Different industries buy different grades
```

### Four grade tiers

**1 · Premium grade timber (small percentage of yield)**

The material staircase and furniture makers want.

- ✓ Straight grain
- ✓ Few defects
- ✓ Long lengths
- ✓ Good colour
- ✓ Suitable for visible finishes

Used for: staircase treads · handrails · newel posts · balusters · doors · high-end furniture · premium flooring.

**Example:** a large clear oak log might produce long handrail sections · wide tread blanks · premium boards. This material commands a much higher price than the same tree's other yield.

**2 · Joinery grade timber**

Very common in woodworking. Used for windows · doors · cabinets · mouldings · interior joinery.

May have small knots · colour variation · shorter lengths — still excellent quality for its purpose. Doesn't need the visible-face perfection of premium.

**3 · Construction grade timber**

A much larger market. Used for roof structures · framing · stud walls · joists · general building.

Focus is on strength · size · availability. Appearance is less important because the timber may be covered by plaster or cladding.

**4 · Lower grades and by-products**

Nothing is normally wasted. Lower-quality material becomes: pallets · packaging · fencing · chipboard · MDF · wood pellets · biomass fuel.

### Example · one oak tree's yield split

```
Premium centre sections       →  Handrails · stair treads · furniture
Good boards                   →  Doors · joinery
Lower grade                   →  General timber
Waste / offcuts               →  Panels · fuel
```

### Why staircase companies pay more for premium

A staircase uses **visible finished components**. A builder making a roof doesn't care if a timber beam has colour variation · knots · different grain — it's covered. But a customer looking at a varnished oak staircase sees everything.

A premium staircase may require matching grain · clean faces · consistent colour · stable timber. That's a small percentage of any given tree's yield — hence the price.

## How the four stages compose (for NEX Estimation and Buying Intelligence)

An owner or customer question like *"why is this oak handrail so much more expensive than the oak treads?"* triggers composition across all four stages:

- **Stage 1 (forest):** long clear lengths require large mature trees with straight trunks — the rarest input
- **Stage 2 (sourcing):** premium-length hardwood tends to move through slower, more controlled channels
- **Stage 3 (ring evidence at the mill):** slower growth + tight grain often preferred for premium visible work
- **Stage 4 (grading):** premium yield is a small percentage of any log — long clear premium is a small percentage of that

NEX composes all four stages into a natural answer — never asserts a single-line verdict (per Principle 0003).

Example answer shape:

> *"The handrail is priced higher because it's a long clear premium section — a small percentage of what a single oak log yields, and long clear lengths need larger mature trees to begin with. The treads are shorter and can be cut from a wider range of the same log, so more of the tree qualifies. Both are 'oak from the same supplier' but they represent very different slices of the raw material."*

That is trade judgement composed from evidence — the kind of answer an experienced buyer would give if a customer asked at the counter.
