# Material Profile · Lamwood

*Material profile for the eventual `material_profiles` Reference Brain module. Reworded and expanded 2026-07-28 with new expert content from Philip O'Farrell plus five reference photographs and two external-evidence sections (process times and cost context).*

Lamwood is a very important material category for staircase manufacturing because it sits **between solid timber and engineered timber**. It gives manufacturers more control over size, stability, and appearance.

The important lesson NEX must carry:

> **Lamwood is not a lower-quality replacement for solid timber. In many staircase applications it is the professional manufacturing choice because it solves problems that solid timber cannot.**

## What lamwood is

Lamwood is made from multiple pieces of timber glued together to create a larger, more stable component. The individual pieces are variously called:

- lamellas
- strips
- staves
- sections

The manufacturing process controls:

- Grain direction
- Moisture content
- Defects (removed or hidden)
- Colour matching (visual grade)
- Movement (opposing grain balances internal tension)

## Two main types used in staircase manufacturing

### 1 · Edge-glued laminated timber

Commonly used for stair treads, risers, window boards, shelves, and worktops. Several narrower boards are joined side-by-side.

Top-view schematic:

```
| oak strip | oak strip | oak strip | oak strip |
        ↑ glue joints ↑
```

Advantages:

- ✓ Wide boards possible
- ✓ More stable than one wide solid board
- ✓ Less natural splitting
- ✓ Better material utilisation
- ✓ Consistent appearance

### 2 · Finger-jointed laminated timber

Uses smaller pieces joined with finger joints along the length.

Schematic:

```
====\/====\/====\/====
```

Used for long lengths, structural components, production timber.

Advantages:

- ✓ Long lengths possible
- ✓ Removes defects (cut around them then rejoin)
- ✓ Better yield from timber
- ✓ More predictable movement

**Trade note:** for premium visible staircase work, many manufacturers prefer **clear edge-glued material** rather than obvious finger joints. Finger-jointed material is often used where the joint is hidden or painted (structural cores · painted risers · hidden framing).

## Why staircase manufacturers use lamwood

A staircase is a **precision product**. The manufacturer needs:

- Straight strings
- Consistent tread thickness
- Matching risers
- Stable handrails
- Repeatable machining

Solid timber can carry natural stresses (grain pull · bowing · twisting · seasonal movement). Lamwood construction reduces every one of those problems — the strip-and-glue process is a deliberate engineering choice to control what solid timber cannot.

## Manufacturing process

Five deliberate stages. Each is a control point where quality is either gained or lost.

**1 · Selected and graded**

Timber is chosen for colour, grain, defects, and moisture content. Lower-quality sections are removed. This step controls final appearance and stability tolerances.

**2 · Cut into strips / boards**

Timber is machined into smaller sections. Strips are arranged to control appearance (colour matching · grain flow) and movement (opposing grain directions).

*Reference image: [`lamwood-oak-entering-milling-line.jpg`](reference-images/lamwood-oak-entering-milling-line.jpg) — oak strips headed into the milling machinery on a production conveyor.*

**3 · Jointed and glued**

Pieces are glued together under pressure. Grain direction and layout are chosen to reduce movement — this is the mechanical reason lamwood is more dimensionally stable than a solid piece of the same species.

**4 · Pressed and cured**

The panel is clamped until the adhesive has fully bonded. Under-cure is the primary cause of glue-line failure in service, which is why storage rules (dry · flat · protected) matter even for finished blanks.

**5 · Calibrated and sanded**

The finished blank is machined to accurate thickness. High-grade products are sanded to very fine finishes before machining into components.

*Reference image: [`lamwood-pine-leaving-drying-process.jpg`](reference-images/lamwood-pine-leaving-drying-process.jpg) — pine lamwood boards leaving the drying process on a large production line, giving a sense of the scale industrial lamwood is produced at.*

Typical times for each stage are captured in the *External evidence · process times* section at the bottom of this file.

### Lamwood strings

Strings are structural — they carry the staircase load and must stay straight over the full flight length.

Requirements: length · thickness · stability · straightness

Common constructions: laminated sections · engineered timber · finger-jointed material · glued-up blanks

Common thicknesses: 28mm · 32mm · 38mm · 40mm+

Common working lengths (depends heavily on supplier and species): 2.4m · 3.0m · 3.6m · 4.2m · longer special-order lengths

**Trade note:** longer lengths become more valuable because they reduce joints and waste. A supplier who can consistently produce 4.2m+ oak strings has an offer worth remembering.

### Lamwood handrails (straight and curved)

*Reference image: [`lamwood-joinery-shop-staircase-assembly.jpg`](reference-images/lamwood-joinery-shop-staircase-assembly.jpg) — the joinery-shop technique of forming lamwood into shape, then cutting on the bandsaw and machining. Same technique used for curved handrails.*

Lamwood is particularly valuable for **curved handrails**. In a joinery shop the process is:

1. Thin lamellas are laid up around a former in the shape of the finished handrail
2. Glued and clamped until cured
3. Cut on the bandsaw to remove excess
4. Machined to the final handrail profile (mopstick · pigs-ear · square · traditional)

The reason lamwood dominates curved handrail work: a solid piece of hardwood **cannot be bent** to follow a curve without steaming (specialist), extensive selection of grain, and significant risk of springback. Laminated construction lets each thin lamella follow the curve independently, and the glue holds the assembled bundle in the new shape.

Straight-length lamwood handrails are also common — they solve the stability problem discussed in `wood-intelligence-principles.md` Principle 2 (long solid hardwood carries grain tension). The trade-off is that a long solid hardwood handrail can be *the more valuable product* because it demonstrates rare timber availability and craftsmanship — same principle, applied honestly.

### The buying rule (critical)

**The country alone does not define quality.** The real quality equation:

```
Country
  +
Species
  +
Timber grade
  +
Glue system
  +
Moisture control
  +
Manufacturing tolerance
  +
Finish quality
  =
Real quality
```

**Practical example:** a high-grade Romanian European Oak lamwood panel may outperform a lower-grade product from a more famous country. Grade + process + specification beats brand geography.

## Length availability

Length is a valuable specification in its own right — long clear sections reduce joints, reduce waste, and demonstrate supplier capability.

**Stair treads / panels:** 1.0m · 1.2m · 1.5m · 2.0m · 2.4m · 3.0m (longer = more valuable)

**Handrail blanks:** 3.0m · 3.6m · 4.2m · 4.8m (long clear solid pieces are premium — see Principle 2)

**String material:** 3.0m · 3.6m · 4.2m+ (long strings reduce joints, less waste, easier production)

## Secondary applications (component reuse)

A lamwood stair tread blank isn't only a stair tread. NEX must know what an experienced tradesperson would consider using it for. See Principle 5 (component reuse intelligence) in `wood-intelligence-principles.md` for the trade rule this enables.

**Common secondary uses:**

- Internal window boards
- Shelves
- Radiator shelves
- Mantle shelves

A 40mm oak laminated tread blank could be cut for any of the above, provided three checks pass:

**1 · Edge finish** — a stair tread carries a nosing profile; a window board may need a square or bevel edge. Factor re-machining into the cost.

**2 · Moisture location** — internal fine. External / condensation / wet-room applications need proper sealing. **Lamwood is never outdoor timber.**

**3 · Cost efficiency** — sometimes a tread blank is *higher* specification than a window board needs; a joinery panel or glued board may be cheaper. Surface both options.

## Handling rules

- ✓ Store dry
- ✓ Store flat
- ✓ Protect from moisture
- ✗ **Never** expose to water — moisture affects glue lines, bonding strength, surface stability, and can create visible defects after drying
- ✗ Never treat as outdoor timber
- ⚠  Sealing required for any application near moisture (external doors, condensation zones, wet rooms)

## External evidence · Process times

*Layer 1 evidence · collected from published external sources · **NOT expert-authored trade content** · times vary by supplier, adhesive, and equipment. Present these as reference ranges only, never as guaranteed times for any specific product.*

### Kiln drying (raw timber → lamella-ready)

| Stage | Typical range | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Green wood at input | 30–60%+ moisture content | Ohio State Extension · hardwood drying overview |
| Kiln target for interior hardwood | 6–8% moisture content | Kiln-dried lumber standards |
| Lamella target (adhesive-dependent) | 3–5% or 10–12% MC | Depends on adhesive + pressing method |

Kiln drying is the longest stage in the whole lamwood cycle — typically **days to weeks** for hardwoods depending on thickness, species, and target moisture content. The kiln cycle has sub-stages: sorting & stacking · warm-up / equalization · main drying (free water then bound water) · conditioning / steam relaxation · final equalization.

### Pressing and glue cure (assembled lamellas → cured blank)

| Stage | Typical range | Source |
|---|---|---|
| PVA press time (adequate shear bond) | ≥ 4 hours (no significant additional gain beyond this in tested studies) | Springer · pressing-time study on edge-glued panels |
| PVA set (unclamp-ready) | 30–60 minutes at 70°F / 50% humidity | PVA cure guides |
| PVA full cure | 24 hours (up to 48h at higher humidity / lower temperature) | PVA cure guides |
| PUR (polyurethane) cure | Varies · consult manufacturer datasheet — typically faster than PVA under controlled conditions | Flagged for future evidence collection |

### Full production sequence timing (industrial rough estimate)

- Kiln drying · **days to weeks** (species/thickness dependent)
- Lamella prep (cross-cut, end-seal, rip, dress) · **hours per batch**
- Glue application + press + cure · **minimum ~4h press, 24h full cure**
- Calibration + sanding + inspection · **hours per batch**

Small joinery shops may run smaller, slower cycles; large industrial plants (like the pine drying line in the reference photograph) run continuous flow. Times are indicative only.

## External evidence · General cost context

*Layer 1 evidence · **relative language only** per the no-prices HARD LAW (published prices go stale within weeks — every manufacturer has different process · buying power · materials · machines · labour). ### Relative material tiers (UK staircase market context)

| Tier | Materials | Relative position |
|---|---|---|
| Budget | Pine · softwoods | Cheapest tier |
| Mid | Standard hardwoods (oak, ash) | Broadly ~2× softwood cost |
| Premium | Walnut · rare / long clear sections · specialist species | Roughly 2× standard hardwood cost — every step up the material ladder roughly doubles the supply portion |
| Complex geometry | Curved · spiral · winder geometries · bespoke hardwood | Multiple further factors — dominated by labour + machining, not material alone |

The 2× tier progression is a **rule of thumb** from published UK cost guides; actual pricing varies significantly by supplier, grade, and specification.
