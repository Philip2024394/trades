## The governing principle

> **The newel is the structural anchor for the handrail and balustrade. If the newel is loose, the entire handrail can become unstable.**

Newel post installation is one of the most important jobs on a staircase because everything above the string level hangs off the newels. Getting the newel wrong risks the whole balustrade — which is a fall-protection element (see `customer-faq-staircase.md` Group I · the DIY-vs-professional boundary is drawn at structure and fall protection).

Two very different jobs:

- **Refurbishment** — removing an existing newel and fitting a replacement into an installed staircase
- **New staircase installation** — factory-made staircase being assembled on site

Both require distinct knowledge and are covered separately below.

---

## Part 1 · Removing an existing newel post (refurbishment)

The first step is **identifying how it is fixed**, because there is no single method.

### Fixing methods NEX must recognise

**Older staircases** may use:

- Mortise and tenon joints
- Large timber dowels
- Wedges
- Glue
- Coach screws
- Nails

**Modern staircases** may use:

- Threaded newel fixings
- Hanger bolts
- Steel fixing plates
- Concealed brackets
- Proprietary fixing kits

Each fixing type requires a **different removal method**. Guessing risks damaging the string, tread, or surrounding timber.

### Step 1 · Remove the handrail assembly

Normally remove:

- Handrail
- Balusters
- Baserail (where necessary)

This exposes the post and makes it easier to inspect what fixing is actually holding it in place.

### Step 2 · Look for hidden fixings

Check for:

- Timber plugs
- Filler hiding screws
- Removable caps
- Bolts underneath the staircase
- Access panels

Many fixings are **deliberately hidden**. A visible newel may reveal its fixing only after a plug is removed or an access panel opened.

### Step 3 · Determine the fixing type

Typical possibilities:

```
Option A                    Option B                          Option C
--------                    --------                          --------
Handrail                    Handrail                          Handrail
   ↓                            ↓                                 ↓
Newel                       Newel                             Newel
   ↓                            ↓                                 ↓
Bolted to floor joist       Mortise & tenon into string       Steel fixing plate
```

Each requires a different removal method — the pattern matters.

### Step 4 · Remove carefully

The goal is to **save as much of the staircase as possible**. Avoid:

- Splitting strings
- Damaging treads
- Damaging flooring

Sometimes the post comes away intact. Sometimes it must be cut into sections before removal to avoid damaging the fixings-that-can't-easily-be-reached without breaking the surrounding structure.

### The common refurbishment shortcut

Many staircase renovators do **not remove the entire original post**. Instead they:

1. Remove handrail
2. Remove spindles
3. Cut the old newel square
4. Leave the lower structural section in place
5. Fit a new box newel over the existing core, OR use a spigot/dowel system

This is quicker and avoids disturbing the staircase structure. The choice depends on the design and how much of the visible newel needs to change vs how much can stay as a hidden core.

## Part 2 · Installing a newel in a new staircase

Professional staircase manufacturers usually **pre-machine the joints**. Factory-built staircases often have mortices · tenons · dowel holes · numbered components — the installer assembles per the drawings.

### The typical installation sequence

```
Fit string
    ↓
Fit newel
    ↓
Check square
    ↓
Fit first tread
    ↓
Fit first riser
    ↓
Glue wedges
    ↓
Fit remaining staircase
```

### The critical check · square to the tread, not to a spirit level

Experienced staircase manufacturers recommend checking the newel with a **carpenter's / roofing square against the tread**, rather than relying only on a spirit level.

The reason: the tread establishes the staircase geometry. A newel that is perfectly plumb by spirit level but not square to the tread will throw the entire balustrade out of alignment. **The tread is the reference, not gravity.**

### Adhesives · glue placement

Normally PVA woodworking adhesive · applied to:

- Mortise
- Tenon
- Dowels

Large amounts of glue are often used inside structural joints. This is a case where more glue is genuinely better — it fills voids and locks the joint against future movement.

### Dowels

Many timber staircases use hardwood dowels. Purpose:

- **Alignment** — the dowel guides the mating parts together in the correct position
- **Strength** — adds shear strength across the joint
- **Pulling the joint together** — a well-fitted dowel + glue creates a tight seat

### Wedges

Traditional housed strings use wedges to lock treads and risers into their housings.

```
String housing
    ↓
Tread inserted
    ↓
Glue applied
    ↓
Wedge driven tight
    ↓
Locks everything together
```

The wedges tighten the joint and help prevent movement. A modern equivalent (mechanical fixings + adhesive) achieves the same result but the traditional wedge is still widely used, particularly on premium hardwood staircases.

### Clamping and cure time

Professional installers often clamp the assembly while the glue cures — keeps the joints fully seated. The strap/tensioner in `staircase-install-strap-clamp-handrail-balusters.jpg` shows this technique used at scale across a whole flight.

**Many manufacturers recommend allowing glued joints to cure fully before putting the staircase into normal use** to reduce future movement or squeaks. Walking on a freshly-glued staircase before cure is one of the most common installation-day mistakes.

### On-site adaptation is normal

Real-world houses have uneven walls · imperfect openings · floors slightly out of level. Even a perfectly-manufactured staircase often needs on-site adjustment — see `staircase-install-dryfit-oak-onsite.jpg`. This is not a manufacturing failure; it's the reality of fitting bespoke joinery into a real building. The dry-fit stage exists precisely to identify these adjustments before any glue is applied.

### Typical tools

**Removal:** pry bar · oscillating multi-tool · handsaw · reciprocating saw · chisels · hammer · screwdrivers · drill

**Installation:** clamps · mallet · glue bottle · roofing/carpenter's square · tape measure · drill · impact driver

### Common mistakes NEX should warn about

- ❌ Using a spirit level instead of a square to the tread — throws the balustrade geometry
- ❌ Forgetting to dry-fit before applying glue — one mistake becomes permanent
- ❌ Not checking handrail alignment before final fix — post looks right on its own but wrong under load
- ❌ Overtightening fixings and splitting timber — hardwood is strong but not infinite
- ❌ Walking on a glued staircase before the adhesive has cured — the movement fights the cure

## Part 5 · Fitting balusters (spindles)

Fitting new balusters is one of the most common staircase renovation projects. **The correct method depends on the type of staircase.** A timber balustrade is a safety feature — balusters must be securely fixed and correctly spaced.

Reference image: [`reference-images/staircase-install-baluster-fitting-composite.jpg`](reference-images/staircase-install-baluster-fitting-composite.jpg) — glue application · fitting to tread · completed balustrade.

### Step 1 · Identify the staircase system

Four common systems, each with a different installation method:

- **Grooved handrail + baserail** — baluster fits into grooves; infill fillets between balusters
- **Handrail without grooves** — baluster fixed directly to the tread and handrail
- **Metal spindle system**
- **Glass panel system**

### Step 2 · Remove the old balusters

- Remove the handrail if necessary
- Carefully cut or pull out the old balusters
- Remove old glue and loose fixings
- Clean all joint surfaces

**Take photographs before removing everything** — captures the original layout for reference.

### Step 3 · Check spacing (safety-critical)

In the UK, the gaps in a domestic balustrade are generally designed so that a **100 mm sphere cannot pass through** — a key safety requirement used in building regulations.

Before fitting every spindle, check:

- Equal spacing
- Straight alignment
- Correct height

### Step 4 · Dry fit everything first

Before using glue:

- Place every baluster in position
- Check they all reach the handrail correctly
- Make sure the handrail sits naturally

**Don't assume every spindle is exactly the same length** — small variation is normal in bespoke work and site-cut spindles.

### Step 5 · Fix the balusters (method depends on system)

**Method A · Grooved handrail and baserail**

```
Handrail
=================
|      |      |
|      |      |
Baluster Baluster
|      |      |
=================
Baserail
```

- Insert the bottom first
- Tilt into the top groove
- Apply the recommended woodworking adhesive where required
- Fit the infill fillets between balusters if your system uses them

**Method B · Direct fixing**

Some staircases use:

- Dowels
- Screws
- Concealed fixing kits
- Glue

Screw heads are often hidden with timber plugs.

### Step 6 · Fit the handrail

Once all balusters are positioned:

- Fit the handrail
- Check the line is straight
- Make final adjustments before tightening fixings

### Step 7 · Final inspection

- ✅ Every baluster is secure
- ✅ The handrail has no movement
- ✅ The spacing is consistent
- ✅ No glue squeeze-out remains on visible surfaces

### Common mistakes

- ❌ Measuring only the first baluster
- ❌ Forgetting to number balusters if they're individually cut
- ❌ Applying glue before checking alignment
- ❌ Uneven spacing
- ❌ Damaging the timber with excessive force
- ❌ Using indoor adhesive in damp conditions where it's not suitable

### Helpful tools

Tape measure · combination square · spirit level · mitre saw (if trimming is needed) · drill/driver · wood glue (where appropriate) · clamps · rubber mallet · sandpaper · pencil.

### Three questions before starting

- Are the new balusters exactly the same size as the old ones?
- Are you replacing timber with timber, or changing to metal balusters?
- Is your handrail already removed, or are you trying to fit balusters without removing it?

The answers determine the easiest and safest installation method.

## Part 6 · Fitting staircase facing wood covers (renovation cladding)

Decorative timber facings cover the front of an existing staircase during renovation. **Four common products, each installed differently:**

- **String cladding (string cover)** — covers the visible side of the staircase
- **Riser covers** — fit over existing risers
- **Tread covers** — fit over existing treads
- **Fascia or apron panels** — decorative panels fitted to the side or underneath

### Universal pre-check (all cladding types)

Before fitting **any** cladding, verify:

- The staircase structure is solid
- No rot or water damage
- No loose treads or strings
- Any squeaks or movement are repaired first

> **Decorative covers should not be used to hide structural problems.**

This is one of the biggest renovation mistakes — cladding over structural failure just delays the failure and makes it more expensive to fix when it emerges.

### Installing a string cover (side facing)

One of the most popular staircase renovation jobs.

**Step 1 · Inspect the existing string** — it should be firmly fixed · dry · reasonably flat · free from loose paint or damaged timber. Repair any loose areas first.

**Step 2 · Measure carefully** — overall length · height · angle · position of newel posts · handrail brackets · any sockets or obstacles. Most string covers are cut slightly oversize and trimmed for a precise fit.

**Step 3 · Dry fit** — place the panel in position · check joints around newels · check the top and bottom fit · trim if required. **Never glue first and measure afterwards.**

**Step 4 · Fix the panel** — most timber string covers are fixed using a suitable construction adhesive recommended by the manufacturer · pins or brads (if specified) · clamps while the adhesive cures, where practical. Avoid excessive adhesive that squeezes onto the finished face.

**Step 5 · Finish** — fill any visible fixing holes · lightly sand where necessary · apply the matching finish if supplied unfinished · seal any small joints with a paintable decorator's caulk if the design calls for it.

### Installing riser covers

```
Existing riser
================

New riser cover
================
```

Sequence: clean the old riser · measure accurately · trim to size · dry fit · bond with the recommended adhesive · clamp or brace until secure if needed.

### Installing tread covers (higher accuracy required)

The tread is more heavily loaded, so accuracy is important:

```
Clean tread
    ↓
Measure
    ↓
Trim
    ↓
Dry fit
    ↓
Apply recommended adhesive
    ↓
Fit tread cover
    ↓
Weight or clamp while curing
```

**The tread must sit flat with no rocking** — any wobble becomes a permanent trip hazard once glued down.

### Common mistakes (all cladding types)

- ❌ Fitting over loose or damaged timber
- ❌ Ignoring moisture problems
- ❌ Not checking the staircase is level before cutting
- ❌ Using the wrong adhesive for the manufacturer's instructions
- ❌ Rushing measurements

### Tools commonly used

Tape measure · combination square · pencil · mitre saw or circular saw (for trimming) · jigsaw (for detailed cuts) · clamps · caulking gun · sandpaper · vacuum or brush for cleaning.

## Part 8 · Site assembly and section delivery

A staircase can be designed to be assembled in sections inside the house — many high-quality staircase companies do this. In fact, **many bespoke staircases are not delivered as one complete piece** because they would be too large or heavy to get through doors, hallways, or tight corners.

### The critical distinction

> **A staircase designed for assembly maintains quality. A staircase cut apart on site to make it fit loses strength and accuracy.**

The first is professional practice. The second is a workshop failure being disguised as an installer problem.

### How professional staircase companies deliver

**Straight staircase — options:**

- Assembled flight with newels · handrail · balusters delivered separately
- OR strings separate · treads and risers fitted on site

**Large or bespoke staircase — often delivered in sections:**

```
Section 1              Section 2              Section 3              Balustrade
--------              ---------              ---------              ---------
Bottom newel     +    Middle flight     +    Top landing      +     Handrail
+ first steps                                section                 + balusters
```

Each section's joints are **already planned** in the factory. Assembly follows the plan, not improvisation.

### Factory-made joints are designed for assembly

Professional manufacturers use: housed string joints · dowels · tenons · wedges · bolts · concealed connectors · engineered fixing systems. The staircase is built so the installer can **accurately reconnect** everything on site.

### What keeps the quality when assembled on site

**1 · Accurate factory machining.** Tread housings · riser grooves · newel joints · handrail angles — all machined precisely before delivery.

**2 · Correct numbering.** Good companies mark parts:

```
A1 · Bottom newel
A2 · First tread
A3 · Baluster position 1

B1 · Upper flight
B2 · Landing section
```

Prevents mistakes when reassembling on site.

**3 · Factory dry assembly before delivery.** Many quality workshops build the staircase in the factory · check fit · dismantle carefully · package · reinstall on site. **It's like a test build** — catches problems before the site visit.

### What should NOT happen

- ❌ Deliver a complete staircase
- ❌ Discover it will not fit through the door
- ❌ Cut strings and joints on site to force it in
- ❌ Rebuild without proper connections

Cost: reduced strength · alignment issues · degraded appearance · future squeaks.

### The one-way problem — dismantling a fully-glued staircase

A factory-assembled staircase using strong glue joints **may not come apart cleanly** · may damage timber · may weaken joints when rebuilt. Professional staircases intended for site assembly are made differently — the joint strategy is chosen at design time, not decided when the van gets stuck at the front door.

### Curved staircases · often always in sections

Large sweeping staircases (like American-style grand staircases) are often delivered in sections including curved strings · laminated handrails · curved landing sections · pre-shaped balustrades. Assembly requires specialist installers · this is not a straight-flight-with-extra-parts.

### Does site assembly reduce value?

**No.** Many premium staircases are assembled on site. Quality comes from timber selection · manufacturing accuracy · joint design · installer skill. **A fully assembled staircase is not automatically better.**

Actually the opposite is often true — a good staircase maker will **prefer** controlled site assembly in many houses because it reduces damage risk during transport and lets the staircase be fitted perfectly to the building. Site assembly is a professional choice, not a compromise.

### Questions every customer should ask before ordering

- Is the staircase delivered assembled or in sections?
- Has it been factory trial fitted?
- Are joints designed for site assembly?
- Who installs it?
- Are installation instructions provided?
- Are any cuts required on site?

These questions are captured in `customer-faq-staircase.md` Group A and Group E; they compose with this file for the workshop-side answer.
