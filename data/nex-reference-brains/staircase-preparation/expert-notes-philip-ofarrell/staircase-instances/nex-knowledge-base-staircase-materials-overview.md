# Staircase Materials Overview

The timber (or other material) you choose changes how your staircase looks, feels underfoot, wears with age, and costs to buy. Here are the options and the questions worth thinking about.

## Common staircase timbers

Popular choices from Nex Stairplan's master material library:

- **European Oak** — warm golden-brown · durable · UK default premium timber · works in Georgian townhouses and modern new-builds
- **American White Oak** — pale honey tones · clean straight grain · favourite for modern and Scandinavian interiors · pairs well with frameless glass balustrades
- **American Walnut** — darker luxury appearance
- **European Ash** · **Sapele** · **Genuine Mahogany** · **American Cherry** · **Hard Maple** · **European Beech** · other hardwoods available for specific looks
- **Scandinavian Pine (Redwood)** — pale yellow to light amber · UK default painted-staircase timber · economical · takes primer and paint smoother than any hardwood
- **Douglas Fir** · **Hemlock** — softwood alternatives
- **Accoya (modified softwood)** · **MDF Paint Grade** · **White Primed Timber** — engineered and painted options

## Oak vs Pine

Not always oak. Oak is harder, more durable, premium appearance. Pine is more affordable, easier to paint, suitable for many homes. The best material depends on budget, design, finish.

## Should the staircase match the flooring?

Not always. Great interiors often contrast — oak floor + painted staircase · walnut floor + oak staircase · stone floor + oak staircase. Colours and styles should complement, not necessarily match.

## Should internal doors match the staircase?

Generally yes — see the Design Ideas article for door-staircase pairings. Traditional (4-panel doors + turned staircase). Modern (Shaker doors + square newels + black balusters). Contemporary (flush doors + glass balustrade).

## Timber or glass balustrade?

- **Timber** — warm · traditional · easier to repair.
- **Glass** — modern · brighter · open feel.

Neither is universally better — depends on the design. **Children can safely use glass staircases** when designed and installed to appropriate safety standards with suitable toughened or laminated glass where required.

## Can I mix wood species?

Yes. Examples: oak staircase with walnut handrail · painted strings with oak treads · ash handrail with painted balusters. Mixing done thoughtfully creates distinctive designs.

## Why do two oak staircases look different?

Timber is natural. Colour, grain, knots, growth conditions vary tree-to-tree. Every board is unique.

## Why does the same staircase look different in my home?

Lighting changes everything — natural light, paint colours, flooring, room size all influence appearance.

## Should every tread look identical?

No. Natural timber variation is character. A good manufacturer matches boards carefully, but identical appearance is not realistic.

## Why is there filler in a knot?

Small knots or tiny defects may be professionally filled before finishing — improves appearance while maintaining structural integrity.

## Timber grade and origin

Fair questions to ask any staircase company:

- Which species?
- Is it kiln dried?
- Is it certified from responsibly managed forests?
- Is it solid or engineered/laminated?

Customers increasingly value origin transparency.

## Finishes

A designer should understand that not all oak or pine is equal — grain direction, knot placement, colour variation, board width, stability, matching between components all matter. The same staircase can look completely different depending on finish:

- **Natural oil** — shows grain, texture, warmth
- **Clear lacquer** — protection, cleaner appearance
- **Stained finish** — changes colour, character
- **Painted** — colour matching, lower timber cost acceptable

## Matte or gloss?

Matte is currently popular — less glare, more natural look. Gloss is a more traditional polished appearance.

## Should I carpet my staircase?

Depends on priorities. Carpet is quieter, warmer, better grip, protects the timber. Exposed timber shows the natural wood, is easier to clean, gives a more premium appearance. Some do both — carpet runner with exposed edges.

## LED lighting

Increasingly popular. Common locations: under handrail · wall string · under floating treads · on the landing. Lighting can transform a staircase visually.

## The spend-where-visible rule

Allocate material and finish budget preferentially to components the owner touches daily and sees prominently.

- **Spend more on:** handrail · visible treads · newel posts · feature balustrade — anything the customer touches or sees.
- **Spend less on:** hidden structure · painted components · areas nobody sees.

Example: a customer wants a luxury oak staircase. A designer may propose oak treads (visible · touched daily) · oak handrail · painted newels (paint hides pine) · painted strings (mostly hidden). Result: the staircase looks premium and costs materially less than "all oak everything". That's design judgement, not cost cutting.
