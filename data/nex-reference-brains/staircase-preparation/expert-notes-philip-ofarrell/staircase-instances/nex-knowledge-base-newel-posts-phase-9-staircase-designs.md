# Newel Posts · Phase 9 · In Different Staircase Designs and Real-World Applications

By this stage you understand what a newel post is, how it is built, where it is positioned, the different sizes, materials and structural requirements. The next step is understanding why different staircase designs need different newel arrangements.

A staircase designer does not choose newel posts simply because they look attractive. Every staircase layout creates different loads, turning forces, fixing methods and visual requirements.

## The Relationship Between Stair Design and Newel Placement

The staircase layout determines: number of newel posts · newel locations · newel height · newel size · fixing method · whether structural or decorative · handrail termination · balustrade direction.

Every staircase should first be designed structurally. The newel layout comes afterwards.

## Straight Flight Staircase

The simplest staircase. Usually contains a bottom newel and a top newel. Sometimes only two newels are required.

- **Bottom newel** carries the first handrail · baserail · beginning of balustrade
- **Top newel** carries landing rails · stair handrail · landing balustrade

This is the most common UK domestic arrangement.

## Straight Flight Between Two Walls

Sometimes no balustrades exist. Wall on each side · no exposed side · no balusters · no handrail on open side. Sometimes no newels at all. Only wall-mounted handrails.

## Cut String Staircase

Traditional joinery staircase. Requires visible newels. The decorative cut string makes the newels highly visible. Customers often choose turned · fluted · stop chamfered options because they become architectural features.

## Closed String Staircase

Modern construction. The string hides tread ends. Usually plain square newels. Cleaner appearance · minimal detailing. Very common in modern housing.

## Open Plan Staircase

Large hallway. Open on one or both sides. Requires multiple newels. Every change in balustrade direction usually needs another newel.

## Quarter Turn Staircase

90° turn. Requires additional support. Typical arrangement: Bottom → Intermediate corner newel → Top landing newel. The turning point creates additional forces. Corner newels are often structural.

## Half Turn Staircase

180° turn. Normally requires: Bottom → Landing newel → Second landing newel → Top newel. Large staircases may contain four or more major newels.

## Winder Staircase

Uses wedge-shaped steps. The turning area creates unusual handrail geometry. Newels often support changing handrail angles · changing baserails · multiple rail intersections. Good manufacturing accuracy becomes very important.

## Curved Staircases

Very different engineering. Curved stairs use curved handrails · curved strings · curved baserails. Newels often become major visual features. Large entrance staircases often have enormous feature newels.

## Helical Staircases

Unlike curved staircases, helical stairs have no central column. The handrail continuously twists. The newel arrangement becomes more complex. Sometimes bottom only · sometimes top and bottom · sometimes intermediate feature posts. Every staircase is different.

## Spiral Staircases

Very different. Usually have one central steel column instead of conventional newels. The centre column performs the structural role. Traditional newels may not exist. Landing rails may still terminate into small square posts.

## Floating Staircases

Floating stairs often appear to have no supports. The newels become visually minimal. Sometimes only 90mm square · sometimes glass · sometimes steel · sometimes wall mounted handrails remove the need completely.

## Mono String Staircases

One central steel beam. Balustrades often fix directly into tread · steel beam · side brackets. Newels become smaller. Sometimes decorative only.

## Twin String Staircases

Steel string each side. Handrails may terminate into steel posts instead of timber newels. Mixed-material staircases are increasingly common.

## Glass Balustrade Staircases

Several options exist:

**Glass with timber newels** — traditional appearance.

**Glass with stainless steel posts** — no timber newels. Posts replace them.

**Frameless Glass** — handrail may disappear into wall. Bottom support hidden. Very little resembles traditional joinery.

## Commercial Staircases

Commercial stairs often use steel posts rather than timber newels. Reason: higher traffic · greater structural loads · lower maintenance.

## Traditional Victorian Staircases

Usually contain large turned newels. Decorative caps. Heavy mouldings. Rich detailing. The newels become furniture.

## Edwardian Staircases

Generally lighter. Elegant. Less heavily carved. Still feature turned posts.

## Georgian Staircases

Very symmetrical. Large proportions. Often box newels or classical turned designs.

## Modern Contemporary Homes

Current trends: square newels · flush caps · concealed fixings · oak · glass · black steel. Very clean appearance.

## Farmhouse Style

Common choices: chunky oak · rustic texture · visible grain · stop chamfers · hand-finished appearance.

## Industrial Style

Often combines oak with black steel. Newels may be steel box section · wrapped oak · powder coated.

## Luxury Staircases

Feature homes often use oversized newels. Examples: 120mm · 140mm · 160mm. Custom-made. Sometimes illuminated · sometimes carved · sometimes incorporating steel cores.

## Which Newel Styles Suit Each Staircase?

| Stair Type | Typical Newel Style |
|---|---|
| Straight flight | Plain square or turned |
| Closed string | Plain square |
| Cut string | Turned or stop chamfered |
| Contemporary | Square oak |
| Glass staircase | Square or steel |
| Commercial | Steel posts |
| Traditional | Turned |
| Victorian | Decorative turned |
| Georgian | Classical box newels |
| Floating | Minimal square or concealed |
| Mono string | Steel posts |
| Spiral | Usually no traditional newels |

## Key Principle

A newel post is never chosen in isolation. The staircase geometry determines where it is needed · whether it must be structural · how large it should be · what material it should be made from · how the handrails connect · and whether it becomes a visual feature or remains almost invisible.

Understanding the relationship between staircase type and newel layout is one of the core skills of staircase design, because the correct newel arrangement is what ties together the structure, safety, and appearance of the entire staircase.
