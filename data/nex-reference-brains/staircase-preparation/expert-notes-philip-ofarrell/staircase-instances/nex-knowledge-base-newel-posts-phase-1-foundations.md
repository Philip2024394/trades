# Newel Posts · Phase 1 · Foundations, Purpose, Types and Materials

A newel post is one of the most important structural and visual components of a staircase. While many customers think a newel post is simply a decorative timber post, its primary job is to anchor and strengthen the balustrade system. Almost every timber staircase relies on correctly positioned newel posts to provide rigidity, support the handrail, and transfer loads safely into the staircase structure or surrounding building.

Understanding newel posts is essential because almost every staircase design decision affects them.

## What is a Newel Post?

A newel post is the main vertical support member of a staircase. It normally performs several jobs simultaneously:

- Supports the handrail
- Anchors the balustrade
- Prevents the handrail moving sideways
- Transfers horizontal loads into the stair structure
- Provides fixing points for strings, rails and panels
- Creates visual transitions where the staircase changes direction
- Forms one of the strongest structural members within the balustrade

Without properly fixed newel posts, even perfectly manufactured stairs can develop movement within the handrail.

## Why Newel Posts Matter

Customers often notice handrails and treads first. Joiners notice newel posts first.

A staircase with poor newel installation usually develops:

- Loose handrails
- Squeaks
- Movement
- Baluster gaps
- Cracked joints
- Rail deflection

Most experienced staircase manufacturers consider the newel system to be the backbone of the balustrade.

## Main Parts of a Newel Post

A traditional timber newel consists of several areas.

**Base** — the section fixed into the staircase. This carries structural loads. Usually hidden partially by strings or flooring.

**Shaft** — the long square section visible above the stairs. Common sizes: 90mm · 95mm · 100mm · 110mm · 120mm · larger for feature staircases.

**Cap** — the decorative top. Examples include Pyramid · Flat · Ball · Acorn · Mushroom · Turned Victorian · Contemporary square · Chamfered · Curved. Caps are usually replaceable.

## Structural Function

Newels resist several different forces:

- **Vertical compression** — weight from the handrail
- **Horizontal push** — people naturally lean on handrails. The newel resists this sideways pressure.
- **Twisting force** — corners create twisting loads. The newel absorbs these.
- **Impact loads** — people often pull themselves around corners. Again the newel provides stability.

## Common Newel Locations

A staircase rarely has just one newel. Typical locations include:

- **Bottom Newel** — located on the first step. Supports the first handrail, first balusters, starting section. Often the largest visual feature.
- **Top Newel** — located at landing level. Supports the landing handrail, stair handrail, landing balustrade.
- **Landing Newel** — used where landings change direction. Often connects multiple rails.
- **Quarter Landing Newel** — used on L-shaped stairs. Supports changing handrail directions.
- **Half Landing Newel** — used on U-shaped staircases. Usually carries rails from both flights.
- **Gallery Newel** — found around open galleries. Supports landing balustrades.
- **Balcony Newel** — supports guard rails overlooking stairwells.

## Types of Newel Posts

**Plain Square** — most popular modern style. Simple. Clean. Minimalist. Common in oak staircases.

**Stop Chamfered** — square post with chamfered corners. Traditional appearance. Popular in painted staircases.

**Turned Newel** — machined on a lathe. Often Victorian · Edwardian · Georgian. Decorative.

**Fluted Newel** — vertical grooves. Often painted. Higher-end traditional homes.

**Box Newel** — large hollow construction. Usually 150–300mm wide. Common in American staircases. Less common in UK homes.

**Feature Newel** — oversized. Often bespoke. Used as the centrepiece of grand staircases.

## Starting Newels

Starting newels receive special attention. They often combine with curved handrails · bullnose steps · curtail steps · volutes · scrolls. These become architectural focal points.

## Wall Newels

Sometimes only part of a newel is required. A wall newel fixes directly onto a wall, supports handrails, and provides visual symmetry. Not normally structural in the same way as a full newel.

## False Newels

Sometimes used purely for appearance. They hide fixings, create matching aesthetics, and are not load-bearing.

## Materials

**European Oak** — most common premium timber. Strong · durable · machines well · attractive grain · stains well · long lifespan. Common in premium homes, contemporary staircases, and traditional staircases.

**American White Oak** — lighter colour. Slightly different grain. Popular in modern interiors.

**Ash** — excellent strength. Very straight grain. Often painted.

**Beech** — smooth texture. Good machining. Often used for painted staircases.

**Pine** — economical. Easy to machine. Usually painted. Common for budget staircases.

**Sapele** — rich reddish hardwood. Traditional appearance. Less common today.

**Walnut** — luxury material. Dark brown. Premium projects. Usually feature staircases.

**Accoya** — modified timber. Highly stable. Excellent moisture resistance. Suitable for external staircases.

**Engineered Oak** — not solid throughout. Usually oak outer laminations plus engineered core. Improves stability. Reduces movement. Widely used today.

## Solid vs Laminated Newels

Modern premium manufacturers increasingly use laminated construction.

Reasons include: reduced twisting · reduced splitting · reduced cupping · better long-term stability · larger sizes possible · improved machining consistency.

**High-quality laminated oak is generally more dimensionally stable than a single piece of solid oak.**

## Grain Direction

Good manufacturers carefully orient grain. Poor grain orientation increases twisting · checking · cracking. Proper lamination allows opposing grain to reduce movement.

## Moisture Content

Most interior staircase timber is kiln dried. Typical moisture: **8–12%**.

If installed too wet: shrinkage · cracking · joint movement.
If too dry: later expansion.

Correct conditioning before manufacture is critical.

## Standard UK Sizes

Common square sizes include:

| Size | Typical Use |
|---|---|
| 70mm | Rare, light-duty applications |
| 90mm | Standard modern domestic staircase |
| 95mm | Common on many UK manufactured staircases |
| 100mm | Premium domestic |
| 110mm | Larger bespoke designs |
| 120mm | Grand staircases |
| 140mm+ | Feature staircases and commercial work |

90mm remains one of the most common domestic sizes in the UK, although many manufacturers also use 95mm depending on their system.

## Matching Components

The newel usually determines the proportions of the rest of the balustrade.

For example, a 90mm newel typically pairs with a 41mm handrail, 41mm baserail, and 41mm square balusters. Keeping these elements proportionally matched creates a balanced appearance.
