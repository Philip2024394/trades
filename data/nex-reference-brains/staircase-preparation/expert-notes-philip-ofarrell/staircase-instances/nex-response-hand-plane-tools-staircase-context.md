# Nex Response

*This image shows a traditional bench hand plane, one of the most important woodworking tools for staircase manufacturing, joinery, and on-site installation. A hand plane is used to remove thin shavings of timber, creating perfectly flat, square, smooth, and accurately fitted surfaces that power tools alone often cannot achieve.*

## The plane shown appears to be a Bailey-style smoothing plane, featuring:

- Cast iron body for stability and weight.
- Hardwood front knob for guiding the plane.
- Hardwood rear tote (handle) for pushing.
- Adjustable high-carbon steel blade (iron).
- Chip breaker to reduce tear-out.
- Adjustable mouth opening.
- Sole precision-machined flat for accurate planing.

*The plane is resting on a traditional woodworking bench surrounded by fresh wood shavings, suggesting recent hand-finishing work in a joiner's workshop.*

## Common Hand Plane Types

### Block Plane

- Length: **140–180 mm (5½–7")**
- Blade: **35–38 mm**
- Used one-handed.
- Perfect for chamfers, trimming end grain, easing edges, fitting treads and mouldings during installation.

### Smoothing Plane (No.4)

- Length: **245 mm (9¾")**
- Blade: **50 mm (2")**
- Most common finishing plane.
- Produces an extremely smooth surface before finishing.
- Ideal for staircase strings, handrails, newel posts, treads and risers.

### No.4½ Smoothing Plane

- Length: **260 mm**
- Blade: **60 mm**
- Wider blade for larger hardwood components.
- Excellent for oak, ash and walnut stair parts.

### Jack Plane (No.5)

- Length: **355 mm (14")**
- Blade: **50 mm**
- General-purpose workshop plane.
- Used to flatten boards, remove saw marks and prepare staircase timber before machining.

### Fore Plane (No.6)

- Length: **460 mm (18")**
- Used for truing long staircase strings before final finishing.

### Jointer Plane (No.7 or No.8)

- No.7: **560 mm (22")**
- No.8: **610 mm (24")**
- Used for creating perfectly straight edges.
- Essential when preparing long oak strings, landing nosings, handrails and laminations.

### Shoulder Plane

- Used for precise trimming of rebates, housed joints and tenons.
- Common when fitting housed strings and handrail joints.

### Router Plane

- Levels the bottom of housings and rebates.
- Frequently used on traditional housed-string staircases.

### Bullnose Plane

- Very short front nose allowing work right into corners.
- Excellent for staircase detailing.

## Common Blade Widths

- 25 mm (1")
- 32 mm (1¼")
- 38 mm (1½")
- 44 mm (1¾")
- **50 mm (2") – most common**
- 60 mm (2⅜")

## Typical Uses During Staircase Manufacturing

- Flattening rough-sawn oak.
- Removing machining marks.
- Straightening stringers.
- Levelling laminated handrails.
- Fine fitting treads into housed strings.
- Trimming wedges.
- Adjusting riser height.
- Fitting newel post joints.
- Cleaning glue squeeze-out.
- Fine tuning mitres.
- Breaking sharp edges before finishing.
- Preparing timber for staining, oiling or varnishing.

## Uses During Staircase Installation

A staircase installer commonly carries:

- Block plane.
- No.4 smoothing plane.
- Shoulder plane.

These are used for:

- Trimming doors after new flooring.
- Fine fitting oak treads.
- Adjusting handrail joints.
- Levelling uneven timber.
- Removing small high spots.
- Fitting skirting around stair strings.
- Adjusting newel post joints.
- Easing edges for a perfect finish.

## Typical Materials

Hand planes are suitable for virtually every timber used in staircase construction:

- European Oak
- American White Oak
- Ash
- Walnut
- Mahogany
- Beech
- Maple
- Pine
- Softwood stair components
- Engineered oak boards
- Laminated handrails

## Why Staircase Joiners Still Use Hand Planes

*Although CNC machines and thicknessers perform the bulk of the machining, hand planes remain essential because they allow adjustments of less than 0.1 mm, leaving a cleaner surface than sanding in many situations. They provide precise control when fitting bespoke staircases where every joint must close perfectly and every surface must feel smooth to the touch. Even in modern staircase workshops, a quality hand plane is considered one of the most valuable finishing tools for achieving a professional, furniture-grade result.*
