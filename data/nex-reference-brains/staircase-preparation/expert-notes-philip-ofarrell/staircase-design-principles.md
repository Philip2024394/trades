# Staircase Design Principles for Workshops and Customers

*Expert design principles by Philip O'Farrell · captured 2026-07-28 · Layer 1 evidence. What an experienced staircase designer does, when their value is highest, the details that separate good from great, and how the staircase belongs to the wider interior — including doors, skirting, flooring, and lighting.*

**Application note:** every principle is subject to Product Constitution Principle 0003 — composed judgement, never rigid rules.

**Visual reference:** [`reference-images/oak-4-panel-doors-with-oak-staircase.jpg`](reference-images/oak-4-panel-doors-with-oak-staircase.jpg) — matched 4-panel oak doors + oak staircase with square newels · square balusters · oak handrail · curved curtail starting step · flowing wood-species and grain family across the whole hallway. Excellent worked example of the *"staircase belongs to the architectural family"* principle.

## Principle A · Involve a designer at the house design stage, not near the end

The biggest mistake is waiting until the house is nearly finished. The ideal sequence:

```
House design stage
      ↓
Staircase concept
      ↓
Structural openings confirmed
      ↓
Manufacturing drawings
      ↓
Construction
      ↓
Installation
```

Staircases affect floor openings · ceiling heights · doors · lighting · walls · storage areas. A staircase designed too late becomes a compromise.

## Principle B · A designer saves money as often as they spend it

Many people assume *"a designer adds cost."* An experienced designer often reduces waste.

**Example:** customer wants a luxury oak staircase. Designer proposes:

- Oak treads (visible · touched daily)
- Oak handrail (visible · touched daily)
- Painted newels (visible but paint hides pine)
- Painted strings (mostly hidden by carpet or wall)

Result: staircase looks premium · costs materially less than *"all oak everything"*. That's design judgement, not cost cutting.

### The spend-where-it-matters rule

**Spend more on:** handrail · visible treads · newel posts · feature balustrade — anything the customer touches or sees.

**Spend less on:** hidden structure · painted components · areas nobody sees.

## Principle C · Design starts with layout, not style

A customer sees a beautiful curved staircase online and wants it. But the house may not have enough space, correct structure, or budget. **The design must start with the building.**

### The three most expensive staircase mistakes

1. **Wrong measurements** — small errors create large problems. Critical measurements: floor-to-floor height · available length · opening size · headroom · wall positions.
2. **Choosing style before layout** — style must fit the building geometry, not the other way around.
3. **Ignoring the handrail** — it's what people touch every day. A premium staircase gets its luxury feel from handrail profile · smooth sanding · timber selection · finish quality.

## Principle D · The staircase is a journey, not just steps

Great designers think of the staircase as a **journey through the house** — not a set of components. Every position along the journey is a design opportunity.

### The first step (bullnose · curtail · feature start)

Many staircases are remembered because of the starting step. Options:

- Bullnose step
- Double bullnose
- Sweeping curved start (curtail)
- Feature newel
- Open tread start
- Decorative apron

*The reference image shows exactly this — a curved curtail starting step wrapping the bottom newel.*

### The landing (often overlooked)

A landing can become a major design feature: glass overlooking the hall · feature window behind staircase · timber panelling · artwork wall · lighting feature.

### Under-stair (valuable space)

- **Storage:** pull-out drawers · cupboards · shoe storage
- **Display:** bookshelves · lighting · wine storage
- **Working space:** small office · reading area

### Wall-side detailing (where cheap staircases look unfinished)

Premium details: matching timber panels · tongue-and-groove boards · decorative mouldings · shadow gaps · hidden lighting. The staircase should look good from every angle.

## Principle E · Proportions matter as much as materials

A beautiful staircase is often about proportion, not just expensive materials. The relationship between rise (step height) and going (step depth) determines walking comfort and appearance. A staircase can be technically correct but feel uncomfortable if the proportions are wrong.

Designers balance walking comfort · appearance · available space.

## Principle F · Design for future repairs

A good staircase designer thinks 20-50 years ahead:

- Can the handrail be refinished?
- Can damaged treads be repaired?
- Are replacement parts available?
- Will the style age well?

The cheapest staircase is not always the cheapest over its lifetime.

## Principle G · The staircase belongs to the architectural family of the house

**The staircase should not be designed alone. It should belong to the architectural family of the house** — doors · skirting · architraves · flooring · wall panels · lighting all form one design language.

### Common pairings

| Door style | Matching staircase | Typical setting |
|---|---|---|
| **4-panel doors** (raised & fielded, oak or painted) | Closed string · turned newels · traditional balusters · oak handrail · decorative brackets · timber panelling | Victorian · Georgian-inspired · country houses · **see reference image** |
| **Shaker doors** (flat recessed panels, clean square lines) | Oak staircase · square newels · square balusters · glass panels · optional black metal balusters | Modern-traditional bridge · currently one of the strongest combinations |
| **Flush doors** (flat face, hidden frames) | Floating stairs · steel spine · glass balustrades · LED lighting · open risers | Modern apartments · luxury new builds |
| **Cottage doors** (vertical boards · ledged & braced · T&G) | Rustic oak · chunky handrails · natural knots · wide boards · farmhouse styling | Exposed beams · stone floors · country interiors |
| **Glazed doors** (glass panels · steel/timber frame) | Open staircases · glass balustrades · lighter woods | Light-flooded modern interiors · visual connection between rooms |
| **Victorian doors** (4 or 5 panels · deep mouldings) | Turned spindles · curved handrails · ornate newels · stained glass · dark woods | Period restorations · mahogany or dark oak |
| **Modern luxury doors** (tall · hidden frames · walnut veneer) | Sweeping · curved handrails · glass · brass details · premium hardwoods | Architect-designed houses · high-end renovations |

### Door height also matters

- **Standard doors** (normal ceiling height) — traditional · comfortable feel
- **Tall doors** (2.1m-2.4m+) — luxury · modern · larger space feel · usually needs a staircase with stronger proportions

### Door colour compositions

- **White painted doors** — works with oak stairs · painted stairs · modern interiors
- **Oak doors** — works with oak staircase · natural timber floors · warm interiors
- **Walnut / dark wood doors** — works with luxury staircases · dark handrails · premium interiors

## Principle H · Timber and finish selection are design decisions

A designer should understand that not all oak or pine is equal — grain direction · knot placement · colour variation · board width · stability · matching between components all matter. A handrail should not be chosen the same way as a hidden string.

The same staircase can look completely different depending on finish:

- **Natural oil** — shows grain · texture · warmth
- **Clear lacquer** — protection · cleaner appearance
- **Stained finish** — changes colour · character
- **Painted** — colour matching · lower timber cost acceptable

## Principle I · Modern designers combine multiple disciplines

The next generation of staircase designer will combine:

```
Traditional stair maker
      +
Interior designer
      +
3D visualisation
      +
Material expert
      +
Cost planner
```

The best staircase companies will not just sell *"a staircase"*. They will sell *"a staircase designed specifically for your home, your lifestyle, and your budget."*

## Why these nine principles matter as a set

- **A** stops NEX from suggesting design changes when the design phase has already passed.
- **B** stops NEX from equating high-spec with high-value.
- **C** stops NEX from letting style choices override building reality.
- **D** stops NEX from treating a staircase as components rather than a designed journey.
- **E** stops NEX from focusing on materials while ignoring proportion.
- **F** stops NEX from optimising for install-day cost while ignoring 30-year cost.
- **G** stops NEX from designing a staircase in isolation from the surrounding hallway.
- **H** stops NEX from treating timber species as an atomic unit — grade, cut, and finish all vary.
- **I** stops NEX from thinking a single-discipline designer is enough for modern high-value work.

A generic AI advising on staircase design will fail all nine. An experienced staircase designer holds them all in composition (Principle 0003).
