## Group A · Before you order

**A1 · What's the biggest buying mistake?**
Choosing on price alone. Better comparison: timber quality · design · warranty · installation · after-sales support · reputation · survey process. The lowest quote is rarely the best value if problems follow.

**A2 · Who takes the measurements, and what if they're wrong?**
Ask up front. If the staircase company measures, the survey error is normally their responsibility. If the customer supplies measurements, responsibility shifts. Get this in writing.

**A3 · Can I review drawings before manufacture?**
Yes — always. Approval should cover: overall width · staircase direction · number of treads · newel locations · handrail style · balustrade type. Approving drawings doesn't make the customer responsible for every technical measurement, but it's the stage that catches misunderstandings.

**A4 · Should I visit the factory or showroom?**
If possible, yes. Factory visit lets you see machinery · timber storage · sanding quality · finished staircases · attention to detail. Showroom lets you compare timber species · handrail profiles · finishes · balusters · newel posts. Seeing and touching materials makes decisions easier.

**A5 · Should I ask to see previous work?**
Yes. Good companies show completed projects · photographs · testimonials · examples of different timber species. Real work beats brochures.

**A6 · Where does the timber come from?**
Fair questions: Which species · Is it kiln dried · Is it certified from responsibly managed forests · Is it solid or engineered/laminated. Customers increasingly value origin transparency.

**A7 · The single best question to ask any staircase company:**

> *"If this staircase were going into your own home, what would you change and why?"*

The answer tells you far more than a price list. It reveals whether they're thinking about long-term quality · appearance · durability · customer satisfaction — not just completing another order.

## Group B · Design and materials

**B1 · Is oak always better than pine?** Not always. Oak = harder · more durable · premium appearance. Pine = more affordable · easier to paint · suitable for many homes. The best material depends on budget · design · finish. (See wood-intelligence Principle 2 + Principle 9.)

**B2 · Should the staircase match the flooring?** Not always. Great interiors often contrast — oak floor + painted staircase · walnut floor + oak staircase · stone floor + oak staircase. Colours and styles should complement, not necessarily match.

**B3 · Should internal doors match the staircase?** Generally yes — see `staircase-design-principles.md` Principle G. Traditional (4-panel doors + turned staircase) · Modern (Shaker doors + square newels + black balusters) · Contemporary (flush doors + glass balustrade).

**B4 · Timber or glass balustrade?** Timber = warm · traditional · easier to repair. Glass = modern · brighter · open feel. Neither is universally better — depends on the design.

**B5 · Can children safely use glass staircases?** Yes, when designed and installed to appropriate safety standards with suitable toughened or laminated glass where required.

**B6 · Should I have LED lighting?** Increasingly yes. Popular locations: under handrail · wall string · under floating treads · landing. Lighting can transform a staircase visually.

**B7 · Can I mix wood species?** Yes. Examples: oak staircase with walnut handrail · painted strings with oak treads · ash handrail with painted balusters. Mixing done thoughtfully creates distinctive designs.

**B8 · Why do two oak staircases look different?** Timber is natural. Colour · grain · knots · growth conditions vary tree-to-tree. Every board is unique — see wood-intelligence Principle 2.

**B9 · Why does the same staircase look different in my home?** Lighting changes everything — natural light · paint colours · flooring · room size all influence appearance.

**B10 · Should every tread look identical?** No. Natural timber variation is character. A good manufacturer matches boards carefully, but identical appearance is not realistic.

**B11 · Why is there filler in a knot?** Small knots or tiny defects may be professionally filled before finishing — improves appearance while maintaining structural integrity. (See wood-intelligence Principle 6.)

**B12 · Should I choose matte or gloss?** Matte is currently popular — less glare, more natural look. Gloss is a more traditional polished appearance.

**B13 · Should I carpet my staircase?** Depends on priorities. Carpet = quieter · warmer · better grip · protects timber. Exposed timber = shows natural wood · easier clean · premium appearance. Some do both — carpet runner with exposed edges.

## Group C · Cost and value

**C1 · Why do some staircases cost so much more?** Bespoke design · curved work · timber quality · hand finishing · glass · installation difficulty · project complexity. A curved staircase can take significantly more skilled labour than a straight one. (See `staircase-category-taxonomy.md` — five complexity levels.)

**C2 · Why are curved staircases expensive?** Specialist design · curved laminations · complex machining · more hand finishing · skilled installation. (Level 4-5 territory.)

**C3 · Will my staircase increase house value?** A high-quality staircase can improve appeal and first impressions. No guaranteed valuation increase, but strong first impressions matter to buyers.

**C4 · Should I buy the cheapest quote?** Compare materials · workmanship · survey quality · installation · warranty · reputation · after-sales support. Cheapest is rarely best value.

**C5 · Can I replace just the balusters (or handrail, or newel caps)?** Often yes — this is a popular renovation approach. Much less cost than replacing the whole staircase.

## Group D · Fit and delivery disputes

**D1 · What does "doesn't fit" actually mean?** Three distinct situations:

- **Manufacturing error** — staircase differs from approved drawing. Company normally corrects.
- **Measuring error** — surveyor got dimensions wrong. If company measured, they normally resolve.
- **The building changed** — builder moved a wall · floor level changed · concrete screed added · opening changed · steel beam installed later. Often requires builder+staircase-company cooperation to decide responsibility.

**D2 · Should I refuse delivery?** Usually no, unless there's an obvious major issue. If there's a problem: tell the driver/installer · note the issue · photograph it · contact the company promptly. Accepting delivery does not necessarily mean accepting that everything is correct.

**D3 · What should I photograph if there's a dispute?** Whole staircase · close-up of the issue · tape measure showing dimensions · landing · floor opening · any damaged components.

**D4 · What paperwork should I keep for a dispute?** Quotation · drawings · approval emails · invoices · delivery note. Good documentation resolves problems much faster.

**D5 · Can the company charge for a remake?** Depends on why. Company error → they normally bear the cost. Customer changes their mind ("I want it to turn the other way") → different, because the staircase may already have been manufactured to the agreed design.

**D6 · Who owns the staircase before it's paid for?** Depends on contract. Many companies include a **retention of title clause** — company retains ownership until payment is made in full, even if delivered or installed (subject to contract and applicable law). Read the quotation and terms before ordering.

**D7 · What if I don't like the colour?** Different from a fault. Ask: was the agreed species supplied · was the agreed stain/finish used · did you approve a sample beforehand. Natural timber varies — two oak staircases from the same species will not be identical (see B10).

**D8 · What if I damage the staircase after installation?** Dropping a washing machine · moving furniture · pet damage · deep scratches → normally treated as accidental damage, not manufacturing fault. Good news: timber often repairs more easily than many materials.

## Group E · Installation

**E1 · Can my old staircase be removed?** Yes. Most companies handle removal, usually quoted separately. The company checks: how the old staircase is fixed · whether walls will be affected · access.

**E2 · Will there be much dust during installation?** Some. Professional teams use dust sheets · protect flooring · vacuum regularly · remove waste. Major on-site trimming produces more dust.

**E3 · Should my flooring be installed before the staircase?** Depends on type. Tile often first. Carpet commonly after. Engineered wood depends on installation method. Staircase company and flooring contractor should coordinate.

**E4 · Who fits the spindles?** Normally the staircase installer. Glass panels may involve a specialist glazing team.

**E5 · How many people install a staircase?** Straight = usually 2 installers. Large bespoke = 3-5 depending on complexity. Curved often requires additional planning and manpower.

**E6 · Does weather affect installation?** Yes. Very damp buildings not ideal. Building should be weather-tight · reasonably dry · heated if appropriate · ventilated. Reduces moisture-related movement (see wood-intelligence Principles 7 and 8).

**E7 · Should I move into the house first?** Ideally the staircase is installed before full occupation — easier access · less risk of damaging furniture · better working conditions. Depends on project schedule.

**E8 · Can I walk on the staircase immediately?** Usually yes, unless finish is still curing · adhesive needs to set · installer says otherwise. Follow installer advice.

**E9 · Should I inspect the staircase immediately after installation?** Yes. Take your time. Check: every tread feels solid · handrail secure · balusters straight · no obvious damage · finish matches order · storage doors open correctly. Mention concerns promptly.

## Group F · Living with your staircase

**F1 · How long should a staircase last?** Decades — often much longer with care. Quality oak staircases regularly serve 100+ years. Factors: timber species · workmanship · moisture control · finish · installation · maintenance.

**F2 · Will my staircase squeak?** Not necessarily. A well-designed and installed staircase should feel solid. Squeaks can develop from timber movement · seasonal humidity · loose fixings · building movement. One occasional creak doesn't automatically mean something is wrong.

**F3 · Should I keep spare materials?** Yes if offered. One spare baluster · touch-up stain · matching paint · finish specification. Years later these make repairs much easier.

**F4 · Can I decorate before the staircase arrives?** Usually better to wait for final decorating around strings · wall panelling · skirting · architraves. Allows final adjustments first.

**F5 · What maintenance should I expect?** Timber staircase is not maintenance-free. Yearly check: handrails for looseness · squeaks · finish wear in high-traffic areas · water damage signs. Catching small issues early prevents larger repairs.

**F6 · Should I insure my staircase?** Once installed it becomes part of the home — usually covered by buildings insurance as part of the property. Check policy wording.

**F7 · What paperwork should I keep?** Quotation · survey drawings · approved design · invoices · payment receipts · warranty · care instructions · timber species · finish details · installer contact. If you sell the house, useful to the new owner.

**F8 · Should I clean with lots of water?** No. Lightly damp cloth preferred over excessive water. Standing water should be wiped promptly.

**F9 · Can I steam clean a timber staircase?** Only if the manufacturer specifically recommends it. Steam introduces heat + moisture that may not suit some finishes or products.

**F10 · Should I wax my staircase?** Only if the manufacturer recommends it. Different finishes need different care. Some staircases are designed for lacquer only · others for hardwax oil. Wrong product affects appearance and maintenance.

**F11 · How often should I re-finish timber?** Depends on traffic · finish type · sunlight · pets. Busy family staircase may need attention sooner than a holiday home.

**F12 · Should I cover the staircase during decorating?** Yes. Protect from paint · plaster · dropped tools · ladders. Prevention easier than repair.

**F13 · Will timber darken?** Many species naturally change colour over time due to light exposure and oxidation. Normal. Oak often deepens · walnut may lighten slightly.

**F14 · Does sunlight affect timber?** Yes — sunlight can change timber colour over time. Natural ageing process.

**F15 · Can I paint the staircase myself later?** Yes, provided the staircase is suitable for painting and you use products recommended for the finish already applied.

**F16 · Can pets damage staircases?** Yes — dog claws · chewing · moisture from accidents. Hardwood generally resists wear better than softwood, but no timber is completely immune.

**F17 · Can children damage staircases?** Toy impacts · dropped objects · scratches. Many small marks can be repaired during refinishing.

**F18 · Will high heels damage timber?** They can leave dents or marks, particularly on softer woods like pine. Hardwoods resist indentation better but are not damage-proof.

**F19 · Can heavy furniture be carried upstairs?** Usually yes — protect treads · nosings · handrails · walls. Professional movers use protective coverings.

**F20 · Can pets slip on polished timber?** They can. Some finishes provide more grip than others. Stair runners can improve traction.

**F21 · Can timber smell?** Yes. Fresh timber · oils · finishes have a natural smell after installation that normally fades with ventilation.

**F22 · Why is my handrail smoother than the tread?** Handrails are often sanded to a finer finish because they are constantly touched by hand — see `staircase-design-principles.md` Principle B (spend where the customer touches).

## Group G · Long-term and repair

**G1 · Can staircases be repaired?** Usually yes. Scratched treads · damaged handrails · loose spindles · worn finish. Timber is one of the easier materials to repair.

**G2 · Can a staircase be refinished instead of replaced?** Often yes. Sound structure may only need sanding · staining · new finish · updated balustrades.

**G3 · Can my staircase be made quieter?** Yes. Professional installation and correct fixing reduce movement and noise. Carpet runners reduce footfall noise.

**G4 · What if I move house?** A bespoke staircase is normally designed for one property and cannot usually be moved into another home.

**G5 · Why do oak samples look different from the finished staircase?** Small samples show only a tiny part. A full staircase includes many boards with natural variation.

**G6 · Can I install a staircase myself?** Some experienced builders can. Most bespoke staircases are best installed by experienced staircase installers because alignment · fixing · safety · finish all matter. See **Group I** for the full DIY vs professional guide.

## Group I · DIY vs professional installation

Some staircase work is suitable for a competent DIYer; other work should be left to experienced installers. A staircase is a **structural feature of the home** and safety is critical.

### The governing rule

> **If the job changes the staircase's structure or affects how people are protected from falling, it's usually worth having an experienced staircase installer do the work.**

### DIY suitability guide

| Project | DIY-friendly? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Paint staircase | ✅ Yes | Cosmetic · reversible · prep matters more than skill |
| Sand and refinish | ✅ Yes | Slow patient work · dust control important (see workshop-operations Principle D) |
| Replace newel caps | ✅ Yes | Decorative · non-structural |
| Replace balusters | ✅ Usually | Match spindle spacing · check fixings hold |
| Install stair runner | ✅ Usually | Rug-gripper installation · measure carefully |
| Replace handrail | ⚠ Moderate difficulty | Alignment · fixing · finish all matter · handrail is what people touch daily |
| Replace treads | ⚠ Advanced | Timber selection · movement · fit-to-string all matter |
| Replace newel posts | ❌ Professional recommended | Structural anchor · handrail attaches here · fall-protection critical |
| Install complete staircase | ❌ Professional recommended | Every stage from measuring to fixing to alignment demands experience |
| Install curved staircase | ❌ Specialist work | Multiple installers · heavy components · complex alignment · specialist tools |

### Even a straight staircase requires real skill

The DIY-*possible* line is not the DIY-*trivial* line. Even a straight staircase requires:

- Precise measuring
- Checking floor levels
- Fixing to the building
- Alignment
- Compliance with building regulations

Mistakes affect both **safety and fit**.

### Structural alterations always need proper advice

Enlarging stair openings · cutting joists · changing floor structures — these should only be undertaken with appropriate design and structural advice. A staircase job that turns into a structural job stops being a DIY job.

### Basic tools a DIY installer might need

Tape measure · spirit level · combination square · drill/driver · clamps · chisels · mallet · random orbital sander · quality wood glue. (Full workshop machinery landscape lives in `workshop-operations-principles.md`.)

### The skills that actually matter

Success is less about owning expensive tools, more about:

- ✓ Measuring accurately
- ✓ Working patiently
- ✓ Understanding timber movement (wood-intelligence Principle 7)
- ✓ Following installation instructions
- ✓ **Knowing when to ask for help**

### When to stop and call a professional

If any of these happen mid-job:

- The staircase moves
- Handrails are loose
- Newels are unstable
- Structural parts need replacing
- You are unsure whether the work affects safety

**Stop and get professional advice.** This is not defeat — it is exactly what an experienced trade person would recommend.
