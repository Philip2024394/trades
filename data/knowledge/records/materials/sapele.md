# Sapele

## Summary

Sapele (*Entandrophragma cylindricum*) is an African tropical hardwood in the mahogany family, prized for its rich reddish-brown colour, distinctive interlocked-grain ribbon-stripe figure, and exceptional dimensional stability once acclimatised. Historically substituted for genuine Mahogany (*Swietenia*) as CITES restrictions tightened on true Mahogany, sapele is itself now CITES Appendix II listed (November 2022), meaning every legitimate order requires CITES permit documentation from range states. FSC-certified sapele is the correct specification. Sapele suits Heritage, Contemporary, and Signature tier staircases, joinery mouldings, and interior architectural work; its interlocked grain is a distinctive design signature but demands careful machining.

## Structured Knowledge

### Species and Botanical Identity

- **Sapele** · *Entandrophragma cylindricum* · native to tropical Africa (Cameroon, Central African Republic, Democratic Republic of Congo, Gabon, Ivory Coast, Ghana, Nigeria, Uganda, Tanzania)
- Family: **Meliaceae** (mahogany family) — a genuine mahogany-family timber
- Related commercial species: **Sipo / Utile** (*E. utile*), **Kosipo** (*E. candollei*), **Tiama** (*E. angolense*) — all *Entandrophragma* species, all CITES Appendix II listed since November 2022

Sapele is a **diffuse-porous hardwood** with the mahogany-family characteristic of **interlocked grain** — successive growth rings twist in opposite directions, producing a distinctive **ribbon-stripe** figure on quarter-sawn surfaces. Flat-sawn sapele shows the classic mahogany-like grain; quarter-sawn shows the ribbon figure that is one of the material's design signatures.

Colour: freshly-milled sapele is a pale pinkish-brown that deepens on exposure to light to a rich reddish-brown or auburn. UV drift is significant — expect meaningful colour change in the first 6-12 months after installation.

### Physical Properties

- **Density (typical, air-dry):** ~640 kg/m³
- **Janka hardness:** ~1,410 lbf — harder than American Black Walnut (1,010), slightly harder than European Oak (~1,120), close to American White Oak (~1,360)
- **Modulus of Elasticity (MOE):** ~12,500 MPa — stiff, comparable to oak
- **Modulus of Rupture (MOR):** ~110 MPa — strong in bending
- **Grain:** Interlocked (the defining working characteristic) · medium texture · closed to medium-open pores
- **Natural durability (heartwood):** Class 3 (moderately durable) — better than oak-sapwood or beech but not equivalent to true teak. Suitable for exterior use with proper detailing and periodic maintenance.
- **Movement in service:** Small once acclimatised — one of sapele's premier characteristics for stable interior joinery. Fresh-milled sapele must be properly kiln-dried and acclimatised, but finished sapele is unusually stable.
- **Steam bending:** Poor — the interlocked grain prevents reliable steam-bending. For bentwood or steam-bent handrail wreaths, specify beech or ash instead.

### The Interlocked Grain Reality

Interlocked grain is sapele's defining design signature and its defining working challenge.

**Design signature:**
- **Quarter-sawn ribbon stripe** — successive dark and light bands running down the board, catching light differently as the viewer moves. A canonical Heritage and Signature tier aesthetic.
- **Chatoyance** — sapele has a subtle three-dimensional shimmer under raking light, similar to (but less pronounced than) true mahogany or figured maple.
- **Depth of colour** — the reddish-brown warms further with oil finish, producing a jewel-like surface.

**Working challenge:**
- **Tearout on the planer/thicknesser** is common; alternating grain directions mean one pass produces smooth surface, next pass at the same setting produces tearout.
- **Sharp tooling is essential** — dull cutters guarantee tearout.
- **Slow feed rates + light cuts** on machining minimise tearout.
- **Sanding requires care** — cross-grain sanding shows immediately; sand strictly along the grain, then finish with fine grit.
- **Some specifications call for specific figure selection** — the ribbon stripe is not uniform; joinery workshops select boards for figure match on visible components.

### Common Uses in the NEX Trades

**Staircase (Connected Staircase™ contexts):**
- **Treads on Heritage and Signature tier specifications** — sapele's warm reddish-brown suits traditional and premium interiors particularly where a mahogany-family aesthetic is desired.
- **Handrails** — the rich colour and warm feel under hand make sapele a classical handrail timber. See `components_stair_handrails_v1`.
- **Newels** — solid or veneered sapele newels, particularly turned newels where the interlocked grain adds visual interest. See `components_newel_posts_v1`.
- **Balusters** — turned sapele balusters for Heritage specifications where the mahogany-family colour is the design intent.

**Furniture and joinery:**
- **Traditional dining tables, sideboards, cabinets** — sapele is the preferred contemporary substitute for genuine mahogany in reproduction Georgian, Regency, and Victorian furniture.
- **Interior mouldings** — architraves, skirtings, dado rails, panelling in premium interiors.
- **Doors** — solid or veneered sapele doors, particularly panel doors in Heritage or Traditional specifications.

**Marine and specialist:**
- **Boat interiors** — sapele's dimensional stability and moderate durability suit fitted interiors in marine specifications.
- **Musical instruments** — guitar backs and sides (a distinctive tone with the interlocked grain).

**Windows (in some UK trade practice):**
- Solid sapele has been used for exterior joinery windows and doors given its Class 3 durability, but treated Accoya or engineered timber has largely displaced sapele for new UK exterior joinery specifications. Sapele remains legitimate where a genuine hardwood exterior joinery is required.

### Working Characteristics

- **Machining:** Requires sharp tooling and light cuts due to interlocked grain. When correct, produces excellent surface. When incorrect, tearout is severe.
- **Turning:** Good — the interlocked grain adds visual interest to turned components.
- **Steam bending:** Poor (interlocked grain).
- **Gluing:** Good. Sapele accepts PVA, polyurethane, and epoxy adhesives reliably. The closed to medium pores accept glue evenly.
- **Fixings:** Screws hold well; pre-drill for large fixings due to hardness. Nails hold moderately well.
- **Sanding:** Sand strictly along the grain; cross-grain sanding shows immediately. Fine finishing grits (240+) required for a Signature-quality finish.
- **Finishes:** Accepts oil, wax, lacquer, and shellac excellently. The reddish-brown deepens under oil finish. Traditional French polish suits sapele particularly well for reproduction furniture.
- **Dust:** Sapele dust is IARC Group 1 carcinogenic (as are all hardwood dusts). See `processes_dust_extraction` (to be authored). Sapele dust can also cause skin sensitisation in some workers.

### Sourcing and Sustainability

**CITES Appendix II status (2022 update):** In November 2022 at CoP19 (CITES Conference of the Parties), all *Entandrophragma* species — sapele, sipo/utile, kosipo, tiama — were added to CITES Appendix II. This means:

- **Every international export from a range state requires a CITES export permit.**
- **Import into destination countries (including the UK) requires the accompanying CITES documentation.**
- **The intent is not to prohibit trade** but to make the trade traceable and to prevent illegal harvesting.
- **FSC-certified sapele remains available** and is the correct specification for NEX purposes — FSC certification includes chain-of-custody traceability that satisfies CITES documentation requirements.

**What this means for a project:**
- Specify FSC-certified sapele.
- Ask the supplier for CITES documentation on delivery.
- Accept that supply may become slightly more expensive and slightly slower as documentation flows through the chain.
- Legitimate sapele is not going away in the short term, but the supply chain is more formalised than previously.

**Comparison with true mahogany:**
- **Genuine Mahogany** (*Swietenia macrophylla*) has been CITES Appendix II since 2003. Sapele was substituted for mahogany as mahogany trade was restricted. Now sapele is itself listed. The market is likely to shift again — possibly toward other tropical hardwoods (afromosia, iroko), possibly toward temperate substitutes (dark-stained oak, walnut), possibly toward engineered/veneered specifications.
- **In NEX terms**, sapele is a legitimate specification for Heritage and Signature tier where the mahogany-family aesthetic is intended, provided documentation and certification are in order.

**FSC and PEFC certified sapele:** Widely available from responsible suppliers. This is the correct specification. Ask for chain-of-custody certification numbers on delivery.

**Reclaimed sapele:** Occasionally available from demolished commercial or civic interiors. Strong sustainability profile and no CITES paperwork.

### Kiln Drying and Moisture Content

- **Target moisture content for interior joinery (UK):** 8-12%
- **Target moisture content for interior joinery adjacent to central heating:** 6-9%
- **Kiln schedule:** Sapele is prone to surface checking if dried too fast; a moderate kiln schedule with careful conditioning is standard. Interlocked grain adds complexity to drying — sapele can develop residual internal stress that manifests as movement after machining if the kiln schedule was aggressive.
- **Verification on delivery:** Confirm moisture content with a resistance or capacitance meter per `processes_moisture_content_verification_v1`.
- **Acclimatisation on site:** Sapele must be acclimatised in the installation environment for at least 5-7 days before final machining and fitting.

### Regulations and Compliance (UK)

- **Approved Document K** applies to any sapele component in a UK staircase. See `regulations_approved_document_k_v1`.
- **CITES Regulations** (retained EU CITES Regulation 338/97 as amended for the UK · UK CITES Wildlife Trade Regulations) apply to import and internal trade of sapele. Suppliers must hold and produce documentation.
- **Fire behaviour:** Untreated sapele has Euroclass D-s2, d0 typical for hardwoods. Fire-retardant treatment available for higher classifications.
- **UK Timber Regulations (retained EUTR)** apply to placing timber on the UK market — due diligence documentation required from suppliers.

## Advantages

- **Distinctive ribbon-stripe figure** — a design signature no temperate hardwood matches.
- **Rich reddish-brown colour** deepening beautifully with oil finish — Heritage and Signature tier warmth.
- **High dimensional stability** once acclimatised — one of the more stable hardwoods for interior joinery.
- **Moderate durability (Class 3)** — suitable for exterior joinery with proper detailing.
- **Hard-wearing on stair treads** (Janka ~1,410 lbf).
- **Excellent gluing and finishing** — accepts French polish, oil, lacquer, wax.
- **Legitimate mahogany-family aesthetic** — the correct contemporary substitute for genuine mahogany in reproduction work.

## Considerations

- **CITES Appendix II listing (November 2022)** — supply chain now requires permit documentation. FSC certification is the practical solution.
- **Interlocked grain** — requires sharp tooling, light cuts, and careful sanding. Tearout on the planer is common with dull cutters.
- **Poor steam-bending** — not suitable for bentwood or steam-bent handrail wreaths (specify beech or ash instead).
- **Significant UV colour drift** — expect deepening in the first 6-12 months after installation.
- **Cost is higher than commodity oak** — reflects both the timber quality and the CITES-documented supply chain.
- **Some workers experience skin sensitisation** from sapele dust — PPE requirements are the standard hardwood-dust protections.

## Common Mistakes

- **Specifying non-FSC sapele** — undocumented supply is neither legal nor ethical under CITES.
- **Skipping the CITES documentation check** — ask the supplier for the certificate on delivery.
- **Aggressive planing/thicknessing** — tearout is guaranteed with dull tools or heavy cuts.
- **Cross-grain sanding** — visible immediately, hard to correct.
- **Confusing sapele with genuine mahogany** — related family, but distinct species with distinct commercial and regulatory profiles.
- **Specifying sapele for steam-bent components** — the interlocked grain prevents reliable steam bending.
- **Installing at high moisture content** — sapele acclimatisation must be respected; installing at 15% MC when equilibrium will be 10% guarantees movement.

## Setup and Machining Notes

- Sharp tooling essential — carbide-tipped preferred for volume production.
- Feed rate slower than for oak; lighter cuts on the planer/thicknesser.
- Scraper or fine-cut hand plane finishes better than heavy planing for feature surfaces.
- Sanding: strictly along the grain; progress through fine grits (120 → 180 → 240 → 320 for Signature finish).
- Predrill for all fixings due to density.

## Maintenance (finished components)

- **Oil-finished treads and handrails:** re-oil annually. The reddish-brown deepens further with each re-oiling for the first several cycles.
- **Lacquer-finished:** wipe clean; refinishing requires sanding to bare timber and re-application.
- **French polish (reproduction furniture):** professional French polisher maintenance; sensitive to moisture and alcohol.
- **Wax-finished heritage components:** re-wax every 12-24 months.

## Canonical Q&A (Auto-Generated From Structured Knowledge · Constitutional Clause 7)

**Q1 · What is sapele?**
A: A tropical African hardwood in the mahogany family (*Entandrophragma cylindricum*), known for its rich reddish-brown colour and distinctive quarter-sawn ribbon-stripe figure. Historically substituted for genuine mahogany once mahogany was CITES-restricted; sapele itself was added to CITES Appendix II in November 2022.

**Q2 · Is sapele the same as mahogany?**
A: Same botanical family (Meliaceae), different genus and species. Genuine mahogany is *Swietenia macrophylla*; sapele is *Entandrophragma cylindricum*. Both are CITES Appendix II listed. Sapele is the correct contemporary substitute for genuine mahogany aesthetics in premium joinery.

**Q3 · Is sapele legal to buy in the UK?**
A: Yes, when accompanied by CITES documentation and ideally FSC certification. FSC-certified sapele is widely available. The CITES Appendix II listing (November 2022) means every order requires permit documentation flowing through the supply chain — it does not prohibit trade.

**Q4 · What does the ribbon stripe look like?**
A: When sapele is quarter-sawn, the interlocked grain shows as alternating dark and light bands running down the board. Under raking light, the bands catch light differently as the viewer moves — a subtle three-dimensional shimmer. It is one of sapele's defining design signatures.

**Q5 · How hard is sapele?**
A: Approximately 1,410 lbf on the Janka scale — harder than American Black Walnut (1,010), slightly harder than European Oak (~1,120), close to American White Oak (~1,360). Well-suited to stair treads and high-touch surfaces.

**Q6 · Can sapele be used for stair treads?**
A: Yes — a legitimate Heritage or Signature tier specification. Sapele's hardness, rich colour, and dimensional stability suit stair treads well. The interlocked grain adds visual interest.

**Q7 · Can sapele be steam-bent for a curved handrail wreath?**
A: No — the interlocked grain makes steam-bending unreliable. For steam-bent components, specify beech or ash instead. Sapele suits straight-run handrails, not steam-bent sections.

**Q8 · How does sapele compare to walnut for a Signature tier staircase?**
A: Walnut is chocolate-brown, walnut lightens slightly under UV. Sapele is reddish-brown, deepens under UV. Sapele is harder (~1,410 vs 1,010 Janka). Sapele has interlocked ribbon-stripe figure; walnut has more consistent grain. Both are legitimate Signature tier specifications; the choice is aesthetic (chocolate vs reddish) and cost (sapele often marginally lower).

**Q9 · What does the CITES Appendix II listing mean for my project?**
A: Specify FSC-certified sapele, ask the supplier for CITES documentation on delivery, expect that the supply chain is now more formalised (slightly slower, slightly more expensive), and understand that legitimate sapele remains available.

**Q10 · Does sapele change colour after installation?**
A: Yes — significant UV-driven colour deepening in the first 6-12 months. The freshly-installed pinkish-brown warms to the mature reddish-brown. Expect the change and design around the final colour, not the fresh colour.

**Q11 · Can sapele be used outdoors?**
A: Yes, with proper detailing and maintenance. Class 3 (moderately durable) heartwood suits exterior joinery windows and doors when detailing sheds water, joints are well-designed, and periodic maintenance is scheduled. For pure exterior applications, treated Accoya has largely displaced sapele in new UK specifications, but sapele remains legitimate.

**Q12 · Is sapele susceptible to movement in service?**
A: Once properly acclimatised, no — sapele is one of the more dimensionally stable hardwoods for interior joinery. Acclimatisation on site (5-7 days minimum) is essential; installation at high moisture content will produce movement.

**Q13 · What finish suits sapele best?**
A: Oil finish is canonical — the reddish-brown deepens with each oiling cycle. Traditional French polish suits reproduction furniture. Lacquer suits high-durability specifications. Wax finish for Heritage restoration. All finishes accept sapele well.

**Q14 · Are there any health concerns machining sapele?**
A: Yes. Sapele dust is IARC Group 1 carcinogenic like all hardwood dusts — dust extraction and appropriate PPE required. Some workers experience skin sensitisation specifically to sapele; sensitised workers must wear protective clothing.

**Q15 · What's the difference between sapele and sipo (utile)?**
A: Both are *Entandrophragma* species from tropical Africa. Sapele (*E. cylindricum*) has stronger ribbon figure and is slightly harder. Sipo/utile (*E. utile*) has plainer grain, slightly better dimensional stability. Both are CITES Appendix II. Sapele is the more visually distinctive; sipo is preferred where a plainer surface is desired.

**Q16 · Should I be worried about buying sapele given the CITES listing?**
A: No — when specified with FSC certification and appropriate CITES documentation. Worry is warranted for undocumented, non-certified supply. The CITES listing exists precisely so legitimate trade can continue while illegal harvesting is prevented. FSC-certified sapele is ethical and legal.

**Q17 · Can sapele be used for balusters?**
A: Yes — turned sapele balusters suit Heritage tier specifications where the mahogany-family colour is the design intent. See `components_stair_balusters` (to be authored) for baluster options and materials.

**Q18 · How does sapele compare to teak?**
A: Sapele has better ribbon figure but poorer durability (Class 3 vs Class 1 teak). Teak's oiliness makes it uniquely suited to marine and exterior applications; sapele is primarily an interior premium timber. Costs and CITES status are broadly comparable.

**Q19 · Why is machining sapele tricky?**
A: Interlocked grain means successive layers twist in opposite directions. A planer cutter travelling across the board will cut with the grain in one section, then against it in the next — producing tearout in the against-grain section. Sharp tools, light cuts, and slow feed rates mitigate this.

**Q20 · What if my joiner cannot get sapele — what do I specify instead?**
A: For chocolate-brown warmth: American Black Walnut. For deep colour with ribbon figure: figured or dark-stained American White Oak. For sustainability priority: reclaimed European Oak. For traditional-mahogany-substitute where CITES paperwork is problematic: iroko or afrormosia (each with its own considerations).
