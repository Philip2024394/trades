# American White Oak

## Summary

American White Oak (Quercus alba) is a premium pale-brown North American hardwood widely used across staircase treads, kitchen cabinet doors, flooring, doors, and cooperage. Janka hardness approximately 1,360 lbf — harder than walnut, one of the hardest common domestic hardwoods. Prominent medullary ray fleck when quarter-sawn. Takes stain, oil, lacquer, and paint well. Premium cost band.

---

## Structured Knowledge

### What American White Oak Is

American White Oak (Quercus alba, family Fagaceae) is a domestic North American hardwood prized for its strength, hardness, workability, and distinctive figure. It is one of the most commercially important hardwoods in the United States and remains a first-choice specification for premium interior joinery, flooring, doors, cooperage (whisky and wine barrel staves), and structural millwork.

The tree is native to the eastern and central United States, with commercial timber sourced across the Central and Appalachian Hardwood Regions. It is not related to European Oak (Quercus robur and Q. petraea), although the two are sometimes used interchangeably. European Oak has a similar general appearance but tends to be slightly softer, with different growth-ring patterns and less prominent medullary ray fleck when quarter-sawn.

Within NEX trade specifications, American White Oak appears across the Classic, Heritage, Contemporary, and Signature tiers of the Connected Staircase™ family, and in the standard specifications for the corresponding Kitchen, Flooring, and Door tiers. It is the single most common premium hardwood specified across NEX trades.

### Botanical and Geographic Origin

- Botanical name: *Quercus alba*
- Family: Fagaceae (beech family)
- Native range: Eastern and central United States (Central and Appalachian Hardwood Regions)
- Growth: Deciduous tree, typically 25-30 metres at maturity, growing on a wide range of soils but preferring deep, well-drained loams
- Sustainability: Commercially abundant; FSC/PEFC certified stock is widely available.

### Physical Properties

- Janka hardness: approximately 1,360 lbf (6.0 kN)
- Density (kiln-dried): approximately 750 kg/m³ (47 lb/ft³)
- Modulus of rupture: approximately 105 MPa
- Modulus of elasticity: approximately 12.3 GPa
- Shrinkage: moderate; radial approximately 5.6%, tangential approximately 10.5% (green to oven-dry)
- Dimensional stability: good once properly kiln-dried and acclimatised, though radial-to-tangential shrinkage ratio is somewhat higher than walnut

American White Oak is notably harder than American Black Walnut (1,360 lbf vs 1,010 lbf) and denser (750 vs 610 kg/m³). Its hardness makes it particularly suitable for staircase treads under heavy domestic and light commercial use, and for flooring in high-traffic areas.

### Distinctive Feature · Closed Grain and Tyloses

A defining feature of American White Oak that separates it from Red Oak (Quercus rubra and related species) is the presence of **tyloses** — cellular structures that block the vessels in the heartwood. This closed-grain structure makes American White Oak effectively waterproof along the grain, which is why it is the traditional timber for whisky and wine barrel staves and for boat building.

Practical consequence for trades: American White Oak resists liquid penetration far better than Red Oak. It can be finished with lower-viscosity finishes without excessive absorption into open vessels, and it survives modest wet-area exposure better than Red Oak (though it is not classified as an exterior-durable timber by modern standards).

### Moisture Content and Kiln Drying

For interior joinery use in the UK, kiln-dried American White Oak is typically supplied at 8-12% moisture content, matching the range for other premium hardwoods. For cabinetry and premium furniture, 6-10% is more common. Because oak's tangential-to-radial shrinkage ratio is higher than walnut's, careful attention to grain orientation (quarter-sawn vs plain-sawn) matters more for stability.

Quarter-sawn oak is particularly stable dimensionally and displays the characteristic silver ray fleck that many premium specifications call for. Plain-sawn oak displays the classic cathedral-arch grain figure and is less stable but more visually varied.

### Working Characteristics

American White Oak is harder to machine than walnut but has excellent working properties overall.

**Machining:** cuts and machines well with sharp carbide tooling. Requires slightly more power than walnut due to its higher density. CNC routing produces clean edges with proper feed rates. Cross-grain machining can produce tearout in areas of interlocked grain (occasionally present); climb-cutting or scraper planes reduce this.

**Gluing:** glues well with standard PVA and polyurethane adhesives. Slightly acidic sap can affect some glue formulations; standard interior-grade adhesives are unaffected.

**Sanding:** sands to a very smooth finish with progressive grits. Fills easily where a full-grain-filled finish is required (for example, for high-gloss lacquer). If left unfilled, the open pores show through, producing the classic textured oak look.

**Fastening:** takes screws and nails well. Steel fasteners can react with the tannins in oak, causing black iron-tannin staining if moisture is present; stainless steel or brass fasteners avoid this. Pre-drilling recommended near end grain and in thin sections.

**Bending:** American White Oak steam-bends well, which is why it is traditionally used for curved rails, chair backs, and shipbuilding. Steam bending is a specialist process (see Processes cluster).

**Health note:** oak dust is a known respiratory irritant and a recognised carcinogen classified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer. Standard dust extraction and PPE are essential.

### Aesthetic Characteristics

**Heartwood:** pale to light brown, sometimes with olive-brown undertones. Colour is uniform but less saturated than walnut.
**Sapwood:** distinctly paler than heartwood, often cream to pale yellow. Sapwood is typically avoided in premium visible work or the piece is arranged so the sapwood is hidden.
**Grain:** generally straight, occasionally interlocked. Coarse texture with prominent open pores in plain-sawn stock.
**Figure:** the distinctive **medullary ray fleck** is the most prized figure in quarter-sawn American White Oak. Silver, ribbon-like flecks appear across the face, producing a look impossible to fake with veneer print. Plain-sawn oak displays a cathedral-arch figure characteristic of ring-porous hardwoods.
**Patina:** oak darkens slightly over time under UV exposure, moving toward a warmer honey-brown. Unlike walnut (which lightens), oak deepens. Interior specifications should account for this drift.
**Ammonia fuming:** oak can be fumed with ammonia vapour to produce dramatically darker colour (the Stickley Craftsman finish is the historical example). This is a specialist finish rarely applied today.

### Applications Across NEX Trades

American White Oak serves the same cross-domain foundation role as walnut but at higher volume — it is specified more frequently across Classic and Heritage tiers because of its lower cost and higher hardness.

**Staircase applications:**
- Signature, Heritage, and Contemporary tier stair treads
- Handrails (solid or laminated for curved work; steam-bent for tight radii)
- Newel posts and newel caps
- String cappings on painted-shell staircases (per D1: selective material placement)
- Balusters (turned or square)
- NEX Premium™ specifications where oak is the specification of choice

**Kitchen applications:**
- Cabinet door faces (shaker style especially · solid or veneered)
- End panels and plinth
- Feature open shelving
- Kitchen island tops (with appropriate finish and use limitations; oak is a traditional choice for kitchen worktops with regular oiling)
- Butcher-block worktops
- Handle overlays and pull rails

**Flooring applications:**
- Premium engineered or solid plank flooring (one of the most common flooring choices generally)
- Character-grade flooring with knot and colour variation (rustic specifications)
- Herringbone and chevron patterns
- Wide-plank Signature specifications

**Doors applications:**
- Raised and fielded panel doors (interior and exterior with appropriate treatment)
- Solid stile-and-rail construction
- Feature entrance doors (exterior with regular maintenance — oak has better outdoor durability than most furniture-grade hardwoods but is not equal to species such as Iroko or Sapele)
- Veneered flush doors for premium specifications
- Historically the primary timber for external doors in traditional UK construction

**Cooperage and specialty:**
- Whisky and wine barrel staves (the tyloses in the heartwood provide the closed-grain structure needed to contain liquid)
- Ecclesiastical joinery and church furniture (historically the premier timber)
- Shipbuilding (historically)

### Finishes That Work Well

American White Oak takes an exceptionally broad range of finishes.

- **Oil finishes** (Danish oil, Osmo Polyx, hardwax oil): enhance the grain and produce a natural feel; classic for Signature tier flooring and treads.
- **UV-cured lacquer**: factory-applied, durable, low-VOC in cured state. Standard for volume-produced oak flooring.
- **Traditional lacquer**: compatible; less durable than UV-cured but easier to spot-repair.
- **Stains**: oak takes stain exceptionally well. From natural clear to dark walnut-look stains, oak accepts a wide range of colour treatments. This is a major differentiator from walnut, which is rarely stained.
- **Grain-filler + high-gloss**: filled and polished oak produces a mirror-finish effect for luxury applications; the grain fill is essential to achieve full smoothness.
- **Limed / white-wash finishes**: driving lime or white pigment into the open pores produces the popular Scandinavian-style limed oak look.
- **Fumed finishes** (ammonia): historical Craftsman-style dark oak.
- **Painted finishes**: oak takes paint well when properly grain-filled. Painted-shell staircases with visible oak treads are a canonical D1 (selective material placement) form.

**Iron-tannin staining warning:** oak's high tannin content reacts with iron in the presence of moisture, producing black or blue-black stains. Iron oxide-based stains and iron fasteners can produce this reaction accidentally. Stainless steel or brass fasteners avoid the risk; deliberate ammonia-and-iron reactions are sometimes used to produce controlled colouring.

### Environments and Uses to Avoid

American White Oak is more versatile than walnut but still has limits:

- **Prolonged exterior use without maintenance.** Oak is more durable outdoors than walnut but not equal to species such as Iroko or Sapele. Exterior oak requires regular finishing.
- **Contact with iron fasteners in damp conditions.** Iron-tannin reaction produces black staining.
- **High-humidity environments.** Oak's higher tangential shrinkage means humidity fluctuations affect it more than walnut. Bathroom and kitchen installations should target stable indoor humidity.
- **Direct wet-area use.** Not recommended for shower floors or bathroom flooring below splash zones.

### Cost Band and Grades

American White Oak sits in the **premium** cost band within the NEX materials taxonomy, typically slightly less expensive than American Black Walnut. It is more expensive than pine, ash, or beech, and less expensive than figured woods (curly maple, quilted walnut, burl).

Common NHLA grading (per US National Hardwood Lumber Association):
- **FAS (Firsts and Seconds)**: highest grade. Preferred for visible premium work.
- **Selects**: clear on one face. Common for cabinet work and hidden faces.
- **No. 1 Common**: shorter clear cuttings. Used for smaller components.
- **No. 2 Common / Character grade**: knots and colour variation. Used for character-grade flooring and rustic specifications.
- **Rift and Quartered**: specialist grade where sawing method rather than defect content is specified. Displays the medullary ray fleck.

### Comparison with European Oak

American White Oak and European Oak (Quercus robur / Q. petraea) are frequently used for similar applications, but they are not identical:

- **Colour**: American White Oak tends toward pale honey-brown; European Oak often shows more variation with olive-brown tones.
- **Grain**: European Oak often has more visible knots and pips; American White Oak is generally cleaner.
- **Rays**: American White Oak's medullary rays are larger and more prominent when quarter-sawn; European Oak's rays are shorter.
- **Density and hardness**: broadly similar; American White Oak is marginally harder on average.
- **Origin**: American White Oak from North America; European Oak from Britain, France, Germany, Eastern Europe.
- **Cost**: comparable, with European Oak sometimes commanding a premium in UK markets due to local specification bias.

For specifications where the specific ray fleck of quarter-sawn oak is required, American White Oak is often the specification of choice. For a slightly softer, more variegated aesthetic with regional provenance, European Oak may be preferred.

### Sustainability

American White Oak is commercially abundant and sustainably managed across the North American Hardwood Region. FSC and PEFC certified stock is widely available. The species is not endangered and is not subject to CITES restrictions.

## Advantages

- **Excellent hardness** for domestic and light commercial use — significantly harder than walnut.
- **Closed-grain heartwood** (tyloses) provides better water resistance than Red Oak.
- **Exceptionally broad finish compatibility** — takes stain, oil, lacquer, paint, wax, fume, lime, and grain-fill treatments well.
- **Distinctive quarter-sawn ray fleck** — a genuine premium figure impossible to fake with veneer print.
- **Well suited for steam bending** — traditional timber for curved work.
- **Widely available** in the UK from importers of North American hardwoods.
- **Certified sustainable stock available** — FSC/PEFC.
- **Cross-domain applicability** — canonical material for staircases, kitchens, flooring, doors, and cooperage.
- **Cost-effective premium material** — the perceived-value-to-cost ratio is one of the strongest in the hardwood market.

## Considerations

- **Iron-tannin staining risk.** Contact with iron in the presence of moisture produces black staining. Stainless or brass fasteners recommended.
- **Higher tangential shrinkage** than walnut — humidity control matters more.
- **Open pores** in plain-sawn stock — some finishes (mirror-polish lacquer) require grain filling.
- **Oak dust is a recognised carcinogen** by IARC — dust extraction and PPE are essential.
- **Slightly harder to machine** than walnut due to higher density; tool wear is faster.
- **Sapwood is distinctly paler** than heartwood — arrange carefully or specify all-heartwood.

## Common Mistakes

- **Substituting Red Oak (Quercus rubra) for White Oak.** Red Oak lacks tyloses, has more open pores, and is less water-resistant. Not a direct substitute where oak's characteristic properties are needed.
- **Using iron nails or screws in damp oak.** Produces black iron-tannin staining.
- **Applying a mirror-gloss finish without grain filling.** Open pores produce a stippled effect that ruins the mirror-gloss result.
- **Specifying "oak" without noting quarter-sawn.** Plain-sawn oak does not display the ray fleck; if the specification requires the ray fleck aesthetic, "quarter-sawn" must be explicit.
- **Under-specifying grade.** For visible premium work, calling for "oak" without an NHLA grade specification can lead to Common #1 stock being supplied where FAS was intended.
- **Ignoring the colour drift.** Oak darkens over time under UV. Interior schemes should account for this if colour matching is critical.
- **Insufficient dust extraction.** Oak dust is a known carcinogen; skimping on extraction is a health-and-safety violation, not a workshop preference.

## Sourcing and Working Notes

- **Sourcing.** UK importers of North American hardwoods typically stock American White Oak in FAS, Selects, and Rift and Quartered grades. Certified FSC/PEFC stock should be specified for Signature tier and NEX Premium™ work.
- **Grade specification.** For visible premium work, specify FAS or Rift and Quartered. For character grades, specify No. 1 Common or Character grade.
- **Kiln drying and acclimatisation.** 8-12% moisture content for UK interior joinery. Acclimatise 1-2 weeks in the working environment before machining premium pieces.
- **Fasteners.** Use stainless steel or brass in damp conditions to avoid iron-tannin staining.
- **Cutting.** Sharp carbide tooling. Extract dust — oak dust is a known carcinogen.
- **Steam bending.** Air-dried stock steam-bends better than kiln-dried. Specialist process; see Processes cluster.
- **Grain filling** (where required for mirror finishes): fill oak pores before final finishing to avoid stippled surface effect.

## Maintenance

- **Oil finishes:** re-oil approximately every 1-3 years depending on use.
- **Lacquer finishes:** clean with a soft dry or slightly-damp cloth. Avoid abrasive cleaners. Refinish if surface wear becomes visible.
- **Waxes:** re-wax annually where used.
- **Environmental:** maintain stable indoor humidity (approximately 40-55% relative humidity). Oak moves more with humidity change than walnut.
- **Iron staining recovery:** oxalic acid can lighten iron-tannin stains where they have occurred; specialist knowledge required.

## Concepts

### Industry Knowledge

The following terms and mechanisms are established industry practice, not NEX-specific. They are referenced by this record but not owned by it. Most terms overlap with the walnut record; species-specific concepts are added.

- **Janka hardness** — a standard test measuring the force required to embed a steel ball to half its diameter into a wood sample.
- **Kiln drying** — the controlled drying of timber in a kiln to reduce moisture content to a specified target.
- **Moisture content (MC)** — the mass of water in a piece of timber expressed as a percentage of oven-dry mass.
- **Tyloses** — cellular structures that block vessels in the heartwood of some oak species, producing closed-grain water-resistant timber.
- **Medullary rays** — radial cellular tissue running from the pith outward; produce the distinctive ray fleck in quarter-sawn oak.
- **Quarter-sawn** — a sawing method that cuts across the growth rings at roughly 90°, producing straight grain and displaying medullary ray fleck in oak.
- **Plain-sawn** — a sawing method that cuts tangentially to growth rings; produces cathedral-arch figure and higher yield.
- **Rift-sawn** — a specialist sawing between quarter-sawn and plain-sawn; straight grain without prominent ray fleck.
- **Ring-porous hardwood** — a hardwood in which large early-wood vessels form a distinct ring around each annual growth ring; oak is the archetypal ring-porous hardwood.
- **NHLA grading** — the NHLA grading system for North American hardwoods.
- **Rift and Quartered grade** — an NHLA specification calling for rift-sawn and quarter-sawn stock without prominent ray fleck.
- **Iron-tannin staining** — the black or blue-black discolouration caused by reaction between iron and tannin (present in oak) in the presence of moisture.
- **Ammonia fuming** — the historical process of exposing oak to ammonia vapour to produce dramatically darker colour.
- **Cooperage** — the craft of making barrels and casks; American White Oak is the primary timber.
- **BS EN 942** — British Standard for timber in joinery.
- **IARC classification** — International Agency for Research on Cancer; oak dust classified as a Group 1 carcinogen.

### NEX Concepts

The following are NEX-proprietary concepts that reference this material record. Most are shared with the walnut record because oak and walnut are frequently alternative specifications for the same NEX tier.

- **Connected Staircase™ Classic, Heritage, Contemporary, and Signature tier oak specification** — oak appears across more tiers than walnut because of its lower cost and higher hardness.
- **NEX Premium™ oak specification** — the presentation layer specifies FAS-grade FSC-certified American White Oak with an appropriate premium finish (usually UV-cured lacquer or premium oil).
- **NEX cost band: premium** — American White Oak sits in the premium cost band alongside walnut and European Oak.
- **D1 (Selective Material Placement)** — the doctrine of concentrating premium materials where seen and touched. Oak is a canonical D1 material because it is often specified for visible treads, handrails, and cabinet fronts while the shell remains softer/painted material.

## Canonical Q&A (Composed From Structured Knowledge · Constitutional Clause 7)

The following Q&A pairs are grounded in the Structured Knowledge above. Composed by Research Claude from the source knowledge in this record. Every answer traces to a claim or structured section within this record.

**Q1 · What is American White Oak?**
A: American White Oak is a premium North American hardwood (Quercus alba) with pale to light-brown heartwood and distinctive medullary ray fleck when quarter-sawn. It is widely used for staircase treads, kitchen cabinets, flooring, doors, and cooperage.

**Q2 · Where does American White Oak come from?**
A: American White Oak is native to the eastern and central United States. Commercial timber is sourced across the Central and Appalachian Hardwood Regions.

**Q3 · How does oak compare to walnut for hardness?**
A: American White Oak is significantly harder than American Black Walnut. Oak's Janka hardness is approximately 1,360 lbf; walnut is approximately 1,010 lbf. Oak is denser too (750 vs 610 kg/m³). This makes oak particularly suitable for staircase treads and high-traffic flooring where hardness matters.

**Q4 · Is American White Oak the same as European Oak?**
A: They are related but not identical. American White Oak (Quercus alba) has larger medullary ray fleck when quarter-sawn and is generally cleaner in grain. European Oak (Quercus robur or Q. petraea) tends to show more variation, sometimes with more visible knots and pips. Density and hardness are broadly similar. For a specification requiring the prominent quarter-sawn ray fleck, American White Oak is often preferred; for regional provenance and slightly more variegated aesthetic, European Oak may be preferred.

**Q5 · Is American White Oak the same as Red Oak?**
A: No. Red Oak (Quercus rubra and related species) lacks the tyloses that make White Oak heartwood effectively waterproof. Red Oak is more porous and less water-resistant. The two are not interchangeable where water resistance or closed-grain performance is required, which is why White Oak (not Red Oak) is the traditional timber for whisky and wine barrels.

**Q6 · What are tyloses and why do they matter?**
A: Tyloses are cellular structures that block the vessels in the heartwood of American White Oak. They make the heartwood effectively waterproof along the grain, which is why oak is used for barrel staves that hold liquid. For construction, tyloses give oak better water resistance than Red Oak and allow lower-viscosity finishes without excessive absorption.

**Q7 · Can I use oak for stair treads?**
A: Yes. American White Oak is one of the most commonly-specified hardwoods for premium stair treads. Its hardness makes it suitable for heavy domestic use and light commercial applications.

**Q8 · Can I use oak for kitchen cabinets?**
A: Yes. Oak is a canonical kitchen cabinet material — solid or veneered. It is particularly common in Shaker-style cabinetry and traditional kitchen designs.

**Q9 · Can I use oak for kitchen worktops?**
A: Yes. Oak butcher-block worktops are a traditional choice. They require regular oiling to maintain their appearance and moderate wet-use tolerance.

**Q10 · Can I use oak for flooring?**
A: Yes. American White Oak is one of the most commonly-specified flooring hardwoods. Available as solid planks, engineered flooring, herringbone, chevron, and wide-plank specifications.

**Q11 · Can I use oak for exterior doors?**
A: Yes, with appropriate treatment. Oak has better outdoor durability than most furniture-grade hardwoods but is not equal to species like Iroko or Sapele. Exterior oak requires regular finishing and maintenance.

**Q12 · What finish works best for oak?**
A: American White Oak takes an exceptionally broad range of finishes. Oil finishes (Danish oil, Osmo Polyx, hardwax oil) are common for flooring and treads. UV-cured lacquer is standard for volume-produced flooring. Oak also accepts stain, paint, lime-wash, ammonia-fuming, and grain-filled high-gloss finishes.

**Q13 · Can I stain oak?**
A: Yes — oak takes stain exceptionally well. From natural clear to dark walnut-look stains, oak accepts a wide range of colour treatments. This is a major difference from walnut, which is rarely stained.

**Q14 · Will oak change colour over time?**
A: Yes. Oak darkens over time under UV exposure, moving toward a warmer honey-brown. This is opposite to walnut, which lightens over time. Interior specifications should account for this drift.

**Q15 · What is medullary ray fleck?**
A: Medullary rays are radial cellular tissue running from the tree's pith outward. When oak is quarter-sawn (cut across the growth rings at 90°), these rays are exposed as distinctive silver, ribbon-like flecks across the face. The ray fleck is a prized feature of quarter-sawn American White Oak and is impossible to fake with veneer print.

**Q16 · Should I specify quarter-sawn oak?**
A: If you want the ray fleck aesthetic, yes. Quarter-sawn oak displays the characteristic silver rays that plain-sawn oak does not. Quarter-sawn is also more dimensionally stable (less movement with humidity change). It is more expensive than plain-sawn because yield is lower.

**Q17 · Is oak expensive?**
A: American White Oak is in the premium cost band, typically slightly less expensive than American Black Walnut but more expensive than pine, ash, or beech. Rift and Quartered stock is more expensive than plain-sawn. Character grades are less expensive.

**Q18 · What is iron-tannin staining and how do I avoid it?**
A: Oak has high tannin content that reacts with iron in the presence of moisture, producing black or blue-black stains. To avoid this, use stainless steel or brass fasteners in oak, especially in damp conditions. If iron-tannin staining has occurred, oxalic acid can lighten it, though specialist knowledge is required.

**Q19 · Can oak be steam-bent?**
A: Yes. American White Oak is one of the traditional timbers for steam bending because it is strong, ductile when hot and wet, and holds its bent shape once cool. Air-dried oak steam-bends better than kiln-dried oak. Steam bending is a specialist process.

**Q20 · Is oak sustainable?**
A: American White Oak is commercially abundant and sustainably managed. FSC and PEFC certified stock is widely available. The species is not endangered and is not subject to CITES restrictions.

**Q21 · Is oak dust dangerous?**
A: Yes. Oak dust is classified as a Group 1 carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer, based on evidence of nasal cancer risk. Standard dust extraction and PPE are essential in any workshop that processes oak.

**Q22 · What alternatives to American White Oak are there?**
A: For similar hardness and general appearance: European Oak (Quercus robur / Q. petraea), Red Oak (less water-resistant, less durable), Ash (softer, similar working characteristics, paler). For a darker premium hardwood: American Black Walnut. For a paler premium hardwood: Ash or Maple.

**Q23 · What NHLA grade should I specify for premium oak work?**
A: FAS (Firsts and Seconds) is the highest NHLA grade and is preferred for visible premium work. For applications requiring the specific medullary ray fleck aesthetic, specify Rift and Quartered. For character-grade specifications with knots and colour variation, No. 1 Common or Character grade is appropriate.

**Q24 · How thick should oak treads be?**
A: Common tread thicknesses for domestic staircases are 32mm and 40mm. Signature tier specifications typically use 40mm solid oak treads. Oak's higher hardness compared to walnut means slightly thinner treads may be acceptable at Classic tier, but 32mm is a common minimum for durability.

**Q25 · How do I clean and maintain oak?**
A: Oil finishes benefit from re-oiling every 1-3 years depending on use. Lacquer finishes can be cleaned with a soft dry or slightly-damp cloth; avoid abrasive cleaners. Maintain stable indoor humidity (approximately 40-55% relative humidity) to minimise seasonal movement, especially with plain-sawn oak which moves more than quarter-sawn.
