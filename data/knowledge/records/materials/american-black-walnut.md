# American Black Walnut

## Summary

American Black Walnut (Juglans nigra) is a premium dark-hued North American hardwood widely used across staircase treads, kitchen cabinet doors, premium flooring, and door raised panels. Janka hardness approximately 1,010 lbf. Machines cleanly, finishes well with oil or lacquer. Not suitable for exterior use. Premium cost band.

---

## Structured Knowledge

### What American Black Walnut Is

American Black Walnut (Juglans nigra, family Juglandaceae) is a domestic North American hardwood prized for its rich chocolate-brown heartwood, straight grain, and excellent working properties. It is one of the most valued hardwoods native to the United States and remains a specification of choice for premium interior joinery, furniture, gunstocks, and architectural millwork.

The tree is native to the eastern and central United States, with commercial timber sourced primarily from the Central Hardwood Region (Ohio, Indiana, Kentucky, Missouri, and adjacent states). It is not related to European Walnut (Juglans regia), although the two are sometimes confused; European Walnut has a paler, more variegated colour and different working characteristics. Where a specification calls for the dark, uniform chocolate colour and straight grain that define American Black Walnut, European Walnut is not a direct substitute.

Within NEX trade specifications, American Black Walnut appears in the Signature tier and NEX Premium™ specifications of the Connected Staircase™ family, and in the premium finish packages for kitchens, doors, and flooring.

### Botanical and Geographic Origin

- Botanical name: *Juglans nigra*
- Family: Juglandaceae (walnut family)
- Native range: Eastern and central United States (Central Hardwood Region)
- Growth: Deciduous tree, typically 30-40 metres at maturity, growing best on deep, well-drained loamy soils
- Sustainability: Commercially available from managed forests. Certified FSC/PEFC stock is available from most reputable suppliers.

### Physical Properties

- Janka hardness: approximately 1,010 lbf (4.5 kN)
- Density (kiln-dried): approximately 610 kg/m³ (38 lb/ft³)
- Modulus of rupture: approximately 100 MPa
- Modulus of elasticity: approximately 11.6 GPa
- Shrinkage: moderate; radial approximately 5.5%, tangential approximately 7.8% (green to oven-dry)
- Dimensional stability: good once properly kiln-dried and acclimatised

American Black Walnut is a moderately hard hardwood — softer than American White Oak (Janka 1,360 lbf) but harder than Cherry (Janka 950 lbf). Its hardness is sufficient for staircase treads in normal domestic use.

### Moisture Content and Kiln Drying

For interior joinery use in the UK, kiln-dried American Black Walnut is typically supplied at 8-12% moisture content. For cabinetry and premium furniture, 6-10% is more common. The moisture content specification depends on the end use environment; joinery installed in a centrally heated UK home should target the lower end of the interior joinery range.

Freshly-milled walnut is unstable and will move as it dries. Any board that has not been properly kiln-dried and acclimatised to the workshop before machining will move again after machining, causing joint failures and finish problems. Acclimatisation of at least 1-2 weeks in the working environment is standard practice before machining premium pieces.

### Working Characteristics

American Black Walnut is one of the most cooperative premium hardwoods to work.

**Machining:** cuts and machines cleanly with sharp tools. CNC routing produces clean edges with minimal tearout. Spindle moulding, thicknessing, and edge sanding are all straightforward. Cross-grain machining requires standard care to avoid tearout in areas of interlocked grain (occasionally present).

**Gluing:** glues well with standard PVA and polyurethane adhesives. No special surface preparation required beyond clean, dry, freshly-machined surfaces.

**Sanding:** sands to a very smooth finish with progressive grits (typically 120 → 180 → 240 for premium work). Fine walnut dust should be extracted; it is a known respiratory irritant.

**Fastening:** takes screws and nails well without pre-drilling in most sections. Pre-drilling recommended near end grain and in thin sections.

**Health note:** walnut dust and, in some individuals, walnut sap can cause allergic reactions. Standard dust extraction and PPE is recommended.

### Aesthetic Characteristics

**Heartwood:** rich chocolate brown, often with darker streaks. Colour uniformity is excellent compared to most hardwoods.
**Sapwood:** cream to pale yellow-brown. Distinct from heartwood. Sapwood is often steamed to darken it, evening out the overall colour, though this reduces the natural colour depth.
**Grain:** generally straight, occasionally wavy or interlocked. Fine, uniform texture.
**Figure:** curly, quilted, and burl figures are prized and command premium prices. Bookmatched panels showcase these features.
**Patina:** unlike some hardwoods, walnut lightens slightly over time under UV exposure, moving toward a warmer honey-brown. This is opposite to Cherry, which darkens over time. Interior specifications should account for this drift.

**Flooring applications:**

### Finishes That Work Well

American Black Walnut takes a broad range of finishes cleanly.

- **Oil finishes** (Danish oil, Osmo Polyx, hardwax oil): enhance grain depth and colour, provide a natural feel, easy to spot-repair. Preferred for Signature tier work.
- **UV-cured lacquer** (matt or satin sheen): factory-applied, durable, low-VOC in cured state. Preferred for volume production.
- **Traditional lacquer** (satin or matt): compatible; less durable than UV-cured but easier to spot-repair.
- **Clear satin polyurethane**: durable, less warm than oil, preferred where high wear resistance is needed.
- **Water-based clear coats**: possible but often show less warmth in walnut than oil-based finishes.

Stains are rarely applied to American Black Walnut — the natural colour is what the specification is buying. If darker or more uniform colour is desired, dye-based colour balancing rather than pigment staining preserves grain clarity.

### Environments and Uses to Avoid

American Black Walnut is not suitable for:

- **Exterior use.** Not durable outdoors. Will grey, split, and decay without protective coatings; even with coatings, longevity is poor compared to species intended for exterior work (Iroko, Sapele, treated softwoods).
- **Direct wet-area use.** Not recommended for shower floors, kitchen worktop surfaces with prolonged wet contact, or bathroom flooring below splash zones.
- **Direct food contact surfaces without appropriate finishing.** Kitchen worktops can be walnut if properly sealed and maintained, but the timber is not a food-contact material in the way maple or beech is traditionally.
- **High-abrasion commercial floors** (public entrances, retail sales floors). The Janka hardness is sufficient for domestic use but softer than optimal for high-traffic commercial applications.

### Cost Band and Grades

Within the NEX cost banding, American Black Walnut is in the **premium** band — significantly above pine, softwoods, and lower-grade domestic hardwoods; comparable to European Oak and American White Oak (which are typically slightly less expensive); below highly figured or rare species (bookmatched burl, quilted maple, some tropical hardwoods).

Common grading (per US National Hardwood Lumber Association):
- **FAS (Firsts and Seconds)**: highest grade, clear on both faces. Preferred for visible premium work.
- **Selects**: clear on one face, minor defects on the other. Common for cabinet work.
- **No. 1 Common (Common #1)**: shorter clear cuttings. Used where component sizes are small.
- **No. 2 Common**: character grade with knots and colour variation. Used for character-grade flooring and rustic specifications.

Grade selection materially affects cost. Signature tier and NEX Premium™ specifications typically call for FAS or Selects.

### Sustainability

American Black Walnut is commercially available from managed forests. FSC and PEFC certified stock is available from most reputable UK importers. The species is not endangered and is not subject to CITES restrictions (unlike some other walnut species such as *Juglans neotropica*). Sustainability specifications for Signature tier and NEX Premium™ work should specify FSC/PEFC certification and country of origin.

## Advantages

- **Rich uniform chocolate colour** — one of few domestic hardwoods with this depth without staining.
- **Excellent workability** — machines, glues, sands, and finishes cleanly.
- **Broad finish compatibility** — takes oil, lacquer, UV-cured coatings, and clear coats well.
- **Adequate hardness for domestic use** — sufficient for stair treads, cabinet doors, and residential flooring.
- **Good dimensional stability** once properly kiln-dried and acclimatised.
- **Widely available** in the UK from importers of North American hardwoods.
- **Certified sustainable stock available** — FSC/PEFC.
- **Cross-domain applicability** — the same species specification serves staircase, kitchen, flooring, doors, and furniture use cases.
- **Premium perceived value** — customers recognise walnut as a premium material.

## Considerations

- **Premium cost.** Significantly more expensive than pine, oak, or ash. Cost limits it to premium specifications.
- **Colour drift under UV.** Lightens slightly over time toward a warmer honey-brown; interior specifications should account for this.
- **Sapwood contrast.** Untreated sapwood is distinctly paler than heartwood; either specify all-heartwood stock or accept the natural contrast.
- **Dust health.** Walnut dust is a known respiratory irritant; standard extraction and PPE required in the workshop.
- **Not exterior-durable.** Cannot be used outdoors without significant loss of appearance and structural integrity.
- **Softer than oak.** Janka 1,010 lbf vs 1,360 lbf for American White Oak. Not the hardest choice for high-wear surfaces.

## Common Mistakes

- **Substituting European Walnut and expecting the same colour.** European Walnut is paler and more variegated. If the specification calls for American Black Walnut colour uniformity, European Walnut is not a substitute.
- **Using walnut for exterior applications.** Not durable outdoors. Specifying it for exterior entrance doors or exterior handrails is a specification error.
- **Skipping acclimatisation.** Machining walnut that has not acclimatised to the workshop environment leads to movement post-machining.
- **Using inappropriate finishes for wet areas.** Even walnut kitchen worktops require appropriate sealing and reasonable expectations about wet-use.
- **Under-specifying grade.** For visible premium work, calling for "walnut" without a grade specification can lead to Common #1 stock being supplied where FAS was intended.
- **Ignoring colour drift.** Specifying walnut for a colour-critical scheme without noting the UV-driven lightening leads to disappointment years later.

## Sourcing and Working Notes

- **Sourcing.** UK importers of North American hardwoods typically stock American Black Walnut in FAS and Selects grades. Certified FSC/PEFC stock should be specified for Signature tier and NEX Premium™ work.
- **Kiln drying and acclimatisation.** Specify 8-12% moisture content for UK interior joinery use. Acclimatise 1-2 weeks in the working environment before machining premium pieces.
- **Cutting.** Use sharp tooling. Extract dust; walnut dust is a respiratory irritant.
- **Gluing.** Standard PVA and polyurethane adhesives work well. Ensure clean, dry, freshly-machined surfaces.
- **Sanding.** Progressive grits (typically 120 → 180 → 240 for premium work). Break edges finely.
- **Pre-finishing.** Where possible, finish components before assembly to avoid glue lines showing.

## Maintenance

Finished walnut in normal interior use requires minimal maintenance:

- **Oil finishes:** re-oil approximately every 1-3 years depending on use. Spot-repair is straightforward.
- **Lacquer finishes:** clean with a soft dry or slightly-damp cloth. Avoid abrasive cleaners. Refinish if surface wear becomes visible.
- **Waxes:** re-wax annually where used.
- **Environmental:** maintain stable indoor humidity (approximately 40-55% relative humidity) to minimise seasonal movement. Sudden humidity swings can cause hairline cracks in wide panels.

## Concepts

### Industry Knowledge

The following terms and mechanisms are established industry practice, not NEX-specific. They are referenced by this record but not owned by it.

- **Janka hardness** — a standard test measuring the force required to embed a steel ball to half its diameter into a wood sample. Expressed in pounds-force (lbf) or kilonewtons (kN).
- **Kiln drying** — the controlled drying of timber in a kiln to reduce moisture content to a specified target before use.
- **Moisture content (MC)** — the mass of water in a piece of timber expressed as a percentage of the oven-dry mass of the timber.
- **Modulus of rupture (MOR)** — the maximum stress a piece of timber can withstand in bending before failure.
- **Modulus of elasticity (MOE)** — the ratio of stress to strain in the elastic region; a measure of stiffness.
- **Shrinkage (radial and tangential)** — the dimensional change in timber as moisture content decreases.
- **Heartwood** — the mature inner wood of a tree, generally darker and more decay-resistant than sapwood.
- **Sapwood** — the outer, younger wood of a tree, generally paler and less durable than heartwood.
- **NHLA grading** — the National Hardwood Lumber Association grading system used for North American hardwoods (FAS, Selects, Common #1, Common #2).
- **FAS grade (Firsts and Seconds)** — the highest NHLA grade for hardwoods, requiring clear cuttings on both faces.
- **Bookmatching** — a veneer technique in which adjacent leaves are flipped like the pages of a book so their grain patterns mirror each other.
- **CITES** — the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species; regulates trade in certain species.
- **FSC / PEFC** — certification schemes for sustainably managed forests.
- **BS EN 942** — British Standard for timber in joinery, including moisture content requirements for interior use.
- **Approved Document K** — UK Building Regulations covering stairs, ramps and guarding.

### NEX Concepts

The following are NEX-proprietary concepts that reference this material record.

- **Connected Staircase™ Signature tier walnut specification** — the tier hierarchy calls for solid or predominantly-solid walnut in Signature tier staircases.
- **NEX Premium™ walnut specification** — the presentation layer specifies FAS-grade FSC-certified American Black Walnut with UV-cured low-sheen finish.
- **NEX cost band: premium** — walnut sits in the premium cost band within the NEX materials taxonomy.
- **D1 (Selective Material Placement)** — the doctrine that concentrates premium materials where seen and touched; walnut is a canonical D1 material because it is often specified for treads and handrails while the shell remains softer/painted material.

**Q1 · What is American Black Walnut?**
A: American Black Walnut is a premium North American hardwood (Juglans nigra) with a rich chocolate-brown heartwood, straight grain, and excellent working properties. It is widely used for staircase treads, kitchen cabinet doors, flooring, and door panels in premium specifications.

**Q2 · Where does American Black Walnut come from?**
A: American Black Walnut is native to the eastern and central United States, with commercial timber sourced primarily from the Central Hardwood Region (Ohio, Indiana, Kentucky, Missouri, and adjacent states).

**Q3 · Is walnut a hardwood or a softwood?**
A: Walnut is a hardwood. American Black Walnut has a Janka hardness of approximately 1,010 lbf, which is sufficient for domestic staircase treads, cabinet doors, and residential flooring.

**Q4 · How does walnut compare to oak for hardness?**
A: American Black Walnut is softer than American White Oak. Walnut's Janka hardness is approximately 1,010 lbf; American White Oak is approximately 1,360 lbf. Walnut is still hard enough for domestic staircase treads and flooring but is softer than oak.

**Q5 · Is American Black Walnut the same as European Walnut?**
A: No. American Black Walnut (Juglans nigra) is native to North America and has a rich uniform chocolate-brown colour. European Walnut (Juglans regia) is a different species with a paler, more variegated colour. If a specification calls for American Black Walnut's colour and uniformity, European Walnut is not a direct substitute.

**Q6 · Can I use walnut for stair treads?**
A: Yes. American Black Walnut is a standard specification for premium stair treads. It is used in the Signature tier of the Connected Staircase™ family and in NEX Premium™ specifications.

**Q7 · Can I use walnut for a kitchen?**
A: Yes. Walnut is widely used for kitchen cabinet doors, end panels, and feature open shelving. Kitchen worktops can be walnut if properly sealed and maintained, though walnut is not the traditional choice for high-wet-use surfaces.

**Q8 · Can I use walnut for flooring?**
A: Yes. American Black Walnut is used for premium flooring, both as solid planks and as engineered flooring. Signature tier flooring specifications may call for wide-plank walnut or bookmatched herringbone.

**Q9 · Can I use walnut for doors?**
A: Yes, for interior doors. Walnut is used for raised and fielded panel doors, solid stile-and-rail construction, and veneered flush doors for premium specifications. It should not be used for exterior doors due to poor outdoor durability.

**Q10 · Can I use walnut outside?**
A: No. American Black Walnut is not durable outdoors. It will grey, split, and decay without protective coatings, and even with coatings its longevity is poor compared to species intended for exterior work such as Iroko, Sapele, or treated softwoods.

**Q11 · Can I use walnut in a bathroom?**
A: Walnut can be used in bathrooms for surfaces away from direct wet contact (vanity units, cabinet fronts, wall panelling) with appropriate finishing. It is not recommended for shower floors, bathroom flooring below splash zones, or any surface with prolonged wet contact.

**Q12 · What finish works best for walnut?**
A: Oil finishes (Danish oil, Osmo Polyx, hardwax oil) enhance the natural warmth and grain depth and are preferred for Signature tier work. UV-cured lacquer is preferred for volume production. Traditional lacquer, clear polyurethane, and water-based clear coats are all technically compatible.

**Q13 · Do I need to stain walnut?**
A: Usually not. American Black Walnut's natural colour is what most specifications call for. If darker or more uniform colour is desired, dye-based colour balancing rather than pigment staining preserves grain clarity.

**Q14 · Will walnut change colour over time?**
A: Yes. Walnut lightens slightly over time under UV exposure, moving toward a warmer honey-brown. This is opposite to Cherry, which darkens. Interior specifications should account for this drift, especially in colour-critical schemes.

**Q15 · Is walnut expensive?**
A: American Black Walnut is in the premium cost band. It is significantly more expensive than pine or lower-grade domestic hardwoods, and comparable to European Oak or American White Oak (which are typically slightly less expensive). Highly figured walnut (curly, quilted, burl) is more expensive again.

**Q16 · What thickness of walnut should I use for stair treads?**
A: Common tread thicknesses for domestic staircases are 32mm and 40mm. Thicker treads (40mm+) look more substantial and reduce deflection under load. Signature tier specifications typically use 40mm solid walnut treads.

**Q17 · Does walnut move much?**
A: American Black Walnut has good dimensional stability once properly kiln-dried and acclimatised. Radial shrinkage is approximately 5.5% and tangential shrinkage approximately 7.8% from green to oven-dry. Freshly-milled walnut is unstable and must be kiln-dried and acclimatised before machining.

**Q18 · What moisture content should walnut be at?**
A: For interior joinery use in the UK, kiln-dried American Black Walnut is typically supplied at 8-12% moisture content. For cabinetry and premium furniture, 6-10% is more common. Joinery installed in a centrally heated UK home should target the lower end of the interior joinery range.

**Q19 · How is walnut machined?**
A: American Black Walnut machines cleanly with sharp tools. CNC routing, spindle moulding, thicknessing, and edge sanding are all straightforward. Cross-grain machining requires standard care to avoid tearout in areas of interlocked grain. Dust extraction is important because walnut dust is a respiratory irritant.

**Q20 · Can walnut be glued easily?**
A: Yes. Walnut glues well with standard PVA and polyurethane adhesives. No special surface preparation is required beyond clean, dry, freshly-machined surfaces.

**Q21 · Is walnut sustainable?**
A: American Black Walnut is commercially available from managed forests, and FSC or PEFC certified stock is available from most reputable UK importers. The species is not endangered and is not subject to CITES restrictions. Signature tier and NEX Premium™ work should specify certified stock.

**Q22 · What alternatives to walnut are there?**
A: Depending on which properties matter: for a similar dark colour without staining, no direct domestic substitute exists (dark-stained oak or dark-stained ash can approximate the colour but with different grain character). For hardness with a similar working profile, American White Oak or European Oak. For cost reduction while preserving premium appearance in a specific application, the D1 Selective Material Placement doctrine suggests concentrating walnut only on visible surfaces (treads, handrails, cabinet fronts) with a softer species used for the shell.

**Q23 · Can walnut be stained darker?**
A: Yes but rarely done. American Black Walnut is already dark, and staining darker tends to obscure the grain that customers are buying walnut to see. Where a very dark finish is required, walnut with a dark oil or a fumed treatment produces the effect while preserving grain visibility better than pigment stain.

**Q24 · What NHLA grade should I specify for premium walnut work?**
A: FAS (Firsts and Seconds) is the highest NHLA grade and is preferred for visible premium work. Selects is acceptable where one face is not visible (e.g., inside cabinet doors). For character-grade specifications where knots and colour variation are desired, Common #1 or Common #2 is appropriate.

**Q25 · How do I clean and maintain a walnut staircase or kitchen?**
A: Finished walnut requires minimal maintenance. Oil finishes benefit from re-oiling every 1-3 years depending on use. Lacquer finishes can be cleaned with a soft dry or slightly-damp cloth; avoid abrasive cleaners. Maintain stable indoor humidity (approximately 40-55% relative humidity) to minimise seasonal movement.
