# Maple

## Summary

Maple is the NEX name for two closely related hardwood groups: **Hard Maple** (Acer saccharum — the North American sugar maple) and **Soft Maple** (a group including Acer rubrum, A. saccharinum, and related species). Hard Maple has one of the highest Janka hardness values of any commonly-specified hardwood (~1,450 lbf). Pale cream to light brown, tight straight grain with subtle figure. Widely used for flooring (especially sports flooring), kitchen worktops, cabinets, staircase treads, and butcher blocks. Premium cost band. No sustainability alert.

## Structured Knowledge

### What Maple Is

Maple is the NEX name for two related groups of hardwoods commonly available in UK trade:

- **Hard Maple** (also called Sugar Maple or Rock Maple) — *Acer saccharum*, native to eastern North America. This is the reference specification when a NEX record specifies "maple" without further qualification.
- **Soft Maple** — a trade term covering several related North American species including Red Maple (*Acer rubrum*), Silver Maple (*Acer saccharinum*), and Bigleaf Maple (*Acer macrophyllum*). Softer, slightly darker, and cheaper than Hard Maple. Trade uses often accept Soft Maple as a substitute where Hard Maple's exceptional hardness is not required.
- **European Maple** — *Acer pseudoplatanus* (Sycamore Maple, often called Sycamore in UK trade). Related but distinct; sometimes specified for UK provenance.

Hard Maple is one of the hardest of the common commercial hardwoods (Janka ~1,450 lbf — harder than oak, harder than walnut, harder than ash). This hardness combined with its pale, uniform colour and tight grain makes it the specification of choice for surfaces that must resist wear while showing minimal patina: sports flooring (basketball courts, gymnasium floors, bowling lanes), butcher-block kitchen worktops, and cutting boards.

Unlike Ash, Maple has no active sustainability crisis. Supply from North American forestry is stable, FSC and PEFC certified stock is widely available.

### Botanical and Geographic Origin

- **Botanical names:** *Acer saccharum* (Hard Maple / Sugar Maple); *Acer rubrum* (Red Maple, Soft Maple); *Acer saccharinum* (Silver Maple, Soft Maple); *Acer pseudoplatanus* (Sycamore, European Maple).
- **Family:** Sapindaceae (soapberry family).
- **Native countries (Hard Maple / Sugar Maple):** Northeastern United States and southeastern Canada.
- **Native countries (Soft Maple group):** Eastern United States and Canada.
- **Native countries (European Maple / Sycamore):** UK, Ireland, France, Germany, Central Europe.
- **Commercial growing countries:** USA (northern and northeastern), Canada (Ontario, Quebec, Nova Scotia); UK for European Maple.
- **Countries where commonly used:** USA, Canada, UK, Europe generally. Hard Maple sports flooring is used worldwide.

### Physical Properties

**Hard Maple (Acer saccharum):**
- **Janka hardness:** approximately 1,450 lbf (6.4 kN) — one of the hardest commonly-specified hardwoods.
- **Density (kiln-dried):** approximately 720 kg/m³.
- **Modulus of rupture:** approximately 109 MPa.
- **Modulus of elasticity:** approximately 12.6 GPa.
- **Shrinkage:** moderate; radial approximately 4.8%, tangential approximately 9.9%.

**Soft Maple (Red Maple as representative):**
- **Janka hardness:** approximately 950 lbf (4.2 kN).
- **Density:** approximately 620 kg/m³.

Hard Maple is significantly harder than every other timber in the current NEX corpus: harder than American White Oak (1,360 lbf), European Oak (1,120 lbf), American Black Walnut (1,010 lbf), and European Ash (1,010 lbf). Only American White Ash (1,320 lbf) approaches it.

### Distinctive Features · Hardness and Sap

Hard Maple's defining functional feature is its exceptional hardness combined with a very tight, closed grain. This produces:
- **Superb wear resistance** — bowling lanes and basketball courts survive daily impact and abrasion for decades on Hard Maple.
- **Minimal grain-tearing under load** — even at high impact, the grain does not lift or splinter.
- **Excellent surface polish** — the tight grain sands to a smooth surface without grain-fill.
- **Uniform appearance** — colour and grain are exceptionally consistent, giving Hard Maple its "clean" aesthetic.

Sugar Maple is famously the source of maple syrup — sap collected from tapped trees is boiled to concentrate the sugars. This has no impact on timber use but reflects the cultural and commercial significance of the tree in eastern North America.

### Moisture Content and Kiln Drying

Standard hardwood practice: 8-12% moisture content for UK interior joinery. See `processes_kiln_drying_v1`. Maple dries relatively slowly due to its density and tight grain; kiln schedules for maple are longer than for oak or ash. Post-kiln acclimatisation is standard (1-2 weeks in the workshop).

### Working Characteristics

- **Machining:** cuts and machines with sharp carbide tooling. Requires more power than walnut due to density. Very sharp tools are essential — dull tools produce burn marks on maple's tight surface.
- **Gluing:** glues well with standard PVA and polyurethane adhesives.
- **Sanding:** sands to an exceptionally smooth finish; the tight grain produces surfaces that need no grain-fill for high-gloss work. Sanding progression typically 120 → 180 → 240 for premium work.
- **Fastening:** takes screws and nails well; standard steel fasteners acceptable (no iron-tannin risk).
- **Bending:** poor. Maple does not steam-bend well; specify ash or oak for bent components.
- **Turning:** excellent. Maple's tight grain and hardness produce clean crisp turned profiles.
- **Health note:** maple dust is a Group 1 carcinogen per IARC (as all hardwood dust). Standard extraction and PPE.

### Aesthetic Characteristics

- **Heartwood:** pale cream to light brown, sometimes with a slight reddish hue in Soft Maple.
- **Sapwood:** very close in colour to heartwood in Hard Maple — often indistinguishable. Maple is one of few hardwoods where sapwood is not typically avoided.
- **Grain:** straight, very tight, uniform texture. Prominent grain lines are absent or subtle.
- **Figure:** normally plain, but figured maple (curly maple, quilted maple, birdseye maple) is highly prized. Birdseye maple in particular is unique to Hard Maple and produces small circular figures across the face; it is an exceptionally expensive specialist grade.
- **Patina:** Hard Maple stays remarkably pale over time. UV exposure produces only slight yellowing. This is opposite to walnut (lightens) and oak (darkens). For applications where the pale colour must be preserved, Hard Maple is the timber of choice.
- **Colour uniformity:** exceptional. Maple has the most uniform colour of the common hardwoods.

### Applications Across NEX Trades

**Staircase applications:**
- Signature and Contemporary tier stair treads where exceptional hardness matters (heavy domestic use, light commercial).
- Handrails (less common than oak; the pale colour suits contemporary designs).
- Newel posts — turned maple newels are traditional in some UK vernacular contexts.

**Kitchen applications:**
- Butcher-block worktops — the defining kitchen application. Maple is the traditional cutting-board timber; its tight grain resists knife damage and does not harbour bacteria in surface pores.
- Cabinet doors (Shaker style particularly, in pale contemporary aesthetics).
- Kitchen islands and worktops in traditional US-style kitchens.

**Flooring applications:**
- **Sports flooring** — basketball courts, gymnasium floors, bowling lanes. This is a canonical Hard Maple application. Specifications typically call for graded MFMA (Maple Flooring Manufacturers Association) maple.
- Domestic flooring — solid or engineered, pale contemporary aesthetics.
- Herringbone maple flooring for luxury domestic use.

**Doors applications:**
- Shaker-style panel doors.
- Contemporary flush doors.

**Furniture applications (future record):**
- Kitchen cabinets and cutting boards.
- Baby furniture (Hard Maple's food-safety credentials suit cots and high chairs).
- Rocking chairs, Windsor chair components.

### Rot and Insect Resistance

- **Rot resistance (interior):** moderate. Maple has better durability than ash but is not as durable as oak for damp conditions.
- **Rot resistance (exterior):** poor. Maple is not suitable for exterior use without significant preservative treatment.
- **Insect resistance:** moderate. Standard powder-post beetle risk in sapwood.
- **Live-wood diseases:** no active crisis. Sugar maple is stable commercially.

### Sustainability and Certification

- **FSC and PEFC certification:** widely available for both Hard and Soft Maple.
- **Forestry stability:** North American maple is commercially stable and well-managed.
- **CITES:** not listed.
- **No active sustainability alert** — this is a supply-stable specification.

### UK Availability and Import Considerations

- **UK availability:** Hard Maple and Soft Maple widely available through UK hardwood importers. Sports flooring grade (MFMA-graded) available through specialist flooring merchants.
- **Import considerations:** standard North American hardwood import; no phytosanitary concerns comparable to Ash. Post-Brexit standard customs treatment.
- **European Maple / Sycamore:** UK-grown Sycamore available through domestic hardwood merchants.

### Cost Band and Grades

Maple sits in the **premium** cost band. Hard Maple typically costs less than American Black Walnut, comparable to American White Oak, and more than Ash. Birdseye maple, curly maple, and quilted maple carry substantial premiums.

Common grading:
- **NHLA grading**: FAS, Selects, Common #1, Common #2.
- **MFMA grading (sports flooring):** First Grade, Second and Better, Third and Better, Third — sports flooring grades based on visual defects.
- **Figured maple grades:** birdseye, curly, quilted — priced separately and often supplied through specialist merchants.

### Comparison with Other Hardwoods

- **vs American White Oak** — Maple is harder (1,450 vs 1,360 lbf), paler, more uniform, tighter grain. Oak has the medullary ray fleck; maple does not. Choose maple where hardness and pale uniform appearance matter; oak where the traditional grain aesthetic matters.
- **vs Ash** — Maple is significantly harder (1,450 vs 1,010-1,320 lbf), paler, tighter grain. Ash bends better; maple does not. Also — Ash has an active sustainability crisis while maple does not.
- **vs Walnut** — Maple is harder (1,450 vs 1,010 lbf), paler and cooler in tone. Choose walnut for dark warmth; maple for pale contemporary aesthetics.
- **vs Beech** — Maple is harder (1,450 vs approximately 1,300 lbf), often paler, more uniform. Beech is more likely to have visible medullary rays.

### NEX Recommendations

- **For sports flooring or high-wear domestic flooring:** Hard Maple with MFMA grading is the specification of choice.
- **For butcher-block kitchen worktops:** Hard Maple is the traditional and functionally superior specification.
- **For Signature tier stair treads with exceptional hardness requirement:** Hard Maple; note the pale colour is a design choice, not a workaround for oak availability.
- **For Contemporary tier kitchen cabinets with pale aesthetic:** Hard Maple in Shaker style.
- **Where a substitute for ash is needed (given Ash Dieback / EAB):** Hard Maple is a viable alternative for surfaces where ash's specific bending or shock-absorption qualities are not required.

## Advantages

- **Exceptional hardness** — one of the hardest commonly-specified hardwoods.
- **Exceptionally tight uniform grain** — sands to smooth surface without grain-fill.
- **Very stable pale colour** — minimal UV drift.
- **Food-safe** — traditional cutting-board and butcher-block timber.
- **No sustainability crisis** — stable supply, widely available FSC/PEFC certified.
- **No iron-tannin staining risk** — standard steel fasteners acceptable.
- **Uniform sapwood-to-heartwood transition** — sapwood not typically avoided.
- **Figured varieties available** — birdseye, curly, quilted for specialist premium work.

## Considerations

- **Poor bending performance** — do not specify for steam-bent components.
- **Very sharp tools required** — dull tools burn maple's tight surface.
- **Denser than oak or walnut** — machining consumes more power.
- **Poor exterior durability** — interior use only.
- **Colour drift is subtle** — this is an advantage for pale-colour longevity but means the material does not develop the character patina that oak or walnut do.
- **Maple dust is a Group 1 carcinogen** (IARC).

## Common Mistakes

- **Substituting Soft Maple for Hard Maple** where hardness matters.
- **Attempting to steam-bend maple** — it does not bend well.
- **Using dull tools on maple** — produces burn marks that are difficult to remove.
- **Specifying maple for exterior use** — it is not durable outdoors.
- **Assuming figured maple (birdseye, curly) can be substituted with plain maple** — the figure is what the specification is buying.
- **Under-specifying grade for sports flooring** — MFMA grades matter for sports floor performance.

## Sourcing and Working Notes

- **Sourcing.** UK hardwood importers stock Hard Maple in FAS and Selects. MFMA-graded sports flooring is available through specialist flooring merchants. Figured maple is available through specialist premium hardwood dealers.
- **Grade specification.** For visible premium work, specify FAS. For sports flooring, specify MFMA grade. For figured work, specify the figure type (birdseye, curly, quilted).
- **Kiln drying and acclimatisation.** 8-12% moisture content for UK interior joinery. Maple dries slower than oak or ash; kiln schedules may be longer.
- **Tooling.** Use very sharp carbide tools. Replace tools sooner than for oak or ash to avoid burn marks.
- **Sanding.** Progressive grits; maple's tight grain accepts high-gloss finish without grain-fill.

## Maintenance

Similar to other pale hardwoods. Oil finishes benefit from re-oiling every 1-3 years; lacquer finishes clean with damp cloth; maintain stable indoor humidity 40-55%.

## Concepts

### Industry Knowledge

- **Sugar Maple** (*Acer saccharum*) — Hard Maple; the reference "maple" specification.
- **Red Maple** (*Acer rubrum*) — Soft Maple.
- **Sycamore** (*Acer pseudoplatanus*) — European Maple; note that this is a different species from American Sycamore (Platanus occidentalis) despite the shared common name in UK trade.
- **MFMA** (Maple Flooring Manufacturers Association) — American grading body for maple sports flooring.
- **Birdseye figure** — small circular figures unique to Hard Maple; premium specialist grade.
- **Curly figure** — undulating figure across the grain; found in maple and other species.
- **Quilted figure** — three-dimensional bubbling figure; premium specialist grade.
- **Ring-diffuse porous** — a wood anatomy classification; maple is diffuse-porous (vessels distributed evenly through the ring) rather than ring-porous like oak.

### NEX Concepts

- **NEX preferred sports flooring specification** — Hard Maple with MFMA grade.
- **NEX butcher-block kitchen specification** — Hard Maple as the reference specification.
- **NEX substitute-for-ash guidance** — Hard Maple is a viable alternative for ash where bending performance is not the specification's driving requirement.

## Canonical Q&A (Auto-Generated From Structured Knowledge · Philip 2026-08-06 FAQ Automation Rule)

**Q1 · What is Maple?**
A: Maple is a North American hardwood (primarily Hard Maple, Acer saccharum, from northeastern USA and southeastern Canada) prized for its exceptional hardness, tight uniform grain, pale colour, and stable supply. Widely used for sports flooring, butcher-block kitchen worktops, cabinets, and premium stair treads.

**Q2 · What is the difference between Hard Maple and Soft Maple?**
A: Hard Maple (Acer saccharum, Sugar Maple) is significantly harder (Janka ~1,450 lbf) than Soft Maple (a group including Acer rubrum and A. saccharinum, Janka ~950 lbf). Hard Maple is the reference specification when a NEX record calls for "maple" without further qualification. Soft Maple is often accepted as a substitute where the extreme hardness of Hard Maple is not required.

**Q3 · How hard is Maple compared to Oak?**
A: Hard Maple (Janka 1,450 lbf) is harder than American White Oak (Janka 1,360 lbf) and significantly harder than European Oak (Janka 1,120 lbf). Hard Maple is one of the hardest commonly-specified hardwoods.

**Q4 · Can I use Maple for stair treads?**
A: Yes. Hard Maple's exceptional hardness suits stair treads under heavy domestic use and light commercial applications. Signature and Contemporary tier specifications may call for maple treads where the pale colour is the design intent.

**Q5 · Can I use Maple for kitchen worktops?**
A: Yes. Hard Maple butcher-block worktops are the traditional and functionally superior kitchen specification. The tight grain resists knife damage and does not harbour bacteria in surface pores, making it food-safe.

**Q6 · Is Maple good for flooring?**
A: Yes. Hard Maple is the canonical timber for sports flooring — basketball courts, gymnasium floors, bowling lanes. It also suits pale contemporary domestic flooring. For sports use, specify MFMA-graded maple.

**Q7 · What is MFMA maple flooring?**
A: MFMA is the Maple Flooring Manufacturers Association, which grades maple sports flooring to standard specifications (First Grade, Second and Better, Third and Better, Third). MFMA-graded maple is the specification for basketball courts and gymnasium floors.

**Q8 · Can Maple be used outdoors?**
A: No. Maple has poor exterior durability and is not suitable for outdoor use without significant preservative treatment.

**Q9 · Does Maple bend well?**
A: No. Maple does not steam-bend well. For curved handrails and bent components, specify Ash or Oak instead.

**Q10 · What colour is Maple?**
A: Pale cream to light brown. Hard Maple is exceptionally uniform in colour, with sapwood almost indistinguishable from heartwood. The colour stays remarkably pale over time — Hard Maple does not darken like oak or walnut.

**Q11 · Will Maple change colour over time?**
A: Only slightly. Hard Maple lightens or yellows slightly under UV exposure but remains much paler than oak, walnut, or ash. This is the reason maple is specified where pale colour must be preserved for the long term.

**Q12 · Is Maple expensive?**
A: Hard Maple sits in the premium cost band, typically less expensive than American Black Walnut, comparable to American White Oak, and more expensive than Ash. Figured maple (birdseye, curly, quilted) carries substantial premium.

**Q13 · What is birdseye maple?**
A: Birdseye figure is a premium specialist grade unique to Hard Maple, characterised by small circular figures across the timber face. It is priced substantially above plain maple and supplied through specialist premium hardwood dealers.

**Q14 · Is Maple sustainable?**
A: Yes. Hard Maple has no active sustainability crisis. Supply is commercially stable and FSC/PEFC certified stock is widely available. This distinguishes maple from ash, which is affected by Ash Dieback and Emerald Ash Borer.

**Q15 · Is Maple food-safe?**
A: Yes. Hard Maple is the traditional cutting-board timber. Its tight grain does not harbour bacteria; it is standard practice for kitchen surfaces in direct food contact.

**Q16 · What finish works best on Maple?**
A: Maple takes a broad range of finishes. Oil (Danish oil, hardwax oil), UV-cured lacquer, traditional lacquer, and stain all work. Maple's very tight grain allows high-gloss finishes without grain-fill — an advantage over oak or ash.

**Q17 · Can I stain Maple?**
A: Yes, but maple can be tricky to stain evenly due to its dense structure — the stain sometimes penetrates unevenly, producing blotches. A pre-stain conditioner is recommended for even results.

**Q18 · What are the alternatives to Maple?**
A: Depending on requirement: American White Oak (harder aesthetic, more traditional character), Beech (similar hardness, similar pale colour), Ash (better bending but current sustainability crisis), Hickory (harder, similar shock absorption).

**Q19 · Can Maple substitute for Ash?**
A: Yes, for applications where ash's specific bending performance or shock absorption is not required. Given the current Ash Dieback and Emerald Ash Borer crises, Hard Maple is a viable alternative for many general interior applications previously specified in ash.

**Q20 · Is Maple dust dangerous?**
A: Yes. Maple dust — like all hardwood dust — is classified as a Group 1 carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer. Standard dust extraction and PPE are essential.

**Q21 · Do I need special fasteners for Maple?**
A: No. Unlike oak, maple has no tannin and does not cause iron-tannin staining. Standard steel fasteners are acceptable.

**Q22 · How do I clean and maintain Maple?**
A: Oil finishes benefit from re-oiling every 1-3 years. Lacquer finishes can be cleaned with a soft dry or slightly-damp cloth. Butcher-block kitchen worktops benefit from periodic oiling with food-safe mineral oil. Maintain stable indoor humidity 40-55%.

**Q23 · What is Sycamore in UK trade?**
A: In UK trade, "Sycamore" typically refers to European Maple (Acer pseudoplatanus), which is native to the UK and Europe. This is different from the American Sycamore (Platanus occidentalis), which shares the common name but is a different species.

**Q24 · Where does most commercial Maple come from?**
A: Hard Maple is commercially sourced primarily from the eastern United States (New England, Great Lakes region) and eastern Canada (Ontario, Quebec, Nova Scotia). This is the same range that produces maple syrup — the timber and syrup industries share the sugar maple resource.

**Q25 · How is Maple dried?**
A: Standard hardwood kiln-drying practice: 8-12% moisture content for UK interior joinery. Maple dries relatively slowly due to its density and tight grain; kiln schedules for maple are longer than for oak or ash. See the canonical Kiln Drying process record.
