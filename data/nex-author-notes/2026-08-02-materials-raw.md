## 1. OSB Board (Oriented Strand Board)

**What is OSB?**

OSB is made from large wood strands arranged in layers and compressed with resin.

The strands are oriented in different directions to improve strength.

Common grades:

- OSB/1 – general indoor use
- OSB/2 – dry structural use
- OSB/3 – moisture-resistant structural board
- OSB/4 – heavy-duty structural use

**OSB Sizes**

Common sheets:
- 2440mm × 1220mm
- 2500mm × 1250mm

Thickness: 6mm · 9mm · 11mm · 15mm · 18mm · 22mm · 25mm

**OSB Strength**

OSB is stronger than MDF in many structural situations. Why? The long
wood strands create a board that handles shear forces, structural loading
and fixings better than MDF.

**OSB Advantages**

✅ Strong · ✅ Good screw holding · ✅ Cheaper than plywood
✅ Good for construction · ✅ Available in large sheets
✅ Moisture-resistant grades available

**OSB Disadvantages**

❌ Rough surface · ❌ Not a premium painted finish
❌ Visible chips/texture · ❌ Edges can swell if wet
❌ Requires more preparation

**OSB for Staircases**

Good uses: ✅ Stair structural boxing · ✅ Temporary stair construction
✅ Under-stair structures · ✅ Wall panels · ✅ Hidden supports

Poor uses: ❌ Painted risers (unless heavily prepared) · ❌ Kitchen doors
❌ Handrails · ❌ Visible premium staircase parts

OSB is a construction board, not usually a finishing board.

## 2. Acrylic Sheet (Perspex / PMMA)

**What is Acrylic?**

Acrylic is a plastic sheet made from polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA).
It is a transparent or coloured alternative to glass.

**Acrylic Sheet Sizes**

Common sizes: 2440mm × 1220mm · 3050mm × 2050mm

Thickness: 2mm · 3mm · 5mm · 6mm · 10mm · 12mm · 20mm

**Acrylic Properties**

- Around 10–20 times more impact resistant than glass
- Lightweight
- UV resistant grades available

**Acrylic Advantages**

✅ Modern appearance · ✅ High gloss finish · ✅ Available in many colours
✅ Can be laser cut · ✅ Can be CNC machined · ✅ Can be polished
✅ Easy to clean

**Acrylic Disadvantages**

❌ Scratches easier than glass · ❌ Expands with heat
❌ Can crack if poorly drilled · ❌ More expensive than MDF

**Acrylic for Kitchen Doors**

Yes, acrylic is used for premium kitchens. Typical construction: MDF core,
acrylic sheet bonded on top, polished edges. Creates high-gloss doors,
modern handleless kitchens.

**Acrylic for Staircases**

Acrylic can be used for balustrade panels (clear, frosted, coloured) and
decorative stair inserts (lighting effects, feature panels, modern designs).

Not suitable for: ❌ Stair treads · ❌ Structural strings · ❌ Load-bearing parts

(HPL = High Pressure Laminate)

Examples: Formica type products, compact laminate. Used over MDF, plywood,

## MDF vs Other Sheet Materials

**Will MDF paint the same as solid wood?**

MDF usually produces a very smooth painted finish because it has no visible
grain. Solid timber has a natural grain pattern that can sometimes show
through paint, depending on the species and finish. When both are
professionally prepared and painted in the same colour, many people cannot
tell the difference from a normal viewing distance. Up close, solid timber
may still show subtle grain texture, while MDF generally appears smoother.

**Why do many painted staircases use MDF?**

Many painted staircases use MDF for selected painted components because it
offers a very smooth painted finish, consistent surface, no knots, no grain
raising, stable flat panels, and excellent machining quality. It is common
to find MDF used for painted risers, painted strings, painted fascia panels,
decorative mouldings and wall panelling. This is an established practice
within the staircase industry.

**Is MDF suitable for a staircase?**

Yes, when used appropriately. MDF has been used successfully in staircases
for many years, particularly for painted components. The suitability depends
on which component it is used for, the grade of MDF, the design, the
environment, and the manufacturer's construction methods. Many high-quality
painted staircases include MDF alongside solid timber.

**Should treads be MDF?**

Solid timber or engineered timber treads are generally preferred because
they receive the greatest wear from foot traffic. While MDF may be suitable
for some decorative parts of a staircase, walking surfaces are commonly
manufactured from stronger timber-based materials designed for repeated use.

**Are MDF risers acceptable?**

Yes. Painted MDF risers are very common. Because risers are not normally
walked on, they experience much less wear than treads, making MDF a
practical choice in many staircase designs.

**Can staircase strings be made from MDF?**

Yes. Many painted closed-string staircases use MDF strings successfully.
The choice depends on staircase design, span, loading, construction method,
and manufacturer's engineering. Where structural performance is critical,
manufacturers may use engineered timber, plywood or solid timber instead.

**Will an MDF bullnose step wear out?**

The answer depends on how it is constructed. If the bullnose is manufactured
correctly using suitable materials and finishes, it can provide many years
of service. However, the front edge of any staircase receives frequent
contact from shoes, vacuum cleaners and everyday use. Over time, all
materials — including timber — may show signs of wear. If you are
considering an MDF bullnose, ask the manufacturer how it is constructed
and finished.

**Can MDF chip?**

Like many manufactured boards, MDF edges can be damaged by heavy impacts.
Good painting systems and careful everyday use help protect the surface,
but no painted material is completely immune to knocks.

**How are MDF edges sealed?**

Professional manufacturers normally prepare MDF edges carefully before
painting. Typical preparation may include sanding, edge sealing, priming,
filling where required, additional primer coats, and a final paint finish.
The exact process varies between manufacturers, but proper edge preparation
is important for achieving a durable painted finish.

**Does MDF absorb moisture?**

Standard MDF is generally more sensitive to prolonged moisture exposure than
many hardwoods. However, interior staircases are normally installed in dry
indoor environments. Moisture-resistant grades of MDF are available for
appropriate applications. Good sealing and painting help protect MDF
surfaces. If your staircase is for an unusually humid environment, discuss
suitable materials with the manufacturer.

**Will MDF swell if it gets wet?**

Excessive water exposure can damage many building materials, including MDF.
A professionally painted interior staircase should not normally be exposed
to prolonged moisture. If flooding or significant water damage occurs,
affected components should be inspected.

**Is solid oak always better than MDF?**

Not necessarily. Each material has strengths. Solid timber offers natural
grain, traditional appearance, refinishing potential and high durability.
MDF offers a smooth painted finish, stable flat panels, no knots, and a
consistent appearance. The best choice depends on the component, desired
finish and design.

**Can you tell if a staircase is MDF?**

Not always. A professionally manufactured and painted MDF component can be
very difficult to distinguish from painted timber once installed.

**Will my staircase company mix materials?**

Yes. Many bespoke staircases combine different materials to achieve the best
balance of appearance, strength and cost. For example: oak treads, oak
handrails, MDF painted strings, MDF painted risers, hardwood newel posts,
hardwood balusters. Using different materials for different components is
common practice.

**Should I insist on solid timber everywhere?**

Not necessarily. Rather than focusing on one material throughout, it is
often more useful to ask why a particular material has been chosen for
each component. A reputable manufacturer should be able to explain the
reasoning behind their specification.

**What if I'm unsure about the material specification?**

Nex will explain the general advantages and limitations of common staircase
materials, but it won't guess whether one manufacturer's specification is
better than another without evidence. If you would like independent
clarification, Nex can, with your permission, help prepare your questions
and connect you with an experienced staircase manufacturer to explain why
a particular material has been recommended for your project.

**Can you buy MDF steps and risers from building suppliers?**

Usually MDF risers YES — most building suppliers sell MDF sheets that can
be cut into stair risers (9mm, 12mm, 15mm, 18mm thickness, 2440×1220mm
sheets). MDF stair treads usually NO — a normal MDF sheet is not sold as
a finished stair tread because it is not ideal as a walking surface. A
tread receives foot impact, point loading from heels, dirt and moisture,
edge wear. A better tread material is oak, beech, ash, engineered timber,
or laminated hardwood.

**Will glue stick to MDF?**

Yes. MDF actually has very good glue adhesion. Common adhesives: PVA wood
glue (D3, D4 waterproof), PU glue (very strong, moisture resistant),
construction adhesive (for fixing risers, decorative panels, stair parts).
However, preparation matters: clean surface, remove dust, apply correct
adhesive, clamp or hold firmly, allow full curing time. The face of MDF
bonds extremely well. The edges are more absorbent, so they may need
sealing.

**Why do some people say MDF is cheap?**

Several reasons. First, MDF has no natural wood value — solid oak has
grain, character, natural variation, premium appearance. MDF is made from
wood fibres, so people associate it with flat-pack furniture, budget
cabinets, low-cost products. Second, cheap MDF products give it a bad
reputation — low-quality MDF furniture often fails because thin MDF is
used, poor edge sealing, cheap screws, water damage, poor assembly.
People remember failures. Third, traditional joiners prefer timber
because it feels natural, can be repaired, has visible craftsmanship.
MDF feels more industrial.

**Why do staircase makers use MDF?**

Because for some parts it is actually the better material. It gives a
perfect painted finish (no knots, no grain showing through, no cracks
from timber movement). It is dimensionally stable — wood moves; MDF does
not expand along the grain, holds its shape, keeps paint smoother. It is
easy to manufacture — a staircase factory can cut hundreds of identical
risers, CNC machine profiles, paint consistently. And it enables cost
control — a staircase with oak treads and MDF painted risers can look
premium without using expensive timber everywhere.

**Can MDF be used as a landing board?**

Generally no, not as the structural landing board. A landing board carries
people standing on it, turning loads, furniture movement, long-term weight.
A landing needs structural strength. Better materials: solid timber,
glulam/engineered timber, plywood (structural grade), timber floor boards,
structural panels designed for flooring. MDF can be used as a decorative
finished surface OVER a structural landing, but not as the load-carrying
board itself.

**Can MDF be used to cover the back of stairs?**

Yes, MDF is commonly used for covering the underside of stair flights,
creating a smooth painted ceiling underneath stairs, closing the rear of
open risers. Typical thickness: 9mm, 12mm, or 18mm. Benefits: smooth
painted finish, easy to cut, easy to decorate, clean modern look.
Important: the MDF is only a covering panel — it is not carrying the
stairs. The structure should still be timber strings, steel frame or
concrete.

**Can MDF be used for a staircase well trimmer?**

No. A staircase well trimmer is a structural component supporting the floor
opening, joists, landing structure and stair loads. MDF is not suitable
because it has lower structural strength, does not hold heavy fixings as
well as timber, moisture can weaken it, and it is not designed for
structural framing. Use C24 structural timber, laminated timber, glulam
or steel sections.

**How does MDF compare to plywood in cost?**

MDF is one of the lower-cost engineered boards. Approximate 18mm sheet
(2440×1220mm) comparisons: standard MDF 100% (base price),
moisture-resistant MDF 120–160%, plywood 180–300%, birch plywood
250–400%, OSB 120–180%, chipboard 70–100%, solid timber board 250–600%+,
laminated timber panel 200–400%. These are typical industry ranges, not
fixed prices.

**What is the professional industry rule for stair materials?**

Use MDF where you want beauty and a painted finish. Use plywood, timber
or steel where you need strength. That is why many expensive staircases
still contain MDF — it is not because they are cheap; it is because MDF
performs extremely well in the correct location.
